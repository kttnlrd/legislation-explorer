# 1-295 - Personal services business

Worked example: Personal services business Issue Bernard Horsfall is a lawyer and the sole director and shareholder of Horsfall Legal Pty Ltd, the corporate trustee for the Horsfall Family Trust (HFT). Bernard provides his legal services, which are personal services as they involve the exercise of his skill and judgment, through HFT under a contract for service.

The trustee for HFT enters into a number of contracts with unrelated clients to provide Bernard's legal services, with total fees from the contracts estimated to be $500,000. Bernard is named in the contracts and performs all the work. HFT does not employ or engage anybody other than Bernard to provide the services and does not have any substantial income-producing assets.

For the year ended 30 June 2025, Bernard receives a trust distribution of $50,000 as remuneration for the services he provided under the contracts entered into by HFT. HFT resolves to distribute the remainder of the net income ($450,000) to the other beneficiaries of the trust who are the immediate family members of Bernard and in a lower tax bracket. Each beneficiary pays tax on the trust distribution at their marginal rate.

The beneficiaries do not perform any services for the trust.

Is this arrangement likely to be effective for tax purposes? If not, how might the ATO attack it?

Solution An individual or personal services entity (PSE) will be excluded from the personal services income (PSI) rules where they are conducting a personal services business (PSB). Where there is no PSB, the PSI rules (in Div 86 ITAA97) will operate to attribute income to an individual and deny deductions for certain expenditure.

A PSB will exist where the Commissioner has provided a determination to that effect, or the relevant individual or PSE satisfies at least one of 4 tests, being:

- the results test

- the unrelated clients test

- the employment test

- the business premises test.

HFT is a PSE because its income includes the PSI of Bernard, the individual who performs the principal work. HFT is a PSB because it meets one of the PSB tests (the unrelated clients test). PSI rules do not apply to Bernard's PSI.

However, s 86-10 of the ITAA97 provides that Pt IVA of the ITAA36 (the general anti-avoidance provisions) may still apply to cases of alienation of PSI that fall outside Div 86 (eg due to the operation of the PSB rules).

Guidance on the question of how the ATO is likely to treat an arrangement such as this can be found in draft Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2024/D2, which sets out the Commissioner's compliance approach on the potential application of Pt IVA where an individual derives PSI through a PSE conducting a PSB. The ATO previously set out its guidance on this area in Taxation Ruling TR 2022/3.

This draft PCG sets out the way that the ATO will apply those rules to certain arrangements where the dominant purpose is to obtain a tax benefit by diverting, alienating or splitting an individual's PSI and/or retaining income in the PSE so it is taxed at an overall lower tax rate.

Draft PCG 2024/D2 provides a risk assessment framework which identifies the types of alienation arrangements the ATO considers to be either a "low" or "higher" risk of triggering Pt IVA.

The draft PCG does not establish an acceptable level of income splitting; however, the degree to which PSI has been diverted away from the individual is a relevant factor in considering the application of Pt IVA.

Low risk arrangements An arrangement is considered low risk where net PSI received through the PSE is assessed to the individual whose personal efforts or skills generated that income and tax is not deferred. These arrangements are considered to be low risk because no tax benefit is obtained.

Higher risk arrangements A higher risk arrangement will include either, or both, an income splitting or retention of profit arrangement.

As a result, the PSI is diverted away from the individual and facilitates the deferral of tax.

The arrangements in question involve the trust diverting approximately $450,000 of the $500,000 profits to Bernard's associates, with only $50,000 (which does not represent a commercial rate of return) going to Bernard himself. The arrangement falls squarely within the "higher risk" rating in the table above as the net PSI is split with an associate of the individual, thereby reducing the overall income tax liability.

The draft PCG states that just because an arrangement has features which could indicate a tax scheme, this does not mean that they will choose to apply Pt IVA. However, such features do indicate an increased likelihood of ATO review, which would include a deeper consideration of whether Pt IVA should apply.

The total amount of tax paid by Bernard and his associates is less than it would have been if Bernard had returned the entire net PSI from his personal services in his individual tax return. The splitting of Bernard's PSI with his associates, including immediate family, results, overall, in less tax being paid, which is a tax benefit.

The ATO is therefore more likely to apply compliance resources to reviewing the arrangement, including consideration of whether Pt IVA should apply.
