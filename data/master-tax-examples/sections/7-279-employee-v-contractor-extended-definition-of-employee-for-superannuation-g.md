# 7-279 - Employee v contractor Extended definition of employee for superannuation guarantee purposes

purposes Worked example: Employee v contractor: Extended definition of employee for superannuation guarantee purposes Issue Jules Wilson was engaged as a cleaner by Big Dog Pty Ltd. She was hired to clean Big Dog's office premises 5 days a week, starting at 7 pm Monday to Friday, for 4 hours a day. The engagement was on the basis that she was a contractor and no provision was made for superannuation guarantee payments.

There was no formal written contract, with the terms set out in various emails between the parties, and by their conduct once the engagement had begun.

Both Jules and Big Dog initially thought that she was an independent contractor, and not an employee.

However, Jules has since received advice that Big Dog should have paid superannuation on her behalf.

Jules was given a generic list of cleaning tasks and some guidance as to what daily and weekly tasks should be undertaken. She was paid an hourly rate and was required to invoice Big Dog using her ABN.

She never delegated her work. Although she was theoretically able to engage someone else, she never actually did so because there were issues with obtaining police clearance for anyone but Jules. Therefore, during periods where she was absent, Big Dog engaged a replacement contractor or one of its other staff to do the cleaning work.

She had some flexibility to extend her shift if she was running late, or if she was sick, she could make up the hours at another time. In addition, she was not required to attend any meetings, including to check the progress of her work.

Although Big Dog referred in several emails to the need for Jules to arrange public liability insurance, she never actually did so.

Big Dog provided all cleaning equipment and materials, and if Jules ever purchased any such items, Big Dog reimbursed her. Big Dog did not perform any checks on her work, other than occasional queries about the number of hours she worked.

Is Big Dog required to pay superannuation guarantee contributions for Jules? If so, what are the potential penalties for Big Dog of not paying superannuation on Jules's behalf?

Solution Section 12(1) of the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992 provides that the terms "employee" and "employer" have their ordinary meanings but that those terms are subject to the expanded meaning set out in s 12(3), which provides that, if a person works under a contract that is wholly or principally for the labour of the person, the person is an employee of the other party to the contract. Therefore, if s 12(3) applies, a liability for superannuation guarantee contributions arises in respect of that person.

So, under s 12(3), a person is an employee if these elements are present:

- there should be a contract

- that is wholly or principally for the labour of a person, and

- the person must work under that contract.

This 3-step approach to applying s 12(3) was laid down by the Full Federal Court in Dental Corporation Pty Ltd v Moffet 20 ESL 07; [2020] FCAFC 118 and was subsequently adopted by the Full Federal Court in Jamsek & Ors v ZG Operations Australia Pty Ltd & Ors (No 3) 2023 ATC ¶20-858; [2023] FCAFC 48 and purposes JMC Pty Ltd v FC of T 2023 ATC ¶20-861; [2023] FCAFC 76, and has now been incorporated into ATO guidance in Taxation Ruling TR 2023/4.

In JMC, the Full Court held that Mr H, a lecturer, was not an employee under the extended definition in s 12(3). The right to subcontract provided by the contract meant that it was not "wholly or principally for the labour of the person". While it was true that Mr H could perform the contract personally, it was equally true that he could have subcontracted or assigned the contract with the consent of JMC. The court found that it is necessary to look at the rights under the contract and not to the actual performance of the contract. The contract was one for the provision of teaching services and not principally for the labour of Mr H. The right to subcontract indicated that the contract was not "wholly or principally for the labour of the person".

By contrast, and based on facts similar to this question, in S&H Investments Pty Ltd v FC of T 2024 ATC ¶10-720; [2024] AATA 893, the Tribunal affirmed the Commissioner's decision that a worker who had been contracted to undertake the same cleaning duties she undertook previously as an employee was still an employee under the extended definition in s 12(3), noting that it was irrelevant what status the parties intended their relationship to have. Once again, the Tribunal looked to the contractual relationship between the parties and considered the respective rights and obligations of the parties under the contract, but this time found that the contract was wholly or principally for the worker's labour because:

- the worker was remunerated for her personal labour and skills in cleaning the taxpayer's office

- the conduct of the parties suggested that there was no right to delegate. The worker never actually

  delegated her work. She had no employees, and there were practical difficulties with police clearance

  and office security when she suggested a friend could cover for her on one occasion

- when she was absent, it was the taxpayer who arranged for a contractor or staff member to cover for

  her. Therefore, it was reasonable to conclude that the performance of the work was personal to the

  worker.

Applying the outcome of the above case to the present very similar circumstances, it can be seen that Jules is an employee under the extended definition in s 12(3). Apart from the right to delegate — which is practically impossible for Jules to do because of the issue with police clearances — she is not required to achieve a result and the work she performed was not connected to any quantifiable result. She did not need to attend meetings to discuss her progress. She was remunerated by the hour, which also suggested that she was not engaged to achieve a particular result. Consequently, the 3 elements of the extended definition of an "employee" in s 12(3) are met, ie there was a contract between the parties, it was wholly or principally for her labour, and she was required to perform the work under the contract.

So, if Jules is an employee under the extended definition, Big Dog will have a liability to the SG charge, imposed by s 5 of the Superannuation Guarantee Charge Act 1992, in relation to Jules, if Big Dog does not pay the minimum required contributions, ie there is an SG shortfall. The SG charge payable on an SG shortfall for a quarter is equal to the amount of the shortfall. An employer's SG shortfall for a particular quarter is the sum of the following components for that quarter:

- individual SG shortfall

- choice liability — the SG shortfall is increased by 25% if the employer does not comply with the

  "choice of fund" requirements

- nominal interest — at 10% per annum

- administrative component — $20 per employee (in respect of whom the employer has an SG shortfall)

  per quarter.

The director penalty regime applies to unpaid SG charge liabilities and estimates, and therefore there is a potential personal liability on the directors in this case.
