# 4-225 - Home office expenses occupancy costs and on call employees

Worked example: Home office expenses: occupancy costs and on call employees Issue Mary Brown is employed by Commonwealth Bank as an IT lead, maintaining the bank's computer systems.

Because of the demands of the job, there is a roster system which requires that for one week in every 4 weeks she is on call 24 hours a day to deal with any problems.

During these one-week periods, Mary works from home because it is not feasible for her to go into the office after hours, as there are security issues. Air conditioning would need to be switched on, which is not reasonably practicable when only one person is present in the office.

Mary is provided with all necessary computer and other equipment by the bank for home use.

Mary's home has 4 levels:

- a garage on the ground floor

- on the next level, a study used by Mary as a base of work when she is working from home, which

  doubles as an entrance to the rest of the house for people arriving through the garage

- the kitchen, dining room and living room on the next level, and

- the bedrooms and bathrooms on the top floor.

Advise Mary if she is entitled to claim a tax deduction for mortgage interest and rates (so called "occupancy expenses") and, if so, what proportion of these costs is she entitled to? Her mortgage interest during the year was $20,000 and her rates were $8,000.

Solution Generally, employees who are provided with a place of work at their employer's business premises cannot claim home occupancy expenses (ie expenses relating to ownership or use of a home which is not affected by the taxpayer's income-earning activities, including rent, mortgage interest, municipal and water rates, land taxes and house insurance premiums) since their home is not their "sole base of operations". This is particularly noteworthy in the context of the post-COVID-19 environment, where many employers have implemented "hot-desking" arrangements or voluntary working-from-home codes due to which employees are not guaranteed to have a desk at the place of work on certain days of the week or, indeed, at all.

The employees are not able to claim occupancy expenses because their home is not their sole base of operations (even though they are often prevented from using their normal place of work by the employer's policies).

However, the deductibility of occupancy costs where an employer requires an employee who is on call to work from home has been clarified in McAteer v FC of T 2020 ATC ¶10-536. In that case, the taxpayer sought a deduction for occupancy expenses where the bank for which he worked required him to perform duties from home relating to his role maintaining the bank's IT systems during periods when he was on call.

In the McAteer case, the AAT found the taxpayer's use of his home study was not a mere convenience when he was on call. Instead, it was an implicit requirement of his role that he use his home during the periods he was rostered to work 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Indeed, his employment would have been terminated if he had refused to use his home for that purpose.

The AAT found that "the occasion of the outgoings referable to the study are a result of the taxpayer holding his particular job" and allowed a deduction for occupancy costs.

The study area was set aside exclusively for work-related purposes because it was used to house computer equipment and did not look conducive to other uses. The fact that it was used incidentally as an entrance to the taxpayer's home did not mean it lost its work-related identity.

So, where an employee is working from home when rostered on call after hours and it is a requirement rather than a mere convenience that the on-call work be performed at home, a deduction can be claimed for the work-related proportion of expenses such as mortgage interest and rates.

The amount of the deduction will take into account the floor area of the taxpayer's home office as a proportion of the total floor area of the house and the time spent during the year working from home as a requirement (excluding periods when the taxpayer chooses to work from home as a convenience).

In each week that Mary uses her office to work from home as a requirement of her job, it is reasonable to assume that she has to work between the hours of 7 pm and 7 am on weekdays (making allowance for weekday work hours and travel time) and 24 hours on weekends. This suggests that, each 4 weeks, she is on call to work from home for 108 hours (ie 5 days × 12 hours per day and 2 days × 24 hours per day) out of 672 hours in one month (ie 7 days × 24 hours × 4 weeks). The total floor area occupied by the home office is 25% (one whole floor of a 4-storey property).

Accordingly, the deductible amount of the mortgage interest will be 108 / 672 × 25% × $20,000 = $803 and of her council rates will be 108 / 672 × 25% × $8,000 = $321.
