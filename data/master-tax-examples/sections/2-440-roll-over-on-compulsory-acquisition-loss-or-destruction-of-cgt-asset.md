# 2-440 - Roll-over on compulsory acquisition loss or destruction of CGT asset

Worked example: Roll-over on compulsory acquisition, loss or destruction of CGT asset Issue Herbert Ross owns a residential investment property in Port Lincoln, which he bought in 1999 for $100,000.

In January 2025, fire rips through his property and it is destroyed. He receives insurance proceeds totalling $400,000 on 1 May 2025.

Herbert spends $240,000 rebuilding the property.

Outline the CGT implications of the above transactions for Herbert.

Solution Roll-over relief may be available for a CGT asset under ITAA97 Subdiv 124-B (ITAA97 ss 124-70 to 124-95) where one of the following events happens to the asset:

- it is compulsorily acquired (¶2-078)

- it is disposed of to an Australian government agency in response to an acquisition notice issued by

  the agency (¶2-078)

- it is wholly or partly lost or destroyed, or

- in the case of a statutory lease — the lease expires and is not renewed.

The roll-over only applies if the taxpayer has made a capital gain on the loss, destruction or compulsory acquisition of an asset acquired on or after 20 September 1985 (ie, a post-CGT asset). The taxpayer receives a CGT exemption for any replacement asset if the original asset was acquired before 20 September 1985.

ITAA97 s 124-70(2)(a) states that the entity must receive money or another CGT asset or both as compensation for the CGT event happening.

If a taxpayer receives money as compensation or as an insurance payment, it is necessary for the taxpayer to incur expenditure in acquiring another CGT asset or where the asset is not wholly lost or destroyed, incur expenditure of a capital nature in repairing or restoring the asset.

Generally, at least some of the expenditure must be incurred no earlier than a year before the relevant event and no later than one year after the end of the income year in which the event happens.

If, just before the event happened, the original asset was being used as a business asset or was installed ready for such use, the replacement asset must be used in a similar manner for a reasonable time after it is acquired. A replacement asset cannot become an item of trading stock just after it is acquired and it cannot be a depreciating asset, whether under the general capital allowance rules in ITAA97 Div 40 or the simplified rules for small businesses in ITAA97 Div 328.

The way the roll-over applies depends on whether the money received exceeds the cost of repairing or replacing the asset. In relation to a post-CGT asset, the following rules apply: 1. if the money received exceeds the expenditure incurred on the replacement asset or in repairing or restoring the original asset and the capital gain is greater than the excess, the gain is reduced to the amount of the excess and the expenditure is reduced by the amount by which the gain was originally greater than the excess 2. if the money received exceeds the expenditure and the gain is less than or equal to the excess, the gain is not reduced, and 3. if the money received does not exceed the expenditure, the gain is disregarded, and the expenditure is reduced by the amount of the gain.

If the compulsorily acquired asset was a pre-CGT asset (ie, acquired before 20 September 1985), the replacement asset will also be deemed to be a pre-CGT asset, provided:

- the taxpayer does not spend more than 120% of the market value of the original asset (immediately

  before its disposal) in acquiring a replacement asset, or

- if the asset was destroyed by natural disaster, it is reasonable to treat the replacement asset as

  "substantially the same" as the original asset.

In this case, Herbert replaces a post-CGT asset. He is, therefore, entitled to roll-over relief in respect of the gain.

There will be a notional capital gain of $300,000 which will arise on the original asset ($400,000 compensation less $100,000 base cost). This notional capital gain of $300,000 is greater than the "excess" of insurance proceeds over actual rebuilding costs (ie, $400,000 insurance proceeds less $240,000 rebuilding costs = $160,000). Therefore, Herbert will realise a capital gain of $160,000, which is the amount of the "excess".

To calculate any future capital gain or loss on the replacement asset, the cost base of the replacement asset ($240,000) is reduced by the amount by which the notional gain ($300,000) is more than the "excess" ($160,000), so $240,000 is reduced by $140,000 ($300,000 less $160,000), leaving a cost base for the replacement asset of $100,000.

Note, if the capital gain that would have arisen from the CGT event is less than or equal to the "excess" of the compensation received over expenditure on a replacement asset, the capital gain is not reduced, as follows: Say Herbert receives $400,000 insurance proceeds for the destruction of his house and the original cost base was $160,000. Herbert spends just $100,000 repairing the house. There will be a notional capital gain of $240,000 ($400,000 less $160,000) and the "excess" will be $300,000 ($400,000 less $100,000). As the notional capital gain of $240,000 is less than the "excess" ($300,000), the notional capital gain is not reduced and is taxed as the actual capital gain. Therefore, Herbert would have an actual capital gain of $240,000.

If the compensation received does not exceed the expenditure incurred on the replacement asset, no capital gain arises — but the cost base expenditure incurred on the replacement asset or repair is reduced by the amount of the capital gain that would otherwise have arisen but for the roll-over, as follows: Say Herbert receives $246,000 in insurance proceeds, spends $257,000 on repairs, and the cost base for the building was $244,000. The resulting capital gain is $2,000 ($246,000 - $244,000) and the shortfall on the replacement expenditure is $11,000 ($257,000 spent on repairs less $246,000 insurance proceeds). As the compensation money does not exceed the replacement expenditure, Herbert disregards the capital gain.

However, the amount of expenditure that Herbert could include in the cost base of the replacement building is reduced by the amount of the capital gain ($2,000) to $255,000.

If the taxpayer receives a replacement asset instead of money, any capital gain made on the original asset is disregarded. If the original asset was a post-CGT asset, the taxpayer is taken to have acquired the replacement asset for an amount equal to the cost base of the original asset. If the original asset was a pre- CGT asset, the replacement asset is also taken to be a pre-CGT asset.

If both money and a replacement asset are received, each part of the compensation is treated separately in accordance with the rules for the receipt of money or the receipt of an asset. The market value of the replacement asset must be more than that part of the cost base of the original asset that is attributable to the new asset. Where the original asset is a post-CGT asset, the first element of the replacement asset's cost base is that part of the original asset's cost base at the time of the event that is attributable to the new asset.

Where the original asset is a pre-CGT asset, the replacement asset has the same status.

If the compulsorily acquired asset was an "active asset" (ie used in a business), the CGT small business concessions would potentially apply and the taxpayer could choose to apply them instead. This would include the CGT small business roll-over. Note that the advantage in applying the small business concessions is that the replacement asset does not have to be acquired until 2 years after the relevant income year and it can be a depreciating asset.
