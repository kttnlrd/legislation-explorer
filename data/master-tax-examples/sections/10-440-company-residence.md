# 10-440 - Company residence

Worked example: Company residence Issue Red Star Ltd is an investment company incorporated in Jersey, Channel Islands. The company is 100% owned by the Steward Family Trust, a Jersey resident trust, with a corporate director (Swindle Trustees Ltd) based in St Helier. The sole beneficiary of that trust is Kate Steward, who lives in and is a resident of Australia. Red Star Ltd has 2 Jersey resident directors.

Red Star conducts a real estate investment business in the UK, holding commercial property for the purpose of deriving rent in various major British cities. The memorandum and articles of association of Red Star provide that the decisions of the directors are only effective if Kate agrees with them.

The directors meet in Jersey and make tentative decisions about the company's business, including finance, the acquisition and disposal of investment properties, and leasing policies. They also undertake the company's day-to-day operational matters such as collecting rent, paying commission, finding tenants and entering leases, and subcontracting out the maintenance of the buildings it leases.

Kate exercises the power given to her under the constitution and has the final say on all the tentative decisions made by the directors. This includes all Red Star's high-level decisions, covering leasing policies, funding and general corporate strategies, and Kate does so solely from Australia. Kate does not always accept the views of the directors and occasionally makes decisions different to the tentative decisions proposed by Red Star's board.

Is Red Star a tax resident of Australia, and therefore subject to Australian tax, despite not being incorporated here or carrying on business here?

Solution Under s 6(1) ITAA36, a company is resident in Australia if:

- it is incorporated in Australia, or

- although not incorporated in Australia it carries on business in Australia and has either:

  – its central management and control in Australia, or

– its voting power controlled by shareholders who are residents of Australia.

Given that Red Star is not incorporated in Australia, it is necessary to consider the second test to determine whether it is resident.

Taxation Ruling TR 2018/5 sets out the guidelines for determining whether a company, which is not incorporated in Australia, is a resident of Australia. The Ruling states that 4 matters are relevant in determining whether a company:

- carries on business in Australia, and

- has its central management and control in Australia.

  Does a company carry on business in Australia?

To be resident under the central management and control test of residency, a company must carry on business in Australia. It is not necessary for the company to undertake any part of its actual trading or investment operations in Australia. This is because the central management and control of a business is factually part of carrying on that business. Therefore, if the central management and control of the business is in Australia, it is automatically carrying on business here (which reverses the ATO's previous guidance in the withdrawn Taxation Ruling TR 2004/15, which stated that the requirement to carry on business in Australia was separate from, and in addition to, the requirement to have the company's central management and control in Australia).

What does central management and control mean?

Central management and control does not refer to command of a company's day-to-day conduct and operations. Rather, as stated in TR 2018/5, it refers to "the making of high-level decisions that set the company's general policies, and determine the direction of its operations and the type of transactions it will enter", including:

- financial matters such as deciding how profits are used

- declaration of dividends

- decisions relating to acquiring and selling significant company assets.

  Who exercises central management and control?

This is a question of fact. As was evident in the case of Bywater Investments Ltd & Ors v FC of T; Hua Wang Bank Berhad v FC of T 2016 ATC ¶20-589; [2016] HCA 45, although a company's directors will generally be those who exercise central management and control, this is not always the case. TR 2018/5 explains that there is a difference between an outsider that is only influential on a board of directors, and one that actually makes the decisions for directors. In Bywater Investments, each company was in fact controlled by Mr G, an accountant based in Sydney. The board of directors for each company rubber-stamped G's directions.

They did not actually consider whether the instructions provided to them by G were in the best interests of the company.

Where is central management and control exercised?

Deciding the location of a company's central management and control is a matter of where high-level decisions are made. As TR 2018/5 explains, what matters is where those who control the company "actually perform the activities to control and direct the company". For example, in Bywater Investments, G was providing instructions to the boards of directors from Australia. This meant that the effective place of management for each company was exercised in Australia.

The Ruling should be read in conjunction with Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2018/9, which provides several examples. Like other PCGs, this contains a risk assessment framework outlining the circumstances and features of the 3 risk zones foreign-incorporated companies sit in (ie "low", "medium" or "high" risk). To fall within the low-risk zone, companies must not have any moderate or high-risk circumstances or features.

Some examples of moderate and high-risk features that could be relevant include:

- The majority of directors spending most of their time in Australia but being stated to make all high-

  level decisions in a foreign jurisdiction (moderate risk).

- A company not being a resident of any foreign jurisdiction, or facts suggesting that central

  management and control is not exercised in any foreign jurisdiction (high risk).

- Artificial or contrived arrangements affecting the location of central management and control, for

  example flying Australian directors overseas to attend board meetings in a country where a company

  does not have a substantive commercial presence (high risk).

Red Star does not carry out any of its day-to-day business in Australia. Nevertheless, this does not matter for the purposes of determining tax residency provided it can be shown that the central management and control of the company is in Australia.

Similar to the Bywater Investments case, as Kate makes high-level decisions (or can veto any of the tentative decisions taken by the directors), and as that authority is exercised in Australia, we can say that Kate exercises the central management and control of Red Star. Therefore, Red Star is a resident of Australia under the central management and control test of company residency.
