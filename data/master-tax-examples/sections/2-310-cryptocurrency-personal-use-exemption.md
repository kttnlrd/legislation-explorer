# 2-310 - Cryptocurrency personal use exemption

Worked example: Cryptocurrency: personal use exemption Issue Donald Drake has been a long-term investor in shares and has a range of holdings in various public companies in a balanced portfolio of high- and low-risk investments. Some of his holdings are income producing and some not, and he adjusts his portfolio frequently on the advice of his adviser.

Recently, Donald's adviser told him that he should invest in cryptocurrency. On 16 December 2024, he bought one unit of the cryptocurrency for $4,435. By 21 June 2025, the value of his unit had increased to $14,100 and, fearing the price rise was unsustainable, he decided to sell, making a profit of $9,665 (excluding fees).

Before purchasing his unit, Donald was told by a friend that profits on cryptocurrencies are tax free provided the cost of the investment is $10,000 or less.

What are the tax implications of Donald's cryptocurrency transactions? Is it correct that the profit could be tax free?

Solution Cryptocurrency is a form of currency that is created and held electronically. It is produced by people and businesses running computers all around the world using software that solves mathematical problems. It is not printed like dollars or euros and nobody controls it — there is no support from any central bank. The best-known cryptocurrency is Bitcoin but there are numerous others (eg Ethereum and Tether).

The disposal of cryptocurrency will give rise to a capital gains tax liability. Disposal occurs when:

- selling cryptocurrency for Australian dollars

- exchanging one cryptocurrency for another

- gifting cryptocurrency

- trading cryptocurrency, or

- using cryptocurrency to pay for goods or services.

In some cases, such as when it is gifted, market value is substituted for proceeds.

If the disposal is part of a business, the profits on disposal will be assessable as ordinary income and not as a capital gain.

According to TD 2014/26 (CGT: Is bitcoin a CGT asset?), the disposal of cryptocurrency acquired for the purposes of investment to a third party gives rise to CGT event A1 under s 104-10(1). A taxpayer will make a capital gain from CGT event A1 if the capital proceeds from the disposal of the cryptocurrency are more than the cryptocurrency's cost base. The capital proceeds from the disposal are, in accordance with s 116-20(1), the money or the market value of any other property received (or entitled to be received) by the taxpayer in respect of the disposal. The money paid or the market value of any other property the taxpayer gave in respect of acquiring the cryptocurrency will be included in the cost base of the cryptocurrency in accordance with s 110-25(2).

A capital gain will arise if the capital proceeds from the disposal of the cryptocurrency are more than its cost base. If the capital proceeds from the disposal of the cryptocurrency are less than its cost base, a capital loss arises.

If the cryptocurrency is held for 12 months or more, the 50% capital gain discount may be available.

While a digital wallet can contain different types of cryptocurrencies, each cryptocurrency is a separate CGT asset.

Under s 118-10(3), a capital gain made from a personal use asset is disregarded if the first element of the cost base (broadly speaking, the amount paid to acquire the cryptocurrency in the context of this question) is $10,000 or less. In addition, any capital loss made from a personal use asset is disregarded under s 108-20(1).

Cryptocurrency may be a personal use asset if it is acquired and kept or used mainly to purchase items for personal use or consumption (s 108-20).

Cryptocurrency is not a personal use asset if it is acquired, kept or used:

- as an investment

- in a profit-making scheme, or

- in the course of carrying on a business.

An example of where cryptocurrency would be considered to be a personal use asset is, according to TD 2014/26, where an individual purchases the cryptocurrency from an exchange and uses it to make online purchases for their personal needs such as clothing or music. If the cryptocurrency is instead purchased to facilitate the purchase of income-producing investments, it would not be a personal use asset. Another example of where cryptocurrency would not be a personal use asset is where an individual taxpayer mines the cryptocurrency and keeps it for a number of years with the intention of selling at opportune times based on favourable rates of exchange.

In Donald's case, there is no evidence that he acquired the cryptocurrency for personal use. He acquired the cryptocurrency as part of his overall diversified investment strategy and sold it in order to realise the favourable rate of exchange that existed in June 2025. Therefore, even though he spent $10,000 or less acquiring the cryptocurrency, he cannot take advantage of the personal use exemption.

The full profit (less purchase and sale fees) of $9,665 will be subject to CGT. As he retained the cryptocurrency for less than 12 months, he is not entitled to the 50% capital gain discount.

*Refs: s 104-10(1), s 116-20(1), s 110-25(2), s 118-10(3), s
108-20(1), s 108-20)*
