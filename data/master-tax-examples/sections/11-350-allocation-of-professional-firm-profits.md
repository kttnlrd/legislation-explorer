# 11-350 - Allocation of professional firm profits

Worked example: Allocation of professional firm profits Issue Max Wall is an individual professional practitioner in a medical practice partnership. Max is the senior partner in a partnership of 3. Max has disposed of 40% of his interest in the partnership to a discretionary trust for open market value, with a view to protecting his assets in the future. The beneficiary of the discretionary trust is an associated company, Redfern Ltd, of which Max is a director and the main shareholder is his wife, Mary.

The partnership has a service trust entity in its group that provides services to the partnership. Max's 21- year-old son, Julian, is the sole beneficiary of the service trust. The service trust employs 12 full-time and part-time medical practitioners who provide their services to the practice and are responsible for about 75% of the total practice billings.

As well as providing medical practitioner services to the firm, Max spends about 50% of his time actively involved in the management of the practice.

In the year ended 30 June 2025, Max's entitlement to income from the partnership is $1,000,000. There is also an entitlement to $100,000 profit from the service trust. The total profit entitlement is therefore $1,100,000.

Max includes $600,000 of the partnership income in his tax return, Redfern Ltd includes $400,000 in its tax return and the service trust income of $100,000 is distributed to Julian.

Max has no other taxable income in the year ended 30 June 2025.

Advise whether Max should be concerned with the tax compliance risks posed by his arrangements. Using Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2021/4 (Allocation of Professional Firm Profits - ATO Compliance Approach), determine his risk profile and the chances of the ATO applying compliance resources to his arrangement.

Solution The starting point with this question is to work out whether Max's partnership income is personal services income (PSI) or income from a business structure. Whether income is derived from providing personal services is a question of fact and degree unique to each case, however, Taxation Ruling IT 2639 suggests the following factors must be considered in making the decision:

- the nature of the taxpayer's activities

- the extent to which the income depends upon the taxpayer's own skill and judgment

- the extent of the income-producing assets used to derive the income, and

- the number of employees and others engaged.

Finally, for professional practices, the "rule of thumb" according to IT 2639 is that if a practice entity employs at least as many non-principal practitioners as principal practitioners, its income will be treated as generated by the business structure rather than the personal services of its principal workers. Clerical and support staff (eg nurses and secretaries) are not regarded as practitioners "unless they earn material fees in their own right". In this case, 12 medical practitioners are employed within the practice, and they generate about 75% of the practice billings.

Therefore, the income that the partners receive is from a business structure and is not PSI.

PCG 2021/4 applies to risk assess arrangements where a professional diverts profits from a professional services business (eg an accountants' partnership or a medical practice) to an associated entity to reduce income tax where:

- an individual professional practitioner (IPP) provides professional services to clients of the firm, or is

  actively involved in the management of the firm and, in either case, the practitioner and/or associated

  entities have a legal or beneficial interest in the firm

- the firm operates by way of a legally effective structure, eg a partnership, a trust or a company, and

- the income of the firm is not PSI.

Income received by a service entity is taken into account in determining whether the practitioner and related entities meet the guidelines. PCG 2021/4 applies from 1 July 2022.

PCG 2021/4 therefore needs to be considered by Max in determining his risk profile, the chances of the ATO applying compliance resources to his arrangement, and ultimately applying ITAA36 Pt IVA (the general anti- avoidance provisions).

Before the risk assessment model that is the centrepiece of the PCG can be applied, the arrangement in question first needs to pass 2 gateways. These are: 1. There is a sound commercial rationale for entering into and operating the arrangement or structure. 2. The arrangement does not display any of these "high-risk features":

- arrangements where a partner assigns a portion of a partnership interest that are materially different

  in principle from FC of T v Everett 80 ATC 4076; [1980] HCA 6 and FC of T v Galland 86 ATC 4885;

[1986] HCA 83

- financing arrangements relating to non-arm's length transactions

- exploitation of the difference between accounting standards and tax law

- multiple classes of shares and units held by non-equity holders.

In relation to the first gateway, there must be a sound commercial rationale for entering into, and operating, the arrangement or structure. Key elements are:

- The arrangement should reflect the commercial needs of the business, that is, there must be a

  genuine commercial rationale for the arrangement.

- The arrangement must be appropriately documented and actually achieve its commercial purpose, for

  example, the arrangement must actually provide improved asset protection if that is part of its purpose.

- The legal form and documentation must be consistent with the economic substance of how the

  professional firm operates in practice.

Indicators that an arrangement lacks a sound commercial rationale include the following:

- unnecessary complexity

- a step, or a series of steps, that appear to serve no real purpose other than to gain a tax advantage

- the tax result appears at odds with the commercial or economic result

- little or no risk where significant risks would normally be expected

- the parties are operating on non-commercial terms or in a non-arm's length manner

- a gap between the substance of what is being achieved and the legal form.

Although Max's arrangements appear to meet most of the above criteria (and hence the first gateway appears to be passed), it is essential that documentation can be produced to support the desired end of additional asset protection.

Assuming gateway one is passed, it is accepted that the second gateway is passed as no "high risk" features are present in Max's particular arrangements.

With Max's circumstances passing the gateways, he can apply the risk assessment framework to receive a "traffic light" risk assessment rating of green (low risk), amber (moderate risk) or red (high risk). If Max, for some reason, were deemed not to satisfy the gateways, the risk assessment framework is not available to him (in these circumstances the PCG says to email the ATO at ProfessionalPdts@ato.gov.au).

This is in the low-risk zone. The ATO will only apply compliance resources to review the allocation of profit in exceptional circumstances, such as where:

- the ATO is not satisfied that Max's self-assessment is correct, or is adequately supported with

  evidence

- the ATO becomes concerned that higher-risk features are present in the arrangement

- the ATO becomes concerned, from their own data and analysis, that there is a change in Max's

  arrangement causing a shift towards the border of compliance

- the ATO becomes concerned that Max's broader arrangements present a compliance risk

- Max's arrangement relates to a broader set of circumstances being reviewed by the ATO

- changes to Max's arrangement may not have been appropriately treated or disclosed.

If there has been no material change, the ATO will generally only apply compliance resources to the arrangement to:

- confirm Max's calculations were done according to the rules laid out in PCG 2021/4

- confirm the absence of any exclusionary factors (eg the high-risk features under Gateway 2)

- provide binding advice should Max request it, if he wishes to obtain certainty.
