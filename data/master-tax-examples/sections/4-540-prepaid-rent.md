# 4-540 - Prepaid rent

Worked example: Prepaid rent Issue Rose Tyler entered into negotiations to purchase a new high-end clothing store on 20 January 2025 with the management of the Eastfield shopping complex in Melbourne.

As part of these negotiations, Eastfield offered to reduce the annual rent to be paid by Rose by 10% per annum provided she signed up for a 10-year minimum lease term and paid a fixed upfront non-refundable payment of $30,000.

As each year's rent was estimated to be $50,000 (or $500,000 over a 10-year lease term (assuming there was no increase in rent in future years), Rose agreed to the terms on 4 April 2025 and paid the amount of $30,000 on 1 May 2025.

She now seeks to deduct this upfront payment from her assessable profits in the year ended 30 June 2025.

Advise Rose on the tax implications of the $30,000 payment. Is it revenue in nature, or is it capital in nature?

What difference does this make to the tax treatment?

Solution It is quite common for a business to prepay deductible business expenses such as rent, land tax, rates or insurance premiums. Where these amounts are specifically apportioned in the contract, the amount paid would normally be deductible to the purchaser unless the prepayments are capital in nature.

The general rule is that prepayments are only deductible outright if the services are to be performed within the income year in which the payment is incurred. Otherwise, the deduction is spread over the period to which the expenditure relates, or 10 years, whichever is the shorter (ITAA36 ss 82KZMA, 82KZMD).

Small and medium business entities can obtain immediate deductions for expenditure paid up to 12 months in advance (ITAA36 s 82KZM).

Therefore, in this case, the question is whether the payment is revenue in nature (in which case it may be deducted over the full term of the lease, 10 years) or whether the payment is capital in nature, in which case it is included as the cost base of the asset, being the right to the property. When the asset ends, in 10 years, CGT event C2 arises, which will give rise to a capital loss equal to the asset's base cost ($30,000).

The guidelines for distinguishing between capital and revenue outgoings were laid down in the Sun Newspapers Ltd v FC of T (1938) 5 ATD 87; (1938) 61 CLR 337. There, it was pointed out that expenditure in establishing, replacing and enlarging the profit-yielding (ie business) structure itself is capital and is to be contrasted with working or operating expenses. The test laid down in the Sun Newspapers case involved 3 elements, although none is in itself decisive:

- the nature of the advantage sought by making the payment(s)

- the way it is to be used or enjoyed

- the means adopted to get it.

In determining the true nature of a payment, it may be necessary to go behind the description of the payment that is given in the relevant documentation. For example, the fact that payments are called rent and are made periodically would not necessarily prevent them from being, in part, an outgoing of a capital nature.

The question of what the payment is for must be determined by reference to the legal obligations or rights under which it was paid, that is, by reference to the agreement that operated to create the obligation to pay.

In the case of Mussalli v FC of T 2021 ATC ¶20-792; [2021] FCAFC 71, the taxpayer entered into lease and licence agreements to operate several McDonald's fast-food restaurants. A series of fixed, upfront payment were made in respect of each site, with the deductions spread over 10 years for the upfront amounts as "prepaid rent". The taxpayers asserted that a payment made to secure a reduction in a revenue outgoing (in this case, rent) was itself on revenue account, with the taxpayers simply converting a future revenue outgoing into a present revenue outgoing.

At first instance (Mussalli v FC of T 2020 ATC ¶20-743; [2020] FCA 544), the Federal Court held that these upfront payments, labelled as prepayments of rent, that were made by the taxpayer on entering the franchise agreements were capital in nature, as they secured an enduring advantage for the term of the agreements.

The payments secured the acquisition of a better business structure and were not for the carrying on of the business, while the non-refundable nature of the payments further suggested that they were not made for occupying the premises.

The Federal Court decision was upheld by the Full Federal Court on appeal. The Full Court observed that, contrary to the taxpayers' arguments, there was no principle that a payment that substituted for future revenue outgoings or which compensated for them, or which more accurately in this case obviated or removed the need for them, must itself be revenue. Further, it was not necessarily the case that a payment made to secure a reduction in a revenue outgoing was itself on revenue account. There was no principle of general application that a lump sum paid to reduce or eliminate future revenue expenses was always to be characterised in the same way as the expenses for which it was substituted.

Accordingly, applying the principles set out in Mussalli to this case, the prepaid rent will be on capital account, not on revenue account. The payment of $30,000 was made to obtain an enduring advantage in the form of a right to pay the lesser rent for the term of the full lease. This gives a preferable profit-making structure to the business, which means that the expense incurred to derive it is capital in nature and is not a working or operating expense.

Even if the payments had been on revenue account, Rose's preferred treatment of an immediate deduction for the entire $30,000, would not have been allowable since the payment would need to have been spread over the 10-year lease term.
