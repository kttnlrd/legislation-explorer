# 4-620 - Labour costs related to capital assets

Worked example: Labour costs related to capital assets Issue Castrovalva Pty Ltd is a large food processing business. Castrovalva intends to build a new processing plant in Victoria which, when complete, will double its manufacturing capacity and it engages Richard Jones to be the project manager to oversee construction of the facility. His salary will be $150,000 per annum and his contract is expected to last for 2 years, at the end of which the facility is expected to be complete. His duties will be entirely focussed on the construction of the new facility, where he will oversee the organisation of labour and materials, co-ordinating with employees and contractors, and looking after the day-to-day supervision of the build.

Richard Jones will report directly to the Facilities Director, Peter Howell, who has overall charge of the building project. Peter is a long-standing employee of Castrovalva Pty Ltd and he looks after all the facilities owned and operated by the company. He expects to spend approximately one day a week on the construction project, liaising with Richard, checking budgets and reporting directly to the Board of Directors.

Castrovalva intends to claim a deduction for Richard's salary, as it already does for Peter Howell. Advise Castrovalva whether this treatment is correct.

Solution The ATO has issued guidance on this subject, in Taxation Ruling TR 2023/2, which deals with the deductibility of labour costs related to the construction or creation of capital assets, both tangible and intangible. The ruling outlines the Commissioner's view on when these costs are not deductible because they are capital or of a capital nature and provides guidance on how to apportion labour costs that have both capital and revenue aspects on a fair and reasonable basis.

As a general rule, employee costs are deductible under s 8-1 ITAA97, on the basis that they are a regular recurring cost of carrying on a business and are not generally incurred in establishing or enlarging the profit yielding structure of the business (in other words, capital costs). However, in the case of costs of employees that exclusively, predominantly or even partially, work on a capital project within a business, this treatment may not be correct.

The Ruling considers both tangible assets (such as land, plant and property) and intangible assets (such as scientific or technical knowledge, design and implementation of new processes or systems, licences, intellectual property, market knowledge and trademarks, including brand names and publishing titles). It defines capital assets as those assets (tangible and intangible) constructed or created which form part of the profit-yielding structure of a business entity, structure or organisation, whilst capital asset labour costs are defined as:

- salary and wages for employees who perform functions in relation to the construction or creation of

  capital assets, and other costs associated with the employment of that labour, and/or

- other amounts for labour or principally for labour incurred in relation to the construction or creation of

  capital assets.

The Ruling focuses on deductibility under the general deduction provisions in s 8-1 ITAA97, whilst acknowledging that deductions may be specifically provided, or denied, under other provisions of the tax law. The Ruling states that, generally, to the extent capital asset labour costs are incurred specifically for constructing or creating capital assets, they are capital or capital in nature and therefore cannot be claimed under s 8-1.

The Ruling notes that costs incurred for the construction or creation of capital assets are not limited to those involved in the construction work itself, but can also include the costs of labour for those who perform functions in relation to the construction or creation of capital assets.

However, not all capital asset labour costs will be considered capital or capital in nature. For example, where employees are involved in the day-to-day and ongoing operations of a business and they engage in activities that are capital in nature infrequently or those activities are considered minor or incidental in the context of their overall activities, duties and functions, the essential character of the capital asset labour costs of those employees is revenue in nature and apportionment will not be required.

The explanation in the Ruling provides examples of circumstances where employee costs would be regarded as being on revenue account even though an infrequent, minor or incidental amount of the employee's time is devoted to the construction or creation of capital assets:

- a human resource manager responsible for all of the employees or personnel of an established and

  ongoing business, including employees or personnel constructing or creating a capital asset

- a finance manager responsible for all of the ongoing financial aspects of an established and ongoing

  business, including the finance aspects of constructing or creating a capital asset

- a general manager responsible for overseeing the ongoing operations of an established and ongoing

  business, and who spends some time overseeing the construction or creation of a capital asset, and

- a general counsel responsible for all general legal affairs of an established and ongoing business,

  including the legal aspects of constructing or creating a capital asset.

Accordingly, no deduction can be claimed for Richard Jones's salary. His entire job is to manage the construction or creation of a capital asset, specifically the new processing plant. His salary should be included in the capital costs of construction of the building and capital works deductions claimed at the prevailing rate of 2.5%, which are not available until the construction of the capital works has been completed.

However, the salary of the facilities manager of Castrovalva Pty Ltd, and the amounts paid to the board of directors to whom this manager reports, will be immediately deductible under s 8-1 as Peter is not considered to be specifically employed for the construction or creation of a capital asset. Rather, he is employed in the ordinary recurrent working operations of the business. The fact that some of his time (one day a week) is spent on activities related to the construction of the facility is incidental to his role and does not change the essential character of, or call for apportionment of, his salary.

In some cases, the Ruling states that apportionment of the costs on a fair and reasonable basis is required, using the best information available to the taxpayer, which may include:

- timesheets

- contractor agreements

- employee or contractor position descriptions

- information available in internal systems, including:

  – project resource allocations

– project planning schedules – activity tracking – cost centre allocations, and – project governance documents

- written reports, and

- correspondence.

  Other methods that may be reasonable include an analysis of:

- an employee's job description

- functions and expectations of the project, or

- historical knowledge about the proportion of time that the employee will devote to capital activities.

However, apportionment is not necessary in relation to either Richard Jones's or Peter Howell's salary.
