# 1-460 - Transfer from a foreign superannuation fund

Worked example: Transfer from a foreign superannuation fund Issue Deborah Richards, aged 57, moved permanently to Australia 10 years ago from the United Kingdom.

At the time she became an Australian resident, her investment in the UK-based Sunlife Provident Pension Fund was worth £120,000. This is a fund that provides benefits for Deborah's retirement upon reaching a prescribed age or by reason of permanent and total disability. It is not possible to use this fund for other purposes, such as housing or education.

During the year ended 30 June 2025, Deborah decided to transfer her UK pension fund to Australia. At that time, it was worth £300,000. She opted to receive the proceeds directly into her bank account, not into an Australian superannuation fund.

In the UK, Deborah is taxed on the £300,000 at her UK marginal rate. This gives her a UK tax charge of £114,000.

Assume that Deborah had $100,000 in other Australian taxable income in the year ended 30 June 2025.

Assume also that the UK has the primary taxing right in relation to the income from her pension fund.

Deborah comes to you for advice on whether the withdrawal from the UK pension fund is Australian taxable income and, if so, what is her Australian tax liability?

Solution The first thing that needs to be determined is whether the Sunlife Provident Pension Fund in which Deborah has invested is a foreign superannuation fund for Australian tax purposes. If it is not, the transfer will be subject to tax under ITAA36 s 99B.

There are strict rules about what constitutes a foreign superannuation fund. The Commissioner's view is that a foreign superannuation fund must exclusively provide a narrow range of benefits that are characterised by some specific future purpose, for example, the payment of superannuation benefits upon retirement, invalidity or death of the individual. That excludes accounts allowing withdrawals for other purposes, for example, housing or education.

Some popular US retirement schemes, such as individual retirement accounts which allow pre-retirement drawdowns for housing and school fees, are not considered superannuation funds. Transfers to Australia from such funds at any time would be taxable under s 99B and may crystallise significant tax liabilities in Australia.

The corpus of the trust is excluded from taxation under s 99B(2)(a), but the question as to what is the corpus is a difficult one. Any income earned that has not been paid out but accumulated over the years (which is generally the case with a pension fund) might be part of the corpus of the trust but would likely be assessable under s 99B.

Therefore, the taxpayer is likely to be taxed under s 99B on the difference between the value received and the level of contributions paid into the foreign pension over time. This is very different to the tax treatment if the fund is regarded as a superannuation fund, in which case only the growth in the fund since the taxpayer became an Australian resident would be taxed.

The question indicates that the taxpayer could access their benefits in the fund only on their retirement, upon reaching a prescribed age or by reason of permanent and total disability. As such, the fund would meet the definition of a superannuation fund and s 99B is unlikely to be relevant. The fund would be a foreign superannuation fund for the purposes of ITAA97 Subdiv 305-B and the tax treatment of lump sum payments would depend on how quickly the funds are paid to the taxpayer.

Payments within 6 months of the member becoming a resident Foreign superannuation lump sums are tax free provided they are transferred within 6 months of the individual member becoming a resident of Australia (ITAA97 s 305-60). They are non-assessable non- exempt income. A similar tax treatment is extended to superannuation lump sum payments received within 6 months of the termination of foreign employment, including payments due to retirement or death.

Payments more than 6 months after the member becomes a resident If the payment or transfer of funds occurs more than 6 months after the taxpayer becomes a resident for tax purposes, the applicable fund earnings will form part of the taxpayer's assessable income (s 305-70). The applicable fund earnings are (s 305-75(3)):

- the excess in Australian dollar terms over the amount that was vested in the fund just before the

  individual became a resident

- less further contributions after the individual became an Australian resident, and

- less any other amounts transferred into the foreign fund from other foreign funds.

The effect of s 305-75 is that the taxpayer is assessed only on the income arising in the fund during the residency period. Earnings made during periods of non-residency, and contributions and transfers into the fund, do not form part of the taxable amount when the lump sum benefit is paid.

This will result in tax being levied on the individual in relation to the growth, if any, in the foreign fund since the taxpayer's Australian residency commenced at the taxpayer's prevailing marginal tax rate.

Any bank charges deducted in relation to the transfer do not reduce the applicable fund earnings.

Using an exchange rate of AU$1:£0.57, the growth and non-growth components of Deborah's benefit would be as follows:

- gross lump sum — £300,000, or $526,316

- growth — £180,000, or $315,789

- non-growth — £120,000, or $210,526.

This applies regardless of whether the funds are received directly or transferred to an Australian superannuation fund. If transferred directly to a superannuation fund, the payment itself is treated as a non- concessional contribution and counts towards the non-concessional contributions cap.

Payment to an Australian superannuation fund Alternatively, if the taxpayer directed that the entire balance of the foreign fund be paid to an Australian superannuation fund, she could choose to treat the growth as a taxable contribution to the Australian superannuation fund. The growth amount would be included in the assessable income of the fund and taxed at the fund's tax rate of 15%.

Having the tax paid from the fund means that less of the transfer would count towards the individual's non- concessional contributions cap.

Foreign income tax offset If the lump sum amount was transferred into an Australian superannuation fund for the growth amount to be taxed at 15%, there is no foreign income tax offset (FITO) available for Deborah.

If payment was made directly to Deborah, a FITO would only be available to the extent the benefit is taxed in Australia, which would be different to the amount that is taxed in the United Kingdom. Therefore, foreign tax paid would need to be apportioned between the part of the fund that is taxable in Australia and the part that is not.

In the UK, the entire £300,000 was subject to tax, but in Australia only the growth component, or £180,000 ($315,789), is subject to tax in Deborah's Australian tax return. Accordingly, Deborah would only be eligible to receive 60% FITO with respect to the British tax paid.

Australian tax payable on $315,789 is $132,455 (excluding the Medicare levy, and bearing in mind that Deborah has $100,000 of other income). British tax paid was £114,000 in total — 60% of that is £68,400 or $120,000. So, Deborah's Australian tax liability would be $132,455 – $120,000 = $12,455.

The total tax payable by Deborah on £300,000 ($526,316) is equal to $212,455 (being UK tax of £114,000/0.57 = $200,000 plus top up Australian tax of $12,455), the equivalent of 40.37%.
