# 9-300 - Family trusts s 100A reimbursement arrangements

Worked example: Family trusts; s 100A reimbursement arrangements Issue The Callaghan Family Trust is a trust that was created by Harold Callaghan for the benefit of his spouse, children and other members of the Callaghan Family. The trustee is Wilson Pty Ltd, whose sole shareholder and controller is Harold Callaghan.

The Callaghan Family Trust derived assessable income of $550,000 in 2024–25. Harold's daughter, Shirley Callaghan, is a beneficiary of the trust. Shirley has recently celebrated her 18th birthday and works part time, earning $22,000 per year from her job. She has no other assessable income.

In May 2025, Shirley and her father meet and agree that any distribution of trust income that the trustee resolves to make will, after the payment of tax, be paid to Harold to reimburse him for part of the costs of maintaining and educating Shirley when she was a minor. These costs include school fees and other extracurricular activities, and they cover the period between Shirley's 5th birthday and her 18th birthday.

Harold has maintained records that indicate that these expenses amounted to $420,000.

The trustee resolves to distribute $210,000 to Shirley. However, this amount is actually paid into Harold's bank account in reimbursement for amounts spent by Harold in satisfying the costs of Shirley's maintenance and education, in line with their agreement. Harold pays Shirley's income tax liability.

Harold's other assessable income in the 2024–25 income year is $250,000.

Explain the tax consequences for the trust, for Harold personally and for Shirley arising from the above transactions.

Solution In Taxation Alert TA 2022/1, the Commissioner outlines his concerns with arrangements whereby trusts purport to distribute income to the adult children of the controllers of the trust but the economic benefits of the distribution (ie the underlying cash) is instead paid to the parents of the adult children.

The common feature of the arrangements is that trust income is appointed between members of the family group but in substance it is the parents who exercise control over and enjoy the economic benefit of the income.

The arrangements the Commissioner is concerned about are those which are more properly explained by the tax outcomes obtained, including the accessing of tax-free thresholds and lower marginal tax rates of family members, rather than ordinary familial considerations. The Commissioner is particularly concerned where the economic benefit of the arrangement is enjoyed by the parents because:

- the children are paying amounts for expenses that would ordinarily be met by their parents, or

- the children's entitlements are otherwise being applied for the benefit of the parents either directly or

  indirectly, or by the charging of excessive amounts, and/or

- there are elements of contrivance.

While TA 2022/1 is focused on arrangements involving the adult children of the controlling individuals, it makes clear that the Commissioner is also concerned with arrangements involving other family members of controlling individuals who have lower marginal tax rates than those of the controlling individual.

The following consequences may arise:

- the purported distribution to the children may be a sham or otherwise ineffective for trust purposes

- the arrangement may be a reimbursement agreement under ITAA36 s 100A

- ITAA36 ss 95A and 97(1) may apply to treat the parents as being presently entitled depending on the

  terms of the trust deed, or

- ITAA36 Pt IVA could apply.

  TA 2022/1 represents the Commissioner's finalised views.

Assessments issued in each of these scenarios would likely result in the distribution being taxed at the highest marginal income tax rate, potentially resulting in substantial increases in the tax liability of the trustee or the parents, as well as incurring interest on any consequential unpaid tax and potentially severe penalties.

Given the Commissioner's focus on trust reimbursement arrangements under s 100A (Taxation Ruling TR 2022/4 and Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2022/2 were issued about 10 months after TA 2022/1), it appears that the most likely approach taken by the Commissioner is to categorise this arrangement as a reimbursement agreement under s 100A.

This is an anti-avoidance provision that can apply to a trust distribution where a trust makes a beneficiary presently entitled to the income of the trust and the present entitlement arose out of a "reimbursement agreement". The beneficiary in such circumstances will be deemed to not have a present entitlement to the trust income and the trust distribution will be invalidated.

The term "reimbursement agreement" is very broadly defined; there does not need to be an agreement in writing and the agreement does not need to provide for a "reimbursement". It means an agreement to provide a benefit to or for a person, other than the presently entitled beneficiary. "Ordinary family or commercial dealings" are excluded from s 100A. To be in the course of ordinary dealing, the transactions between family members and their entities must be capable of explanation as achieving normal familial or regular commercial ends.

The ordinary family or commercial dealings exclusion does not apply simply because all parties to an agreement are members of the same family. In addition, if an arrangement has features suggesting it is being driven by tax outcomes, it will be invalidated.

TA 2022/1 disapplies the ordinary commercial dealings exclusion in cases such as this one by noting that the arrangement has the effect of avoiding tax on the net income of the trust by utilising the lower marginal tax rate applying to the children in circumstances where the benefit of the arrangement is, in substance, enjoyed by the parents.

Where s 100A applies, the ATO has the power to assess the trust distribution to the trustee of the trust, rather than the beneficiary made presently entitled to the income, at the top marginal rate of tax.

TR 2022/4 sets out the Commissioner's view on the scope of s 100A, and PCG 2022/2 seeks to define a risk assessment framework to enable taxpayers to determine whether their arrangements are low risk (green zone), or high risk (red zone).

In the context of the arrangement within the question, Shirley is made entitled to a trust distribution and this amount is used to reimburse her parents for expenses that they would ordinarily meet. The arrangement, which results in Harold obtaining the economic benefit of the trust income without that income being subject to tax at the top marginal tax rate he would otherwise have paid is, in the view of the Commissioner, more readily explained by the tax outcomes achieved rather than any familial objectives.

Specifically, Harold and Shirley have entered into these arrangements to avoid tax on the net income of the trust by utilising the lower marginal tax rate applying to Shirley in circumstances where the benefit from these arrangements is, in substance, enjoyed by Harold.

If s 100A applies, neither Harold nor Shirley will be taxed on the trust distributions. Instead, the trust distribution will be disregarded as it forms part of a "reimbursement agreement" and instead tax will be imposed on the trustee at the top marginal tax rate (currently 47% including the Medicare levy).
