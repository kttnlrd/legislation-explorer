# 7-170 - Salary sacrifice and superannuation guarantee charge

Worked example: Salary sacrifice and superannuation guarantee charge Issue Rosalind and Peter Russell are keen to boost their retirement savings and accordingly each has determined to contribute an additional $1,000 per quarter into their superannuation fund by way of a salary sacrifice arrangement with their respective employers, commencing 1 April 2025.

Rosalind Russell earns $60,000 per annum. Her employer paid $1,725 per quarter into her superannuation fund before the salary sacrifice arrangement came into effect. When Rosalind checks her superannuation account at the end of the financial year, she is disappointed that the superannuation contributions paid into her fund by her employer remained at $1,725 for the April to June 2025 quarter, despite the commencement of the salary sacrifice arrangement from 1 April 2025.

Peter Russell also earns $60,000 per annum. His employer also previously paid $1,725 per quarter into his superannuation fund before the salary sacrifice arrangement came into effect. When Peter checks his superannuation account at the end of the financial year, he notices that the superannuation contributions paid into his fund by his employer were $2,610 for the April to June 2025 quarter, $115 less than he expected.

Rosalind and Peter have asked for your advice as to why their employer's superannuation contributions are less than expected for the April to June 2025 quarter and what action they can take if their employers have contributed less than was required.

Solution Both Rosalind and Peter earn $15,000 per quarter. In the absence of the salary sacrifice arrangements, each of their employers would contribute $1,725 per quarter into their superannuation funds on their behalf ($15,000 × 11.5%) as superannuation guarantee (SG) contributions.

In Rosalind's case, by salary sacrificing $1,000 per quarter into super, her expectation is that the quarterly contribution her employer makes on her behalf will increase to $2,725 per quarter. However, it appears that her employer has used the $1,000 in salary sacrificed contributions to count towards the employer's mandated SG obligation. As such, the employer has continued to make a contribution of $1,725, mostly consisting of Rosalind's $1,000 salary sacrificed amount.

Peter also expects the quarterly contribution made by his employer to increase to $2,725. However, it appears that Peter's employer has calculated SG liability on his post-salary sacrifice wage ($14,000 instead of $15,000 per quarter). The employer contributed $2,610 per quarter ($1,610 mandated employer contribution + $1,000 salary sacrifice amount) instead of $2,725 ($1,725 + $1,000).

From 1 January 2020, the way in which Rosalind and Peter's SG contributions have been made by their employers is no longer lawful. From that date, SG contributions must be paid on the pre-salary sacrifice base. In addition, salary sacrificed amounts cannot be used to reduce the employer's required minimum level of SG contributions.

The Treasury Laws Amendment (2019 Tax Integrity and Other Measures No 1) Act 2019 closed a loophole that was used by some employers to reduce the level of SG contributions made for their employees.

Amounts that an employee salary sacrifices to superannuation cannot, from 1 January 2020, reduce an employer's obligation to make SG contributions for the employee, and Rosalind's $1,000 per quarter sacrificed salary cannot therefore reduce her employer's liability to make contributions on her behalf. The employer has actually made a contribution amounting to 4.83% ($725/$15,000 × 100). As the employer is required to contribute 11.5% of the ordinary time earnings (OTE) base (which is calculated as including the salary sacrificed amount), they must contribute an additional 6.67% to meet their minimum SG obligations.

The employer has a shortfall of $1,000 (6.67% × $15,000).

As sacrificed contributions no longer reduce the SG charge, Rosalind's employer will need to contribute $1,725 (the mandatory employer contributions) in addition to the $1,000 employee sacrificed amount, to avoid a shortfall and liability for the SG charge. The total payment of $2,725 is in line with the amount that Rosalind expected to see contributed.

In relation to Peter's situation, his employer has contributed 10.73% ($1,610/$15,000 × 100) as a result of the incorrect method of calculating SG contributions. Since 10.73% is less than the required 11.5%, the employer will have a charge percentage of 0.77% under s 19 of the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992 and a shortfall of $115.

To avoid the shortfall, Peter's employer must contribute $2,725 each quarter (consisting of $1,725 in mandated employer contribution and $1,000 in sacrificed amounts) which is the amount that Peter expected to see in contributions.

In order to take action to address the situation, Rosalind and Peter should first speak to their employers to see if the underpayments can be resolved. If that fails, they should lodge a complaint with the ATO, which can order the employers to make retrospective catch-up payments. An online form can be found at ato.gov.au.

In addition, the ATO itself may be able to identify the underpayments through Single Touch Payroll (STP). STP is a way of reporting certain payroll information to the ATO. Using STP-enabled software, employers are required to send details of employees' salary and wages payments, tax withheld and relevant superannuation events to the ATO as they occur.

Employers are not required to report superannuation contributions made for their employees, but the ATO receives this information from the superannuation funds that receive the contributions.

STP reporting began on 1 July 2019 for all employers, so Peter and Rosalind's employers should be using STP-enabled software.
