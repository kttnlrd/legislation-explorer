# 3-045 - Electric vehicles

Worked example: Electric vehicles Issue Cleancut Pty Ltd purchases an electric vehicle, a Hyundai Ionic 5, on 1 July 2024 for its managing director, which is expected to be used for both work and private purposes, split 50:50 between the 2. The cost of the vehicle is $72,000, excluding GST. Cleancut Pty Ltd wishes to understand the fringe benefits tax (FBT) and other taxation implications of the purchase.

In particular, the directors of Cleancut Pty Ltd wish to understand whether:

- the car will be subject to FBT

- the car will be a reportable fringe benefit, and

- an FBT return is necessary, assuming there are no other fringe benefits provided by the company.

The directors also wish to understand how the car will be treated for depreciation and GST purposes.

Cleancut Ltd has an aggregated turnover in excess of $50 million but less than $5 billion.

Solution From 1 July 2022, employers will not pay FBT on benefits provided for eligible electric cars and associated car expenses. However, the amounts are included as reportable fringe benefits amounts.

Benefits provided for electric cars are exempt from FBT if all the following criteria are met:

- the car is a zero or low emissions vehicle

- the first time the car is both held and used is on or after 1 July 2022

- the car is used by a current employee or their associates (such as family members)

- luxury car tax (LCT) has never been payable on the importation or sale of the car (ie for 2024–25, if it

  costs less than $91,387, being the LCT threshold for fuel efficient cars in that year)

Registration, insurance, repairs (or maintenance) and fuel expenses provided for eligible electric cars are also exempt from FBT. Note that a home charging station is not a car expense associated with providing a car fringe benefit for electric cars and is not, therefore, exempt (see ¶4-500).

A vehicle is a zero or low emissions vehicle if it satisfies both of these conditions:

- It is a battery electric vehicle, hydrogen fuel cell electric vehicle, or plug-in hybrid electric vehicle

  (FBTAA s 8A(2)).

- It is a car designed to carry a load of less than one tonne and fewer than 9 passengers (including the

  driver).

Accordingly, the Hyundai Ionic 5 is an FBT-free vehicle.

Although the private use of an eligible electric car, like the Hyundai Ionic 5, is exempt from FBT, Cleancut Pty Ltd still needs to determine the taxable value of the benefits provided and include it in the managing director's reportable fringe benefits amount (RFBA). This does not include the value of any associated car expense benefits (eg registration, etc). An employee has an RFBA if the total taxable value of certain fringe benefits provided to them (or their associate) is more than $2,000 in an FBT year, including the electric vehicle. The RFBA must be reported through Single Touch Payroll or on the employee's payment summary.

The amount of RFBA reported for the managing director is not added to his taxable income for determining income tax and Medicare Levy liabilities. However, it is added to his taxable income for calculating Medicare Levy Surcharge liability, and is included in income tests for family assistance, child support assessments and some other government benefits and obligations.

While it is not necessary for the managing director to keep a log book for the electric vehicle, if he expects to use the car for income-producing purposes, it might be prudent for him to keep a log book in case the FBT operating cost method would provide a lower reportable amount than the statutory method.

Assuming Cleancut Pty Ltd has not paid any FBT instalments during the relevant period and does not have an FBT liability (eg because they are only providing exempt fringe benefits), they should not need to lodge an FBT return.

Other tax implications This measure only impacts the FBT treatment. It has no impact on the car limit for depreciation or GST purposes.

If Cleancut Pty Ltd purchases a car and provides the vehicle to someone in their capacity as an employee and they work in the company's business, the company would be able to claim depreciation deductions on the vehicle (subject to the car depreciation limit).

A "car" is defined in ITAA97 s 995-1 as "a motor vehicle (except a motor cycle or similar vehicle) designed to carry a load of less than 1 tonne and fewer than 9 passengers".

Cleancut is able to depreciate the cost of the car using the general capital allowances provisions in Div 40 ITAA97. Depreciation applies to the cost of the vehicle, capped at $69,674 (see below), written off over the vehicle's effective life.

Cleancut can estimate the effective life of the vehicle or rely on the Commissioner's determination (ITAA97 s 40-95). Assuming that Cleancut chooses to rely on the Commissioner's determination, this means that the effective life of the vehicle is 8 years. Cleancut then has the choice of using either the prime cost method (ITAA97 s 40-75) or the diminishing value method (ITAA97 s 40-72) for calculating the annual depreciation deduction.

Note that the car limit for both tax depreciation purposes (ITAA97 s 40-230) and GST purposes (GST Act s 69-10) still applies where the electric vehicle is classified as a car. The car limit for depreciation deductions is $69,674 for the 2025 income year (which is different to the threshold that is used for determining whether the FBT exemption applies).

Accordingly, Cleancut Pty Ltd can claim a maximum deduction of $17,418. The balance of the purchase price — $2,326 ($72,000 − $69,674) — cannot be depreciated.

If the company purchases a car and provides the vehicle to someone in their capacity as an employee and they work in the company's business, the company will be able to claim back the GST credits on the purchase of the vehicle. The GST credits are limited to 1/11th of the car limit (which is $6,334 in 2024–25 based on a car limit of $69,674).

The fact that the car might be FBT exempt or that the employee uses the car partly for their own private purposes, should not impact on the ability of Cleancut Pty Ltd to claim an input tax credit.
