# 3-390 - FBT income tax and GST on end of year functions

Worked example: FBT, income tax and GST on end of year functions Issue Goose Pty Ltd holds a Christmas party for its staff, to which it also invites selected clients. The function is held off-site, at a local reception centre, on the evening of Saturday 9 December 2024 and is attended by 60 employees, 20 spouses or partners of employees and 20 clients.

The GST inclusive cost of the function was $20,000 split between the cost of food and drinks ($15,000) and the cost of hiring a band ($5,000). The company also paid the return taxi fares for the employees and their spouses/partners, with a total cost of $6,000 (none of the employees incurred more than $120 in tax fares).

In addition, the company provided each attendee with a theatre ticket to a well-known musical valued at $110 each and a bottle of wine costing $40 per head to thank all attendees for their contribution over the past year.

This is the only function held by Goose Pty Ltd during the FBT year ended 31 March 2025.

The company uses the actual method to value its meal entertainment.

Explain the FBT, income tax and GST consequences of the expenditure.

Solution FBT If an entity holds a work function (such as a Christmas or end-of-financial year party):

- on a working day, on business premises, and only for current employees, the cost of food and drink

  consumed is exempt from FBT, regardless of whether entertainment was involved or where the food

  was prepared.

- off the business premises, or the party includes associates of employees (such as their partners),

  no FBT is payable if the party is a minor benefit, even where entertainment is provided. An employee

  benefit which has a value of less than $300 may be a minor benefit (s 58P FBTAA 92). Where

  the employer provides an employee with separate benefits that are in connection with each other,

  for example, a meal, a night of accommodation and taxi travel, each individual benefit is counted

  separately for the purposes of the $300 exemption (para 272 of TR 2007/12). If the value of a benefit

  is indeed less than $300, it is then necessary to determine whether it would be unreasonable to treat

  the benefit as a fringe benefit by virtue of the value, frequency and regularity of its provision in order to

  determine if the minor benefit exemption applies.

In this case, the event is held off site and, in addition, includes associates of the employees. Therefore, Goose Pty Ltd has a potential FBT liability.

Typically, food and drink are regarded as "entertainment", as is the cost of travel (eg taxi fares) to and from the venue. The cost of hiring an entertainer (eg a band, comedian, etc) is a recreation expense, as are the theatre tickets. The cost of a bottle of wine is a non-entertainment gift (a property fringe benefit).

However, as the "per head" cost of each of the benefits provided is under $300 and their provision is infrequent and irregular (this is the only function held by Goose Pty Ltd in the year), the minor benefit exemption applies such that no FBT is payable.

Furthermore, where employees are provided with a Christmas gift (such as the bottle of wine), the minor benefits exemption also applies such that no FBT is payable if the value of the gift is less than $300 per person and it would be considered unreasonable to treat it as a fringe benefit.

FBT does not apply to the benefits provided to clients.

Income Tax and GST No income tax deduction or GST input tax credits can be claimed for the food and drinks expenditure, the provision of taxis and the theatre tickets as these benefits all have the character of entertainment and are FBT exempt (due to the minor benefit exemption).

A deduction also cannot be claimed in respect of the costs incurred in providing entertainment to clients. For the same reason (this is entertainment), input tax credits also cannot be claimed.

The cost of the bottle of wine is tax deductible (and input tax credits can be claimed), even though it is also FBT exempt for the employees (the minor benefits exemption applies). This is not entertainment but a gift (TD 94/55).
