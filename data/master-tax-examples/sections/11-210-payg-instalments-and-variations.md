# 11-210 - PAYG Instalments and variations

Worked example: PAYG Instalments and variations Issue Andrew Davies runs a small business from home, buying and selling items on Ebay. The business is a supplement to his income from teaching. For the year ended 30 June 2025, the ATO estimated that his quarterly PAYG instalment amount is $4,000.

Andrew pays this amount in his first quarter (1 July to 30 September) and second quarter (1 October to 31 December).

However, due to the increasing pressures of teaching, Andrew can no longer devote as much time to running his online business, and he becomes aware that for the year ended 30 June 2025, his turnover from the business will greatly decline. In January, he decides to vary his PAYG instalment amount on his third quarterly activity statement (1 January – 31 March) to take account of his revised business situation.

Working through his books for the business, Andrew estimates that the tax on his instalment income for the year ended 30 June 2025 will be only $12,000.

Advise Andrew how to make a variation to his PAYG instalments and how much the revised payment will be.

On submission of his tax return, it transpires that his turnover did not decline as much as expected and his final tax liability (before the instalment payments are taken into account) works out to be $14,000. Advise whether the ATO can impose interest or penalties for the shortfall.

Solution Under the Pay As You Go (PAYG) instalment regime (Div 45, Sch 1 TAA 1953), certain taxpayers deriving business or investment income are required to pay progressive instalments towards their income tax liability.

When the taxpayer lodges their tax return, these instalments are credited against their income tax liability and any excess amounts paid are refunded.

The calculation of these PAYG instalments, and the number of instalments required in a year, is dependent upon a taxpayer's circumstances. Taxpayers can vary the amount of a PAYG instalment if the total does not reflect their expected income tax liability for the year.

A taxpayer only needs to pay PAYG instalments if they have been notified by the ATO, based on information reported in the latest tax return. The ATO then works out the level of instalment income based on gross business and investment income, excluding GST and capital gains.

Alternatively, if the taxpayer is new to business, or expects to earn business and investment income above the relevant threshold, it is possible to voluntarily enter PAYG instalments.

Individuals and trusts are automatically required to pay PAYG instalments if they have:

- business and investment income in their most recent tax return of $4,000 or more for residents or $1

  or more for non-residents

- tax payable on their latest notice of assessment of $1,000 or more, and

- estimated tax for the current income year of $500 or more and they did not receive a full or partial

  SAPTO in the most recent assessment.

Companies and superannuation funds are liable where they:

- have business and investment income in their most recent tax return of $2 million or more

- have to have an instalment rate greater than 0% and have notional tax greater than $500, or

- are the head company of a consolidated group.

Taxpayers may be required to make PAYG instalments on a monthly, quarterly or annual basis. The ATO will advise a taxpayer of how often they need to pay PAYG instalments at the time they are first entered into the PAYG instalment system.

Most taxpayers make PAYG instalments on a quarterly basis. Taxpayers with business income of more than $20 million are required to make monthly PAYG instalments. Individuals eligible to make quarterly PAYG instalments that carry on a primary production business or are special professionals may have the choice to pay in 2 instalments. Some taxpayers may also be eligible to make annual PAYG instalments if their most recent estimated tax amount notified by the Commissioner was less than $8,000 (see ¶7-040).

Taxpayers can vary their PAYG instalments if the total instalments for the year, as advised by the ATO, will not reflect their expected income tax liability. Examples of when PAYG instalments can be varied include:

- reducing or expanding investment activity (or receiving significantly lower or higher dividends from a

  share portfolio)

- changes in the level of business activity and, therefore, business income

- having much higher or lower than expected tax deductions for about the same level of investment and

  business income

- having repaid all amounts owed under HELP or Student Financial Supplement Scheme (SFSS)

- changes in business structure, eg mergers, takeovers or internal restructuring.

There is no requirement to vary PAYG instalments. It will not change the total amount of income tax paid for the year.

After lodgement of the tax return, if the instalments were:

- too high, the taxpayer has the excess refunded to them

- too low, the shortfall must be paid.

However, underestimated PAYG instalments may result in a substantial tax bill when the relevant tax return is lodged after the year end. If varied instalments are less than 85% of the benchmark tax for the year, taxpayers may be liable for general interest charges on the difference.

Benchmark tax is not the same as the total tax payable as capital gains are not included in the calculation of benchmark tax (except for superannuation funds) (s 45-365 to 45-375). Further, certain offsets are disregarded.

Depending on the circumstances there may also be penalties, although these are rare. They require a scheme that is inconsistent with the purposes and objects of the PAYG instalments regime (Subdiv 45-P).

A straightforward use of structural features of the PAYG instalments system (such as varying an instalment) does not give rise to such a penalty (s 45-595(2)). Even if a tax benefit is found to exist, the "penalty" is generally just GIC.

To manually calculate the amounts of the quarterly instalment amount, the following method is followed.

- For the first quarter

– 25% of the taxpayer's estimated tax for the income year (the ATO advised GDP-adjusted notional tax will be used if the taxpayer has not varied the instalment and the estimated benchmark tax will be used where there has been a variation in this or a previous quarter)

- For the second quarter

  – 50% of the taxpayer's estimated tax for the income year

– minus the amount of the first quarter instalment

- third quarter

  – 75% of the taxpayer's estimated tax for the income year

– minus the amount of the first and second quarter instalments – plus any PAYG instalment credits claimed for the second quarter

- fourth quarter

  – 100% of the taxpayer's estimated tax for the income year

– minus the amount of the first, second and third quarter instalments – plus any PAYG instalment credits you claimed for the second and third quarters.

There are no penalties for tax shortfalls where a taxpayer does not vary the instalment amount or rate notified by the ATO.

In January, Andrew estimates that the tax on his instalment income for the year ended 30 June 2025 will be $12,000. Andrew works out his varied PAYG instalment as follows: Estimated tax: $12,000 Multiplied by 75% (how much to pay for the third quarter): $12,000 × 75% = $9,000 Minus amounts paid for the first and second quarters: $9,000 − ($4,000 + $4,000) = $1,000 The varied amount Andrew needs to pay in the third quarter is $1,000.

In the fourth quarter Andrew will pay his total estimated tax minus the amounts paid in the first 3 quarters: $12,000 − ($4,000 + $4,000 + $1,000) = $3,000 On his activity statement or instalment notice, Andrew must enter:

- his estimated tax for the year at T8

- his varied instalment amount for the period at T9

- the reason for the variation at T4

- his varied instalment amount at 5A – if he is filling in a paper form, enter the amount from T9.

Upon lodging his tax return for the year, his final tax liability for the year is $14,000, not $12,000 as he had estimated. As Andrew has paid $12,000 in PAYG instalments, he will be liable to pay the outstanding amount of $2,000 (income tax of $14,000 − $12,000 instalments already paid) As Andrew's varied instalments were more than 85% of his actual tax liability, the ATO will not charge any interest (or penalties) on the shortfall.

Footnotes 1      Capital gains (and other statutory income) are included for certain taxpayers, notably superannuation funds.
