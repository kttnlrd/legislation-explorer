# 2-312 - CGT consequences arising from holding cryptocurrency debit cards

Worked example: CGT consequences arising from holding cryptocurrency debit cards Issue During the year ended 30 June 2025, Mary Whittaker applied for and received a cryptocurrency debit card issued by Pacific Eastern Bank. Mary has used the card for various purchases from a number of retailers, both in-store and online, during the year. She has not given much thought to the tax implications of the card and her purchases during the year and, with the end of financial year in sight, she is becoming increasingly worried that the card and her associated spending may have caused tax problems.

Advise her of the potential tax implications.

Solution The ability to spend cryptocurrency as easily as fiat currency (ie Australian dollars) has been a long-held ambition in cryptocurrency circles. Now, with the introduction of cryptocurrency debit cards, that goal is within sight of being met. However, the tax implications of using a cryptocurrency debit card continue to cause problems, not least because too many people, like Mary, have been signing up for and using cryptocurrency debit cards without taking appropriate tax advice first.

A cryptocurrency debit card is linked to an individual's crypto wallet. In this way, the cryptocurrency debit card can be used anywhere regular debit cards are accepted, including for purchases online or at a retailer's physical premises, or to withdraw cash at an ATM. When the card is used, it draws on the individual's cryptocurrency balance to pay for the cost of the transaction, plus generally an additional amount to cover a transaction fee, converting it into fiat currency at the card's exchange rate. The fiat currency (in this case Australian dollars) is then spent or withdrawn as required using the Visa, Mastercard or EFTPOS network.

On the face of it, a cryptocurrency debit card appears to operate in a similar manner to a regular debit card.

As such, the tax issues associated with them are rarely considered.

However, there is a capital gains tax (CGT) risk because the exchange of cryptocurrency for fiat currency is considered to be a disposal of the cryptocurrency for CGT purposes. Therefore, a capital gain will arise if the capital proceeds from the disposal (ie the value of the cryptocurrency at the time of the exchange) exceed its cost base (which is basically its acquisition cost plus non-deductible ownership costs plus incidental disposal costs).

As such, each time goods or services are bought using the debit card (or a cash withdrawal takes place using the card), a portion of the cryptocurrency that backs that card will be sold, and the proceeds used to finance the purchase/withdrawal. So, each time this happens, the taxpayer will trigger either a capital gain or a capital loss in relation to that portion of their cryptocurrency holdings.

Any capital gain made on the disposal of cryptocurrency may be reduced by the CGT 50% general discount if the cryptocurrency was acquired at least 12 months before being disposed of. A capital loss may instead arise if the capital proceeds are less than the cryptocurrency's reduced cost base, which is the cost base less costs of ownership (eg interest on borrowings used to acquire the cryptocurrency, to the extent that they have not been deducted elsewhere).

The taxpayer could seek to avoid CGT by arguing that the purchases made using the card are for personal use and therefore the personal use exemption applies.

Personal use assets are CGT assets that are kept for your personal use or enjoyment. They are exempt from CGT where the first element of the asset's cost base is $10,000 or less. Mary would undoubtedly like to use this exemption in this case but, even if she has used the cryptocurrency debit card to buy items for her personal use or enjoyment, it may not be available.

The ATO's factsheet "Crypto asset as a personal use asset" (QC 69954) provides guidance on the issue of personal use assets. The factsheet notes that, where cryptocurrency is acquired and used within a short period of time to acquire items for personal use or consumption, the cryptocurrency is more likely to be a personal use asset. However, where the cryptocurrency is acquired and held for some time before any such transactions are made, or only a small proportion of the cryptocurrency acquired is used to make such transactions, it is less likely that the cryptocurrency is a personal use asset. In addition, the cryptocurrency will not be a personal use asset:

- when the cryptocurrency has to be exchanged for Australian dollars (or to a different cryptocurrency)

  to purchase items for personal use or consumption, or

- if a payment gateway or other bill payment intermediary has to be used to purchase or acquire

  the items on the taxpayer's behalf (rather than purchasing or acquiring the items directly with the

  cryptocurrency).

Cryptocurrency is generally not a personal use asset, in the ATO's view, unless it is directly used to acquire personal use items. Cryptocurrency debit cards are only indirectly used as the transaction involves first exchanging cryptocurrency to fiat currency before being used to make a payment.

Therefore, the cryptocurrency may be regarded as being held for investment purposes, and the personal use exemption does not apply, regardless of its ultimate use.

As the use of a cryptocurrency debit card triggers a disposal of cryptocurrency (and a capital gain or loss, as applicable), Mary should ensure that she keeps (or obtains) accurate records of transaction dates, the values of the cryptocurrency on these dates (in Australian dollars), what the transactions were for and who the other parties to the transactions were (even if this consists of just their cryptocurrency address).
