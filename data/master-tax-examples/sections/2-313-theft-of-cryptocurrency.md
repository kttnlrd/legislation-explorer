# 2-313 - Theft of cryptocurrency

Worked example: Theft of cryptocurrency Issue During the year ended 30 June 2025, Doris Smith decided to become a cryptocurrency trader.

On 1 August 2024, she was introduced to a trader-led platform called DoubleX, which appeared to allow more regular trading at lower fees than the alternatives. All transactions were based on the website, which was essentially like an online banking/brokerage platform. All trades could be viewed, and the balance and earnings were easy to track.

During September and October 2024, she withdrew amounts totalling $200,000 from her cash savings held in various bank accounts, and transferred them to exchanges to purchase cryptocurrency. She then transferred the cryptocurrency to DoubleX, to engage in cryptocurrency arbitrage trading.

She bought and sold cryptocurrency during the months following, tracking her trades and their performance on DoubleX's platform. However, on 1 April 2025, she read in the media that a number of investors who used the platform were having difficulty liquidating their investments, amid reports that the site was actually a scam.

On 1 May 2025, Doris attempted to liquidate her funds with the platform but failed; the site was indeed a scam and she was unable to access any of the monies she had invested. It appeared that the website owners had taken her funds from the initial investment and there was nothing left, with all transactions reported being entirely fictitious.

She reported the matter to the authorities and there is an active and ongoing investigation into the matter.

She also decided to engage an independent specialist crypto recovery organisation; however, attempts to recover the amounts that Doris invested have stalled and the advice that Doris received was that the funds have been moved into numerous other locations and could not be traced. DoubleX eventually ceased all communication and the company website was taken down.

Advise Doris of the tax implications of the loss of her funds.

Solution First of all, Doris is not entitled to a deduction under s 8-1 ITAA97.

This section generally allows a deduction for all losses and outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, except to the extent they are losses or outgoings of capital or of a capital, private or domestic nature.

However, a loss which is due to the misappropriation of funds is not deductible under s 8-1 but is capital in nature. In AAT Case V24 88 ATC 244, the taxpayer company, at the suggestion of its solicitor, entrusted moneys to that solicitor as its agent for the purposes of acquiring gold bullion and its immediate prearranged resale at a profit. However, the solicitor misappropriated the money in advance of the proposed scheme. The company claimed, as a deduction, the loss incurred from its dealings with the solicitor. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal held that a deduction was not allowed because the taxpayer proposed to embark on a course of action intended to produce a profit and the sum of money to be invested in that project was capital as it was to provide the profit-making structure. The act of misappropriation by the solicitor occasioned the loss, not any dealing in bullion.

In this case, it was Doris's intention to engage in trading on DoubleX and to create a passive income stream.

However, the facts indicate that DoubleX was acting in a fraudulent manner and the cryptocurrency that Doris acquired and transferred to it was misappropriated. However, the losses were not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income and are therefore not deductible under s 8-1.

The alternative is that the loss is a capital loss.

Taxation Determination TD 2014/26 explains that Bitcoin, and by extension, cryptocurrency in general, is a CGT asset.

Section 102-20 ITAA97 states that a capital gain or capital loss is made only if a CGT event happens. CGT event A1 occurred when Doris transferred cryptocurrency from the exchange to DoubleX, ie when she ceased to be the cryptocurrency's legal or beneficial owner due to the DoubleX scam.

Section 116-60(1) provides that the capital proceeds from a CGT event are reduced if an employee or agent misappropriates (whether by theft, embezzlement, larceny or otherwise) all or part of those proceeds. The capital proceeds are reduced by the amount misappropriated. An agent can be described as someone who acts on behalf of another. The person(s) running DoubleX were Doris's agent in "managing" her "investment".

Based on the information provided, it appears that the investment turned out to be a "scam" and the cryptocurrency that Doris transferred to the platform was lost.

Therefore, under s 116-60(2) the capital proceeds that Doris was entitled to receive from the CGT disposal event will be reduced by the amount misappropriated, meaning that she is entitled to claim a capital loss for the disposal of the cryptocurrency transferred from her cryptocurrency exchanges to DoubleX.

*Refs: s 8-1, s 116-60(2)*
