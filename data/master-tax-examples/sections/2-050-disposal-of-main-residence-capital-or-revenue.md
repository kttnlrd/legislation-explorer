# 2-050 - Disposal of main residence Capital or revenue

Worked example: Disposal of main residence: Capital or revenue?

Issue In November 2023, John Stevens decided to retire from his role as a security expert with a multinational company. Simultaneously, with the expectation of time on his hands, he sold his home in Brighton, Melbourne.

He then purchased a run-down property in a desirable street in South Melbourne for $1 million with a view to renovating and selling the property.

Over the course of the next 18 months, he undertook substantial renovations costing $300,000. He lived in the property while the work was being undertaken. In May 2025, he sold the South Melbourne property for $2 million and would like to claim the main residence exemption in his tax return.

John comes to you to confirm that the main residence exemption will be available. Advise John.

Solution Under s 118-110, a capital gain or loss made from a CGT event which occurs in relation to a dwelling that is the main residence of a taxpayer is excluded from the operation of the CGT provisions. Therefore, it appears at first glance as though John will indeed be able to claim the exemption.

However, where the disposal of a dwelling gives rise to a profit which is treated as ordinary income under s 6-5 (for example, as part of a business or an isolated profit making transaction), the main residence exemption will have no effect on the assessability of the gain (Taxation Determination TD 92/135), because it is not subject to CGT in the first place.

This was explored in detail in the recent AAT case of Bowerman v FC of T 2023 ATC ¶10-693, in which the tribunal found that losses incurred by a taxpayer on the disposal of a property were deductible despite the fact it had been the taxpayer's private residence.

Mrs Bowerman was a retiree with extensive experience investing in property. In July 2015, within weeks of her husband's death, she sold the matrimonial home in Sydney and signed a contract to purchase an apartment that was under development in a new complex, the Foreshore Boulevard apartment.

However, due to delays with the development of the complex, she decided to acquire a similar apartment which she would sell once the Foreshore Boulevard apartment was completed.

After residing at the second apartment for 2 years, she sold it, incurring a loss of $265,936 on the sale. She needed to use the proceeds of the sale to afford the purchase of the Foreshore Boulevard apartment.

Mrs Bowerman objected to the Notice of Assessment issued by the Commissioner in respect of the year ended 30 June 2020 (the period in which the sale of the second apartment occurred). She claimed that she had an allowable deduction under s 8-1 for the loss on the sale of the apartment.

The Commissioner disallowed the objection on the basis that the sale of the apartment was a mere realisation of a capital asset, and the loss was to be disregarded as a capital loss because the apartment was Mrs Bowerman's main residence. Mrs Bowerman subsequently filed an application for review with the AAT, arguing that the apartment was held on revenue account given that she acquired it for a profit-making purpose in a business operation or commercial transaction.

Mrs Bowerman's argument relied upon the principles established in Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Ltd 87 ATC 4363. Accordingly, she asserted that the loss was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and should thereby be deductible.

The objection was allowed in full by the Tribunal. Despite losing the case, the Commissioner chose not to appeal and later issued a Decision Impact Statement on the case, stating that "in circumstances where the principles in Myer Emporium (that is, a profit-making purpose and a commercial element to the transaction) do not apply, the Commissioner will continue to apply the CGT rules to gains and losses on the sale of real property including a person's main residence".

The issue for John in this case is that the principles of Myer Emporium might well apply in his case. The house was indeed purchased with a view to profit and there was a commercial element to the transaction and therefore the fact that he lived in the property as his main residence during the period of renovation may well not be enough to apply the main residence exemption, as in Bowerman.

In short, although the property was his main residence, this is only relevant where the property is held on capital account. In this case, the ATO might well argue (using the principles set out in Bowerman) that the property was held on revenue account and any profit should in fact be assessed under s 6-5. So, while the client is free to claim the main residence exemption, this should come with a warning that the ATO might challenge it on the above grounds.

A possible line of defence may be to argue that Bowerman does not apply as Mrs Bowerman was a long- term property developer, whereas John has no such history. However, the fact that this is John's first foray into property development does not necessarily mean that the asset was held on capital account.

*Refs: s 118-110, s 6-5, s 8-1*
