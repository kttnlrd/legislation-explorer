# 11-380 - Remission of additional superannuation guarantee charge

Worked example: Remission of additional superannuation guarantee charge Issue Tardy Pty Ltd is a small company based in Perth. Its turnover is consistently less than $50 million per annum.

Roger, the sole director of Tardy Pty Ltd, has been absent from the company, looking after his sick mother who has been terminally ill for several months and has now sadly died. Her illness caused Roger to take significant periods of leave for the second 6 months of the year ended 30 June 2025, because of which the financial management of the company suffered considerably. Not only did the company's trading results for the second half of the year slump but also Roger did not attend to the company's obligations regarding tax and superannuation.

The company has 3 employees aside from Roger. No superannuation guarantee was paid in respect of these employees (and in respect of Roger himself) for the March and June quarters.

Roger is now back at work and is striving to get the company back on an even keel. He has won several significant contracts which should enable the company to get back into the black, but he is worried about the possible SG penalties. He has heard that if a superannuation payment is missed, the company becomes liable to an automatic penalty of 200% of the outstanding superannuation.

Before addressing the company's superannuation guarantee backlog, Roger comes to you to find out whether the ATO would be prepared to remit the penalty, and if so, how much of the penalty they would be likely to charge or whether the penalty is simply imposed automatically, with no hope of remission.

The company has no previous history of lodging statements late and has never been audited by the ATO.

Solution Penalties may be imposed on employers for a breach of their SG obligations, including:

- an SG charge for entering into arrangements to avoid payment of the charge (SGA Act s 30),

  calculated on salary and wages (including any overtime)

- a Pt 7 penalty for failing to provide an SG statement, or statements or information for the purposes of

  assessing the employer's SG liability (SGA Act s 59)

- an administrative penalty or other penalties under the TAA.

A Pt 7 penalty is payable by an employer who fails to provide, when and as required, an SG statement or information relevant to assessing the employer's liability to pay an SG charge for a quarter, for example:

- where an employer lodges an SG statement for a quarter after the due date, or

- where the ATO makes a default assessment of an employer's liability for the SG charge because

  the employer has not lodged an SG statement for a quarter, and the ATO is of the opinion that the

  employer is liable to pay SG charge for the quarter.

The Pt 7 penalty, which is automatically imposed on an employer by law, is equal to 200% of the amount of the SG charge payable for the quarter (minimum penalty is $20) (SGA Act s 59).

However, the ATO does, in certain circumstances, remit this penalty.

In 2020, the ATO released Law Administration Practice Statement PS LA 2020/4 dealing with how ATO officers are to exercise their discretion to remit Pt 7 penalties. PS LA 2020/4 directed ATO officers that they were not to remit these Pt 7 penalties below 100% of the SG charge except in the most exceptional circumstances.

This was criticised because it did not appear to cater for the difference in circumstances between, for example, an employer with a good compliance history who inadvertently made SG payments a couple of days late and repeat offenders with poor compliance history and little regard for their SG and overall tax obligations.

In response to the criticism, the ATO released Law Administration Practice Statement PS LA 2021/3, which sets out a new administrative approach to remission of Pt 7 penalties. This document replaces PS LA 2020/4.

Importantly, para 7 of PS LA 2021/3 notes that an employer should be considered for a penalty relief arrangement (ie where the employer is provided with additional remission in conjunction with a direction for education) where they have a turnover of less than $50 million and they:

- took voluntary action to comply with their obligation to lodge SG statements

- do not have a history of lodging SG statements late

- have lodged no more than 4 SG statements after the lodgment due date

- have no previous SG audits where they were found to have not met their SG obligations, and

- have not previously been provided with penalty relief.

  Penalty relief would not be appropriate where the employer has:

- been issued with an SGC default assessment

- lodged more than 4 SG statements after the lodgment due date, or

- previously been issued with an SG education direction.

So, following the principles set out in PS LA 2021/3, it is possible for penalties to be remitted in full where there is overall good compliance behaviour. This contrasts with the approach in PS LA 2020/4 where remission below 100% was permitted only in the most exceptional of circumstances.

An employer should not be provided with penalty relief at any point before the relevant SG assessments have been finalised and the ATO is ready to finalise its remission decision.

An employer cannot apply for penalty relief, and an employer cannot specifically object to a decision not to apply it.

Therefore, in Roger's circumstances, there is a good chance that he will be able to get the penalty remitted to nil as Tardy Pty Ltd meets all the criteria laid out in s 7 of PS LA 2021/3.

Applying the 4-step penalty remission process set out in the Appendix to PS LA 2021/3, Tardy Pty Ltd should be entitled to remit the penalty by 40% as late payment will have been made before the ATO contacting the company and payment is to be made less than 3 months after the due date (where payments have been made with varying degrees of delay in different quarters within a period that is being considered for remission, the ATO says it will adopt a remission level corresponding to the greatest degree of attempt to comply that the employer has demonstrated across the period. Therefore, even though the March quarter payment is more than 3 months late, he will be judged in relation to the June quarter, which is less than 3 months late). In addition, a further 90% remission will be available because he intends to lodge a late SG statement before any ATO contact. Therefore, the combination of the first 2 steps of the process is sufficient to eliminate the penalty altogether and there is nothing in steps 3 or 4 that could potentially lead to a decrease in remission.
