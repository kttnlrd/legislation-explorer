# 2-480 - CGT events I1 and I2 Ceasing to be an Australian resident

Worked example: CGT events I1 and I2: Ceasing to be an Australian resident Issue Melanie is an Australian tax resident and works in the Sydney office of a major investment bank, which is headquartered in London. On 1 May 2025, she is posted to the UK for a 5-year secondment, with the possibility of a further extension. Melanie relocates to the UK together with her husband and children. During her time in the UK, Melanie is a foreign resident for Australian tax purposes.

Melanie owns Australian listed shares, an investment property in Queensland and her family home (which she intends to rent out during her absence). Melanie acquired all of these assets post-CGT.

The value of the shares has gone up substantially since Melanie acquired them in 2010, meaning that, based on current market value, there is an unrealised gain in relation to the shares amounting to $120,000.

Melanie does not have any capital losses, either brought forward or arising in the year.

What are the CGT implications for Melanie on becoming a foreign resident for Australian tax purposes?

Solution If a taxpayer ceases to be an Australian resident (ie becomes a foreign resident), CGT event I1 is triggered in respect of certain assets owned by the taxpayer at the time of becoming a foreign resident (similarly, CGT Event I2 happens if a trust stops being an Australian resident trust for CGT purposes).

The time of the event is when the individual or company (or a trust, in the case of CGT event I2) stops being an Australian resident (s 104-160 ITAA97).

If the taxpayer is an individual, they are required, under s 104-160(3), to work out if they have made a capital gain or a capital loss for each CGT asset owned just before the time of the event, ie for each asset owned just before the individual ceased to be an Australian resident.

The only exceptions relate to:

- taxable Australian property (with the exception of an indirect Australian real property interest or an

  option or right to acquire such an interest), and

- assets acquired before 20 September 1985 (as the capital gain or loss is disregarded under s

  104-160(5) or 104-170(5)).

The concept of taxable Australian property (TAP) is further discussed at ¶2-500.

The reason for excluding TAP assets (except an indirect Australian real property interest and an option or right to acquire such an interest) is because these assets are always subject to Australian CGT, regardless of the residency status of the owner of the asset.

Under s 104-160(4), the taxpayer makes a capital gain if the market value of the asset (at the time of the event) is more than its cost base. The taxpayer makes a capital loss if that market value is less than the asset's reduced cost base.

An individual can also choose to disregard the capital gains and losses from all CGT assets caught by CGT events I1 and I2 (s 104-165(2)). If this choice is made, each of those assets is taken to be taxable Australian property until the earlier of:

- when another CGT event happens to the asset, possibly crystallising a capital gain or loss at that

  time, or

- when the taxpayer again becomes a resident, at which time it again becomes an ordinary asset within

  the CGT net (s 104-165).

Where this choice is made, the Australian CGT provisions will apply regardless of the residency status of the owner of the asset.

If an election is made under s 104-165(2) and a CGT event does not happen in relation to the asset (which results in the taxpayer no longer owning the asset) during the period of non-residence, the choice ceases to apply from the time the taxpayer becomes an Australian resident again. This is because Australian residents are subject to CGT on all assets regardless of whether they are TAP, which means that the asset is already back within the Australian CGT system.

For the following reasons, many taxpayers will make a choice to disregard a capital gain:

- When CGT event I1 is triggered, the taxpayer is "deemed" to have made a disposal. As a result, there

  are no capital proceeds from which the taxpayer can pay their tax liability.

- The choice under s 104-165(2) allows an indefinite deferral of any capital gain arising on the change

  of residency (ie until the asset is ultimately disposed of).

However, it must be remembered that sometimes triggering CGT event I1 can be used to a taxpayer's advantage, especially where a capital loss can be crystallised and utilised, or when the taxpayer is not entitled to the general CGT discount after becoming a non-resident.

In determining whether to make this election or not, the taxpayer needs to consider any expected increases in the value of the asset and the potential reduction of the general discount for the period of non-residency, which may increase the assessable gain, against paying tax prior to actual disposal. The impact of any double tax agreement should also be considered.

Generally speaking, the way in which a taxpayer prepares their tax return is sufficient evidence of their choice (s 103-25 ITAA97).

If an election is not made, upon ceasing residency, normally CGT event I1 will be triggered and Melanie will be taken to have disposed of each of her CGT assets, unless they are TAP assets. The associated CGT implications for Melanie of CGT event I1 being triggered are as follows:

- Investment property and the family home — both of these CGT assets are TAP assets and

  therefore no capital gain (or loss) will arise when Melanie ceases to be an Australian resident.

However, as they are TAP assets, their eventual disposal will continue to be subject to Australian CGT, irrespective of Melanie's residency status at the time of sale.

- Australian listed company shares — as the shares are not TAP assets (as defined in s 855-15),

CGT event I1 is triggered. Therefore, a capital gain (of $120,000, before the 50% discount) may arise as a result of Melanie's change in residency.

In relation to the shares, Melanie will have crystallised a taxable capital gain of $60,000 in the 2025 income year (ie after applying the 50% general discount). Therefore, she will have a tax liability of up to $27,000 (ie $60,000 x 45%), even though she has received no proceeds from the deemed disposal.

Furthermore, should the shares drop in value before Melanie sells them, she will not be able to recoup any of the tax paid as the shares would be outside of the Australian tax system.

However, Melanie also has the choice to disregard her capital gains from CGT event I1. If this happens and she later sells the shares while she is still a non-resident for Australian tax purposes, the Australian CGT rules will still apply to the sale.

This is because the shares are treated as TAP under the choice, resulting in them still being subject to Australian CGT for foreign residents. The general 50% CGT discount will not be available for any period of non-residence (see ¶2-500).
