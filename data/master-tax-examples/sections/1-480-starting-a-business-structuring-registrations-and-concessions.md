# 1-480 - Starting a business structuring registrations and concessions

Worked example: Starting a business; structuring, registrations and concessions Issue Ezra Manning is an amateur baker who has won a number of competitions for her cakes, bread and pastries.

She is planning to give up her "day job" as a graphic designer in Melbourne and commence a bakery business. As part of that, she expects to rent a small industrial unit in the north of the city, she will acquire baking equipment and over time will recruit a small number of staff. She had a feasibility study prepared for her and some initial costings were prepared by a team of consultants who charged her $3,350 for both assignments. Based on the outcome of that work, the proposal looks feasible, and she intends to proceed.

Ezra is married with 2 adult children. Neither her spouse nor her children will be involved in running or working in the business.

Ezra comes to you seeking advice on the best way to structure her business. In addition, she wishes to claim a tax deduction for the $3,350 consultancy fees already spent and also some additional costs expected to be incurred in setting up the business. Lastly, she seeks information on what tax registrations she will require and how she should go about obtaining them.

Solution Structuring Ezra needs to consider both short- and longer-term factors when setting up her business. She needs to take into account factors associated with the establishment of the business and its ongoing operation, the ease with which it allows new equity participants (if required) and her eventual exit from the structure.

Amongst the factors that Ezra should bear in mind are the following:

- whether income can be retained tax effectively in the structure (or paid out tax effectively to Ezra and

  her associates)

- the tax rate that will be applied to profits retained in the structure, and

- the capital gains tax (CGT) implications for:

  – disposal of assets held within the structure

– the availability of the 50% CGT discount, and – eligibility for the ITAA97 Div 152 small business CGT concessions.

The following factors may also have a bearing on the choice of a structure:

- the expected level of profit to be generated, and

- whether the business involves personal services.

As the purpose of this business is to manufacture goods for resale, the latter point is not relevant to Ezra.

Sole trader The main advantage of this structure is its simplicity, in that there is less red tape to start the business, and the associated legal and professional costs are minimal. When a business is run as a sole trader, the business's income and expenses are simply recorded at the business schedules in the personal tax return.

From a tax point of view, the main advantage is that, if it takes time to build the business up, any tax losses can usually be applied at the individual level against all other forms of assessable income, including salary and wages and income from other business activities.

On the downside, once the business starts trading at a profit, income tax is payable at whatever the marginal tax rate is for the sole trader (which could be up to 45% for those earning more than $180,000). The potential to split income between family members, which is often available where a trust is used as the business vehicle, does not exist.

In addition, setting up as a sole trader does not provide any form of asset protection from creditors or protection in the event of family break-ups.

Discretionary trust A trust is a business structure where a trustee (an individual or company) carries out the business on behalf of the members (or beneficiaries) of the trust.

Family businesses are often set up as a trust so that each family member can be made a beneficiary without having any involvement in how the business is run.

The major advantage of using a discretionary trust to run a business is that the trustee is able to decide who benefits from the income of the trust. So, when the business starts trading profitably, the trust will be able to distribute its income in the most tax effective way permitted by the trust deed, typically to the beneficiaries with the lowest marginal tax rates.

Each beneficiary records their share of the income or capital gains of the trust in their personal tax return and pays the tax themselves at their marginal rates. The trust usually only pays tax (at the highest rate) if it does not distribute all the profits that arise in the business.

There are also asset protection advantages in holding assets through a discretionary trust. Because the beneficiaries of the trust are not the legal owners of the business, creditors cannot easily access the assets of the business if a particular beneficiary encounters financial problems.

Company The other possible scenario is to set up the business through a company.

Shareholders own the company while directors run it. With many small businesses, the company directors are also the shareholders. To become a company, an entity must:

- be incorporated under the Corporations Act 2001, and

- be registered with the Australian Securities and Investment Commission (ASIC).

The company is a separate legal entity to the people who run it. That means that the company lodges its own tax return and pays tax on its profits at the company tax rate — currently 25% (provided the company's annual aggregate turnover is less than $50 million and the company is a base rate entity, otherwise 30%).

The company can then distribute profits to shareholders in the form of franked dividends. These dividends are taxable to the shareholders less a credit for the tax already paid by the company.

In some cases, companies do not pay out profits to shareholders but retain the profits, possibly for future investment in the business. In that sense, companies can be regarded as tax shelters since the rate of tax payable by the company (25% or 30%) is significantly lower than the higher rates of personal taxation. That is only part of the story of course; ultimately the cash in the company needs to be extracted and at that point tax will need to be paid, so the tax is deferred rather than avoided.

The most common reason why people choose a corporate structure is that it provides limited liability to the shareholders. The extent to which shareholders are liable for the debts of the company is limited to the amount they have invested as share capital. There are also asset protection benefits because creditors of the company cannot access the assets of the shareholders.

On the downside, companies cannot access the general 50% CGT discount. In addition, use of the 50% active asset discount is not tax effective for the shareholder as the subsequent payment out of the company will be treated as a dividend and unless there were sufficient franking credits available from other sources, the dividend would be unfranked. So, a company can only tax-effectively distribute amounts sheltered by the 50% active asset discount to shareholders on liquidation. Setting up and maintaining a company is also more expensive than the alternatives, with greater compliance obligations imposed by regulators like ASIC.

A hybrid Many people opt for a mixed structure, often running their trade through a company, which is then owned by a discretionary trust. This provides both the asset protection and lower tax rate advantages of a company combined with the ability to stream income (in the form of dividends) to beneficiaries of the trust.

Alternatively, they run the business through a trust, which has a company as either sole trustee or one of a number of trustees. The trust can then stream profits to the corporate trustee, which is taxed at the corporate rate, rather than the higher personal rates.

The more complex the structure, the higher the fees for setting it up and maintaining it. In addition, compliance costs tend to be higher for a mixed structure because there is a need to prepare accounts, tax returns, corporate filings, etc, for different entities.

Deductibility of costs incurred before the business starts It is quite common to incur costs relating to a proposed business even before trading has started. Certain costs incurred can be claimed even before the business starts.

Under ITAA97 s 40-880, taxpayers are entitled to deduct certain business-related capital expenditure (known as "blackhole expenditure") that is neither deductible under a provision of ITAA36 or ITAA97, nor otherwise taken into account for income tax purposes, for example, by being included in the cost base of a CGT asset or included in the cost of a depreciating asset.

The general rule is that the deduction is claimed over 5 years, or an immediate deduction may be available for certain start-up expenses incurred by taxpayers who are not in business or who are small business entities.

Where deductions are claimed over a 5-year period, there is an exception to the general rule that the deduction is first available in the year in which the expenditure is incurred where the expense is in relation to a business that is proposed to be carried on. In such instances, the deduction only commences once the business commences.

An immediate deduction for expenses that may otherwise be deductible over 5 years is available where:

- the expenditure relates to a business that is proposed to be carried on

- the expenditure is either:

– incurred in obtaining advice or services relating to the proposed structure or the proposed operation of the business (discussed below), or – a payment to an Australian government agency of a fee, tax or charge incurred in relation to setting up the business or establishing its operating structure (discussed below), and

- the entity that incurs the expenditure:

  – is, for that income year, a small business entity

– is, for the 2020–21 and later income years, a medium business entity, or – does not carry on a business and does not control, and is not controlled by, an entity carrying on a business in the relevant income year that is not a small business entity (or medium business entity for 2020–21 and later income years).

Examples of what could be claimable include:

- advice from a lawyer or accountant on how the business may be best structured

- services from a lawyer or accountant in relation to setting up legal arrangements or business systems

  for the business structure

- professional advice on the viability of the proposed business (including due diligence where an

  existing business is being purchased) and the development of a business plan, and

- costs associated with raising capital (whether debt or equity) for the operation of the proposed

  business.

An immediate deduction may be also claimed where the expenditure involves payments to Australian government agencies (Commonwealth, a state or territory or an authority thereof). This includes taxes, fees or charges relating to establishing the business or its structure paid to an Australian government agency.

These costs can be claimed by whoever incurs them, even if the business ends up in a different entity (eg a cost is incurred by an individual but the business is ultimately run through a company).

Therefore, Ezra can claim an immediate deduction for the $3,350 paid to the consultants for the feasibility study and costings and also for the fee paid for advice regarding the structuring, etc. Additional costs that she incurs in starting up the business may be deductible to the extent that they meet the criteria for the deduction set out in s 40-880.

Ezra is able to claim a deduction for the costs because she does not currently carry on a business and does not control, and is not controlled by, a small or medium business entity.

What registrations does she need?

Tax file number If the business is being operated as a sole trader, there is no requirement to obtain a tax file number (TFN).

The business will simply use the individual's own TFN.

If instead the business is run through another entity, such as a trust, partnership or company, that entity will need its own TFN. The procedure for applying for a business TFN is different to applying for an individual one. A TFN can be obtained via the Australian Business Register (ABR) website (Apply for a TFN for business | ABR).

The ABR is the central registry of Australian business information so, as well as obtaining a TFN, it is possible to obtain an Australian business number (ABN), register for GST, apply for pay-as-you-go (PAYG) withholding and register the business name.

Australian business number An ABN is essential for any Australian business since it is used in numerous business interactions, with customers, suppliers and other government agencies. An ABN will be required, for instance, before the business can register for GST. If the business does not have an ABN, other businesses must withhold 47% tax on any payments they make to the business for goods or services.

An ABN can be obtained through the ABR website (Applying for an ABN | ABR).

Australian company number (ACN) This will only be required if the intention is to run the business through a company. If so, this will be required before applying for an ABN. An ACN can be obtained through the ASIC website (Steps to register a company | ASIC - Australian Securities and Investments Commission).

Director ID If a company is involved, it is necessary for all directors of that company to apply for a director ID. A director ID is a unique identifier that is attached to a director forever, and it helps to prevent the use of false or fraudulent director identities. There is no need to apply for another director ID where the individual becomes a director of other companies. For individuals who first became a director from 5 April 2022, it is necessary to apply for a director ID before commencement of the appointment. A director ID can be obtained at Apply for your director ID | Australian Business Registry Services (ABRS).

Pay-as-you-go withholding Registration for PAYG withholding is required where there is an intention to employ people who will be paid wages or salary. Tax must be deducted from each wage or salary payment and paid to the ATO through the PAYG system.

Registration is completed through the ABR website (see above). Where a business already has an ABN, registration is through online services for business, accessed through the ATO website.

Goods and services tax A business generally needs to register for GST once its annual turnover is $75,000 or more.

Once the annual turnover threshold is passed, registration is necessary within 21 days, so it is worthwhile monitoring turnover each month to see if this point is being reached. Registration is voluntary before that point.

Where a new business has been started and there is an expectation that it will reach the GST turnover threshold or more in its first year of operation, it is sensible to register for GST even if the $75,000 threshold has not yet been reached.

Registration is via the ABR website (see above) or online services for business, via the ATO website.

Note that registration is only required once for GST, even where the registrant operates more than one business. So, where an individual runs, for example, a pastry shop and a clothing shop, only one GST registration is needed unless one of the businesses is operated through a different entity, such as a company.
