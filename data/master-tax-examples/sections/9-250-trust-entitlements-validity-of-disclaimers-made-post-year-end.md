# 9-250 - Trust entitlements validity of disclaimers made post year end

Worked example: Trust entitlements: validity of disclaimers made post year end Issue The Scarborough Family Trust was established in 2013 by Richard Corrigan, who was also the sole trustee.

The primary beneficiaries of the trust were Richard's adult children, Roger, Debra and Jacob. It was Richard's custom over many years to pay any income of the trust to varying combinations of the 3 children, taking advantage of the fact that all 3 had low levels of taxable income.

The trust deed contained a default beneficiary clause. This stated that in the event that the trust income was not paid out to the primary beneficiaries by the end of the financial year, Richard's cousins, Elizabeth and Joan were to benefit instead. However, no trust income has ever been paid to Elizabeth and Joan and they had never been told of their default beneficiary status.

Due to ill health, Richard did not resolve to distribute the income of the trust to the primary beneficiaries by 30 June 2024.

On hearing of the situation, and to avoid a substantial income tax liability, Elizabeth and Joan decided to disclaim their default interests in the trust on 1 September 2024.

Will the disclaimers made by Elizabeth and Joan be accepted by the ATO?

Solution This issue is similar to the one which was decided by the High Court in the recent case FC of T v Carter & Ors 2022 ATC ¶20-822; [2022] HCA 10. The main issue in that case was the taxation of gains from the sale of properties held in a trust. The question for the High Court was whether the default beneficiaries who were entitled to those gains under the deed remained liable to tax despite validly disclaiming their right to those gains after the year end.

Under ITAA36 s 97, a beneficiary who is presently entitled to a share of the income of a trust for a particular year includes in their assessable income that share of the trust's net (taxable) income for that year. The default beneficiaries in this case subsequently executed a series of disclaimers in respect of their default entitlements. The third (and final) of these disclaimers were expressed broadly to disclaim any and all rights and interests conferred by the trust deed to any income.

The High Court, on appeal, held that a legally effective disclaimer executed by a default beneficiary after year end did not have the effect of retrospectively avoiding the application of s 97. As such, the beneficiaries were presently entitled to income within the meaning of s 97 as at 30 June. The Court said that s 97(1) was drafted to tax a beneficiary by reference to present entitlement not receipt. Beneficiaries were to be assessed on their share of a trust's net income based on their present entitlement immediately before the end of the relevant income year. The question of a beneficiary's present entitlement was to be tested and examined at the close of the financial year and not some reasonable period of time after the end of the financial year.

The ATO stated, in its Decision Impact Statement on the Carter case, that the High Court decision settles the question regarding the tax effect of disclaiming trust entitlements in a legally effective manner sometime after financial year end. It tells us that such disclaimers do not disturb what would otherwise be the tax result.

Therefore, Elizabeth and Joan may well have lodged a legally effective disclaimer for trust purposes but for tax purposes it was ineffective because it was prepared after the year end and the trust distributions it was disclaiming were in relation to the year ended 30 June 2024, the previous financial year.

The High Court's decision overturns previous decisions such as FC of T v Ramsden 2005 ATC 4136; [2005] FCAFC 39. In addition, the ATO says in Interpretative Decision ID 2010/85 (Trust income: disclaimer of an entitlement to trust income) that a beneficiary who has disclaimed trust income was not presently entitled for the purposes of s 97. The ATO has withdrawn ID 2010/85 as a result. If the ATO amends an assessment as a result of the Carter decision, taxpayers who relied on ID 2010/85 have administrative protection against penalties and interest but not against tax.

With many trust deeds, it is common to find a provision that creates a default distribution to individual beneficiaries in the event of the trustee failing to exercise its discretion. Accordingly, where discretionary trusts are created, consideration should be given as to whether it is still appropriate to include such provisions as, based on the outcome of Carter, this could result in a substantial tax liability if the default distribution clause is triggered.

In addition, trustees, and their advisors, need to be aware that once the trustee appoints income, those who are presently entitled to that income just before the end of the financial year will be assessed on their share of the net income of the trust under s 97. Any subsequent disclaimer, while effective at law, will not be effective for tax purposes.

As part of year-end tax planning, where beneficiaries (including default beneficiaries) will be presently entitled to income on 30 June, the beneficiary should be given notice of that intention before it is conferred or the default clause applies.
