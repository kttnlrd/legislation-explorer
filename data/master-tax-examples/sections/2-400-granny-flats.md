# 2-400 - Granny flats

Worked example: Granny flats Issue Rita is a 79-year-old widow who has found that her own home is too much for her to manage. Accordingly, after discussing her situation with her daughter, Susan, and her son-in-law, Robert, it is decided that she will sell her home and move into the property that is jointly owned by Susan and Robert, who live there with their children.

In November 2024, Rita, Susan and Robert formalise a granny flat arrangement by entering into a written agreement under which Susan and Robert will grant Rita a "right to occupy" their home for life, along with a right to ongoing care and support.

The agreement states that Rita will contribute $150,000 towards the cost of building a granny flat on the property and that she will pay $100 per week to cover her share of the utility bills (electricity, gas, etc).

The agreement provides that this right to occupy will terminate should Rita pass away or permanently vacate the flat due to ill health (eg to move into a residential care facility). If the right terminates for either of these reasons, it is agreed that nothing will be repaid to Rita.

The right to occupy will also terminate should Susan and Robert both pass away, should the property be sold or should the relationship between the parties break down. It is agreed that Rita's $150,000 contribution will be returned to her if the right terminates for any of these reasons.

The written agreement confirms that the parties intend to be legally bound by the arrangement.

The granny flat is fully self-contained, and Rita eats and sleeps there. Nevertheless, Rita's daily life is closely integrated with those of Susan and Robert. She spends most evenings and some days in the main house and family members are often in her flat.

Susan and Robert come to you for advice on whether they will trigger a capital gain by entering into the granny flat arrangement.

In addition, Susan and Robert are keen to understand the long-term repercussions of the granny flat arrangement. Assume that, some years later, following a deterioration in Rita's health, Rita moves into a nursing home and the granny flat arrangement is terminated. Assume further that, at that time, Susan and Robert decide to sell the property, which results in a capital gain arising under capital gains tax (CGT) event A1. Will a full main residence exemption be available with respect to the sale of Susan and Robert's home?

Solution Will Susan and Robert trigger a capital gain by entering into the granny flat arrangement?

No. Under the exemption set out in ITAA97 s 137-15 (applicable from 1 July 2021), CGT event D1 (creating contractual or other rights) that would otherwise have arisen from the creation of Rita's right to occupy the flat on Susan and Robert's property (and which would, in the absence of the new law, lead to a capital gain of $150,000) will not happen and Susan and Robert will have no CGT liability resulting from the creation of the right.

Certain requirements that must be met for s 137-15 to apply are satisfied in this case, as follows:

- Eligibility for a granny flat interest: First, an individual must be "eligible for a granny flat interest",

  that is, the individual has either reached pension age or has a disability that means they require

  assistance for most day-to-day activities for at least 12 months (ss 137-15(a) and 137-20(a)). Rita is

  eligible because she has reached pension age (she is 79 years old).

- Owning, or agreeing to acquire, the dwelling: Second, an individual must either own the dwelling

  where the granny flat interest is held, or is to be held, at the time of entering into or varying the

  arrangement, or agree to acquire such a dwelling under the arrangement (ss 137-15(b) and 137-20(b)).

This is met because Susan and Robert own the dwelling where the granny flat interest is held.

- Parties to the arrangement: Third, both the individual who is to hold the granny flat interest and the

  individual who owns (or agrees to acquire) the dwelling where the granny flat interest is to be held,

  must be parties to the arrangement (ss 137-15(c) and 137-20(c)). In this case, Rita, Susan and Robert

  are all parties to the agreement.

- Legally binding and written arrangement: Fourth, the arrangement must be in writing and indicate

  an intention for the parties to be legally bound by it (ss 137-15(d) and 137-20(d)). A formal arrangement

  would deal with basic matters such as who the parties to the arrangement are, the circumstances in

  which the arrangement could be varied or terminated, and what happens on variation or termination.

The arrangement in this case is in writing and it indicates that all the parties intend to be legally bound by it.

- Arrangement not commercial: Finally, the arrangement must not be of a commercial nature (ss

137-15(e) and 137-20(e)). Merely contributing to the costs of running the household that they are living in is in the nature of a reimbursement of household expenses and suggests that the arrangement is not of a commercial nature. Rita is not being charged market rent although she contributes to weekly household costs. This does not make the arrangement commercial in nature.

Termination of the arrangement If the granny flat arrangement between Rita, Susan and Robert is terminated so that Rita ceases to have a "right to occupy" Susan and Robert's home, no CGT consequences would arise, and Rita would not make a capital gain or capital loss as a result of the termination of her granny flat interest. This is on the basis that s 137-15 applied when the granny flat arrangement was commenced and s 137-25 applies to exempt the termination of that arrangement. This is the case regardless of whether Rita's initial contribution of $150,000 is returned to her (eg if the arrangement is terminated because of a breakdown in the relationship between the parties).

Can Susan and Robert claim a full main residence exemption when they ultimately sell their home?

Yes. Susan and Robert can claim the main residence exemption in relation to the whole of their property and can disregard the capital gain that would otherwise arise. The ATO would accept that the main house and the granny flat are effectively one dwelling for the purposes of the main residence exemption (Taxation Determination TD 1999/69), having regard to the proximity of the flat and the integration of family activities.

In addition, Susan and Robert lived in the "one dwelling" for their full ownership period (ITAA97 s 118-110).

The fact that the dwelling was subject to a granny flat interest for part of the ownership period is irrelevant to the availability of the main residence exemption for Susan and Robert which arises on the sale of the dwelling. The granny flat interest merely gives Rita a right to occupy the property, not a right to the property itself.

In addition, upon the sale of the house, no CGT event arises when the granny flat interest terminates (s 137-25) — see above.
