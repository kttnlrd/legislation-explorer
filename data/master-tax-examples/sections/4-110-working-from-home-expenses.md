# 4-110 - Working from home expenses

Worked example: Working from home expenses Issue Amelia Swithins is a senior lawyer with a legal firm. Her employer implements flexible working arrangements because of which Amelia works at home 3 days a week, and in the office 2 days a week. Amelia records her working hours by completing daily timesheets. These show that she worked 1,095 hours at home in total for the period from 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025.

Amelia uses a converted spare bedroom as her home office. This room was not used for any private purpose during the period. Based on her house plans, she estimates that the home office accounts for 10% of the total floor area of her home.

She incurred the following expenses during 2024–25:

- electricity — $2,200

- gas — $780 (used for home heating)

- home phone/internet — $600 (estimated 50% work use)

- mobile phone — $600 (estimated 65% work use)

- cleaning products — $160 (10% work-related)

- tea, coffee, milk and toilet paper — $60

- stationery — $120 (100% work use).

  In addition, she was provided with a laptop and printer by her employer.

She did not incur any other expenses on depreciating assets (such as office furniture) during the period as these were already acquired in earlier periods of home working arising from COVID-19 lockdowns and the costs involved were less than $300 per item.

Amelia asks for advice regarding the methods available for calculating her working from home expenses for the 2024–25 year and the total amount that she can claim.

Solution The ATO has released Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2023/1, which outlines the revised fixed rate method for claiming a deduction for working from home expenses for 2022–23 and beyond.

For 2024–25, the choices are:

- 70 cents per hour fixed rate

- actual costs.

The 80 cents per hour "shortcut rate" ceased to be effective on 30 June 2022. The 52 cents per hour fixed rate also cannot be claimed for periods after 30 June 2022.

Included in the revised fixed rate are the following items of expenditure:

- energy expenses (electricity and/or gas) for lighting, heating/cooling and electronic items used while

  working from home

- internet expenses

- mobile and/or home telephone expenses, and

- stationery and computer consumables.

It is not possible to claim an additional separate deduction for any of these expenses if the fixed rate is used.

The fixed rate does not include:

- depreciation of office furniture and furnishings (eg chairs, desks, bookcases)

- depreciation of home office equipment (eg laptops, computers, tablets and mobile phones)

- repairs to any of the above

- costs of cleaning the home office.

  Separate claims can be made for these expenses.

There is no need to have a separate home office or dedicated work area set aside in the home in order to use the 70 cents fixed rate. This is unlike the old 52 cents rate but aligned with the previous 80 cents rate.

Note, that this applies only to the 70 cents fixed rate, not the actual method.

To claim the fixed rate, 3 criteria must be met: 1. The taxpayer must be working from home while carrying out their employment duties or while carrying on their business. The work has to be substantive and directly related to the taxpayer's income-producing activities, so, for example, minimal tasks such as occasionally checking emails or taking telephone calls while at home will not qualify as working from home. 2. The taxpayer must incur additional running expenses because of working from home. It is not necessary to incur every type of running expense (eg heating, phone/mobile, stationery), however, for every cost that is incurred, the substantiation requirements must be met. 3. The taxpayer must keep appropriate substantiation of the claim. In practice, this means that a record showing the total number of hours worked from home during the income year must be kept, which can be in the form of timesheets, rosters, a diary or similar document kept contemporaneously. In addition, the taxpayer must keep a copy of one document, such as an invoice, bill or credit card statement, for each of the additional running expenses which were incurred during the income year. For example, for energy, mobile and/or home telephone and internet expenses, it is necessary to keep one monthly or quarterly bill. If the bill is not in the taxpayer's name, it will be necessary to keep additional evidence showing that the expense was incurred, for example, a joint credit card statement showing payment or a lease agreement showing that the taxpayer shares the property, and, therefore, the expenses, with others.

If evidence is not kept of the total hours worked from home and for each of the running expenses incurred, the revised fixed rate method cannot be used. This is a significant tightening up of the compliance requirements for making a claim. For example, Practice Statement PS LA 2001/6 (the former guidance on this issue) says at para 5: "Taxpayers who use the rate per hour method to claim a deduction for home office running expenses only need to keep a record to show how many hours they work from home. They can do this over the course of the year, or if their work from home hours are regular and constant, by keeping a record for a representative four-week period." Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2023/1 by contrast says at para 58: "For the 2023-24 and later income years, you must keep a record for the entire income year of the number of hours you worked from home during that income year. An estimate for the entire income year or an estimate based on the number of hours you work from home during a particular period and applied to the rest of the income year will not be accepted." Therefore, for the 2024–25 income year, a record of all the hours worked from home must be kept for the entire income year.

Applying the above to Amelia, the 70 cents per hour fixed rate can be claimed as follows: 1,095 hours @ 70 cents per hour = $766 Plus, additional deductions for:

- Cleaning — $16

  Total deductions = $782

Note: She cannot claim a deduction (using either the 70 cents fixed rate or the actual method) for the tea, coffee, milk and toilet paper. These items are private and domestic in nature, even though her employer would previously provide these at work.

In addition, she also cannot claim a deduction for the decline in value of the laptop and printer as the expense was incurred by her employer.

She satisfies the requirement to prove her hours working from home because she maintains daily timesheets for the whole year. However, she should ensure that she keeps at least one bill for each of the categories included in the fixed rate.

Alternatively, she can claim the actual costs incurred. Her deduction is:

- electricity — $220 ($2,200 × 10%)

- gas — $78 ($780 × 10%)

- home phone/internet — $300 ($600 × 50%)

- mobile phone — $390 ($600 × 65%)

- cleaning products — $16 ($160 × 10%)

- stationery — $120

  Total deductions = $1,124

For substantiation, she needs written proof of all the expenses (eg a receipt, invoice or bill). A diary (or other substantiation, such as timesheets) is needed to record working from home hours (for a typical 4-week period). She must also be able to demonstrate that her split between work and private usage is reasonable.

Conclusion From the above, if she meets the substantiation requirements, she would be better to claim the actual costs method as this produces the bigger deduction:

- revised fixed rate method = $782

- actual method = $1,124.
