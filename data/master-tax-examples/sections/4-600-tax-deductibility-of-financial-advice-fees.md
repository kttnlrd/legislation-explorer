# 4-600 - Tax deductibility of financial advice fees

Worked example: Tax deductibility of financial advice fees Issue Alison Soames, 50, works for one of the big banks, dealing with cyber security and online threats to the bank's customers. During the year ended 30 June 2025, she earned $610,000 in salary and bonuses from the bank.

Alison intends to enhance her investment portfolio over the next few years as she intends to retire within the next 10 years. As such, she has spent some time over the last financial year with her financial adviser, Roger Jones, discussing her retirement and her associated investment strategy to take her up to and beyond retirement.

An analysis of the invoices issued by Roger reveals that that she incurred the following expenses during the year ended 30 June 2025:

- $6,000 for professional assistance to discuss her future investment strategy

- $1,500 for ongoing management and retainer fees for her existing share portfolio

- $2,000 as a loan arrangement fee for an investment property which she decided to buy. The loan term

  was 25 years. The loan arrangement fee was charged on 1 July 2024.

- $800 for a cash flow management report which analysed her monthly cash flow over the whole year to

  identify possible savings which could be reinvested

- $1,200 regarding a plan for further investment in her Self-Managed Superannuation Fund

Roger also advised that he earned $6,500 in commission for arranging the property loan, which was added to the loan balance.

He also charged $2,400 in relation to advice regarding the tax implications of her planned future share investments which are expected to provide her with an income after she retires, including advice about her tax liabilities, obligations and entitlements when acquiring, holding and disposing of the investments. Roger is registered with the Tax Practitioners Board as a tax (financial) adviser.

In addition, Roger separately billed her SMSF for $3,000 for on-going administration costs and a further $2,500 for advice regarding an investment strategy for funds already within the SMSF.

Advise Alison whether she will be entitled to claim a tax deduction in respect of any of the expenses and the level of substantiation that is required in order to prove the claim.

Solution Guidance regarding the tax deductibility of financial advice fees, in so much as it relates to individuals who are not in business, has recently been published by the ATO as TD 2024/7. As a result of regulatory reforms to the financial services industry in recent years, this determination was published to replace the very old guidance on this subject, previously published as TD 95/60. However, it does not represent a change in the Commissioner's view on the deductibility of financial advice fees as outlined in TD 95/60.

Fee for professional assistance to discuss her future investment strategy TD 2024/7 states that a fee for financial advice on a proposed investment prior to acquisition of the asset will not be deductible under s 8-1 because it is a capital expense that is associated with putting the income-earning investment in place. Accordingly, no deduction can be claimed for the fee of $6,000 as the expenditure does not have a sufficient connection with earning income from the investments. Instead, the $6,000 was incurred to establish the foundation for long-term financial growth and stability. It related to providing general financial advice or preparing a financial plan and did not directly contribute to the generation of assessable income.

Fees for financial advice on a proposed investment are also not deductible under s 8-1 because the amount is capital or of a capital nature. This is because the expenditure is incidental to the cost of acquiring the income-producing investment.

Ongoing management and retainer fees Generally, fees paid for investment advice related to income producing investments are tax deductible. On the other hand, fees for general financial advice or advice that does not directly contribute to assessable income will not be tax deductible.

Fees incurred on a regular or recurrent basis for an existing or ongoing income-producing investment are deductible under s 8-1.

The ongoing management and retainer fees of $1,500 are deductible as they are for managing and optimising Alison's existing share portfolio. These fees relate to assets that generate income, such as shares in this case.

Loan arrangement fee This fee is classified as a borrowing expense and is associated with securing funds for income-generating investments, being the rental property. A deduction is therefore available for the cost of $2,000.

If total borrowing expenses are more than $100, the deduction is spread over 5 years or the term of the loan, whichever is less, on a daily basis (s 25-25(4)). Alison is therefore entitled to a deduction of $400 ($2,000 × 365 / 1,826) for the current year.

Cash flow management report Fees for advice incurred in relation to an individual's household budgeting are considered to be private or domestic expenditure and will not be deductible under s 8-1 as they do not directly contribute to income generation. A deduction is therefore not available for the $800 cost of the cash flow management report.

Plan for further investment through the SMSF The $1,200 expense for a plan for further investment in Alison's Self-Managed Superannuation Fund will not be deductible as it relates to possible future investment (for example, increased contributions) and not current investment, hence there is not the required link to her current assessable income, which would be required for the deduction to be claimed under s 8-1.

Note that this advice could be deductible if there was a provable link to earning her current assessable income. This differs from the fees billed to her SMSF ($2,500 and $3,000, covered below) as this advice was provided to Alison personally. The fees billed to her SMSF relate to costs incurred directly by the trustees in relation to assets already within the fund and are not deductible to Alison.

Commissions Indirect commissions, such as those paid to financial advisors by product providers or investment companies, do not qualify for tax deductions. Since this commission was a payment made by a third party and not directly incurred by Alison, the individual seeking financial advice, it is not eligible for a tax deduction.

Advice regarding tax implications of her future investments Alison will not be entitled to a deduction under s 8-1 as this advice relates to putting an income-earning investment in place to support her future income. However, fees for financial advice an individual incurs can, alternatively, be deductible under s 25-5 to the extent that the advice relates to managing their tax affairs. "Tax (financial) advice" as defined in s 90-15 of the Tax Agent Services Act 2009 would be included within the meaning of "tax affairs".

In this case, Roger gives Alison advice about her tax liabilities, obligations and entitlements when acquiring, holding and disposing of the investments. This qualifies as tax advice and a deduction under s 25-5 can be claimed.

Note that not all advice provided by a financial adviser will be tax (financial) advice. For example, factual information about a financial product that does not involve the application or interpretation of the tax laws to the client's personal circumstances would not be tax (financial) advice. In addition, a deduction can only be claimed for tax advice where that advice is provided by a recognised tax adviser, suitably registered with the Tax Practitioners Board.

A taxpayer must be able to identify that the payment was for advice to assist them in managing their tax affairs for a deduction to be claimed.

Whilst not relevant in this case, if the financial advice fees are deductible under both ss 8-1 and 25-5, the fees can only be deducted once under the most appropriate provision (where financial advice fees are deductible under both provisions, the most appropriate provision will be the specific deduction in s 25-5).

Fees billed to her SMSF No deduction is available for either the $3,000 for on-going administration costs or the $2,500 for advice regarding an investment strategy as, in both cases, the advice was provided to the SMSF trustees, not to Alison personally. Fees for advice in relation to investments for a superfund (such as an SMSF) are only deductible to the super fund and only if the fund has paid for the advice and if they are an allowable deduction.

Substantiation An itemised invoice (including for example, a fee disclosure statement or an advice fee consent form) from a financial adviser which details the following will be sufficient written evidence to substantiate the deduction:

- the name of the financial adviser

- the amount of the expense

- an explanation of the advice provided

- the date that the expense was incurred, and

- the date that the invoice was produced.

*Refs: s 8-1, s 25-25(4)), s 25-5, s 90-15, s 25-5)*
