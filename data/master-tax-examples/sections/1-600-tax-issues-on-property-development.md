# 1-600 - Tax issues on property development

Worked example: Tax issues on property development Issue In July 2019, the Icarus Unit Trust was formed with a view to developing property in Sydney. There were 4 unitholders, Matthew Boynton, Mark Scott, Luke Treadwell and Harry Clarke. These 4 unitholders had a long track record of property development across New South Wales, for which they formed a different entity (usually a unit trust) for each development project.

The Icarus Unit Trust acquired a block of land in a desirable location in Sydney's inner eastern suburbs, which was previously a disused industrial space. Their plan was to demolish the existing industrial building, clean up the site and then develop and build a new apartment block, which they expected to contain 20 luxury apartments. They would then sell these apartments at a profit.

Unfortunately, they were hit by the COVID-19 economic slowdown and increased interest rates, which caused a pause in their development activity in March 2020. They did not resume development until they could obtain appropriate finance, which was in December 2023. The property was not rented out in the interim but was left vacant. By May 2025, the first apartments were available to be marketed, although it is expected that the whole project might take a further 2 years to complete.

They believe that their profits from the development will be subject to capital gains tax (CGT), partly because they have now owned the site for 6 years, during which its unimproved value has increased by more than 50%.

Explain the differing tax consequences of property transactions to the 4 unitholders, explaining why this particular transaction may not be subject to CGT, and explaining the differing tax treatments depending on whether the transaction is part of a business of property development or an isolated profit-making undertaking.

Solution There are 3 ways in which profits from land transactions may be taxed: 1. on capital account (CGT) as the mere realisation of an asset 2. on revenue account (income tax) where the development constitutes the carrying on of a business of property development 3. on revenue account (income tax) where the development goes beyond the mere realisation of the land but is less than the carrying on of a business. This is subject to income tax as a profit-making undertaking arising from an isolated transaction.

Essentially, the difference between capital treatment and revenue treatment boils down to whether the property has been acquired as an investment or for long-term use in its current form (which suggests capital treatment) or if, instead, has been acquired with a view of making a gain on resale or in the course of carrying on a business (both of which suggest revenue treatment).

Property investors acquire land as an investment with the intention of using it to earn assessable income (business income or rent). Some development may occur with a view of increasing the capital value of the land as it is retained for the long term.

Hallmarks of property investment include:

- the property is acquired for investment and not for resale

- there is a lengthy period of ownership.

Where the sale is on capital account, there is potential for the 50% general CGT discount to apply. In addition, other concessions may be available depending on the precise circumstances of the taxpayer (eg the small business CGT concessions, main residence exemption, etc).

The 4 unitholders believe that they qualify for capital treatment on the basis that they have held the land for an extended period (6 years, and possibly 8 years before the completion of the project). However, this is not determinative; the fact that the property was acquired for development and resale and that intention has never changed, despite the delays, undermines any argument that the sale will be on capital account.

As noted above, this treatment will not apply where the land is acquired for resale at a profit or for development. The typical scenarios where capital treatment as a "mere realisation" applies include:

- a taxpayer selling a main residence or long-term investment property

- a taxpayer selling an office building or factory used in their business

- a farmer subdividing and selling off farming land, provided they do just enough preparation of the

  property to realise the best price possible — and no more.

By contrast, property developers are in the business of acquiring, subdividing and selling land or acquiring and renovating property with a view to profit. They will be subject to income tax on their profits. Key identifying factors include where:

- the land is acquired with the purpose of making a gain on resale, or in the course of carrying on a

  business

- the taxpayer(s) has/have a history of developing land or acquiring and renovating property for profit

- extensive work is undertaken on the land, including installation of drains, construction of parks, lakes,

  footpaths, roads, etc

- there is a high degree of personal involvement in the development

- the transaction is carried out in a business-like manner (there are business records, an office,

  branding, etc).

Where the sale is not a mere realisation but a profit-making undertaking (either as part of a business or an isolated profit-making undertaking), it can be expected that many of the following factors will be present (applying the principles from Miscellaneous Taxation Ruling MT 2006/1 and Taxation Ruling TR 97/11):

- there is a change of purpose for which the land is held

- additional land is acquired to be added to the original parcel of land

- the parcel of land is brought into account as a business asset

- there is a coherent plan for the subdivision of the land

- there is a business organisation — for example a manager, office and letterhead

- borrowed funds financed the acquisition or subdivision

- interest on money borrowed to defray subdivisional costs was claimed as a business expense

- there is a level of development of the land beyond that necessary to secure council approval for the

  subdivision

- buildings have been erected on the land.

Where some or all of the factors above are present, the transaction is unlikely to be on capital account and will instead be subject to income tax. However, even here, the tax treatment will differ depending on whether there is a business of property development or an isolated profit-making transaction.

The difference is likely to come down to the regularity of the transaction/s: individuals that routinely buy and sell property for profit will be engaged in a business whereas an entity which simply undertakes one transaction, with no intention of repeating it, is probably engaged in an isolated profit-making transaction.

In this case, the 4 unitholders are experienced developers, with a long track record of undertaking similar development, albeit through different entities, and hence more likely than not to be carrying out a property development business.

With property businesses, the property can either be acquired as trading stock or, if it was already held by the owners prior to commencement of the property development business, it will cease to be a capital asset and will become trading stock at the date the business commences.

In the latter situation, there is a deemed sale of the property at market value (which gives rise to CGT event K4) and the property is deemed to have been immediately reacquired as trading stock at the same value by the business (s 70-30 ITAA97).

The taxpayer can elect to use cost instead of market value. This election must be made by the time the tax return for the period in which the taxpayer starts holding the property as trading stock is lodged (meaning the taxpayer needs to know that the change has occurred and the date on which it occurred).

The upside of using market value is that the various CGT concessions (eg pre-CGT asset, 50% discount, main residence exemption or small business concessions) are locked in. However, the downside is that if there are no concessions that apply, a CGT liability arises well before the ultimate sale of the property.

In this case, the property was acquired with a view to profit and therefore has always been trading stock. No capital gain will arise under CGT event K4. This means that all of the profits from the development will be on revenue account.

Section 70-5 ITAA97 sets out the rules for trading stock and is intended to "… produce an overall result that … properly reflects … activities with … trading stock during the income year …". It provides that:

- acquisition and ancillary costs (eg development costs) of purchased trading stock are an allowable

  deduction under s 8-1 in the year the trading stock is held for sale in the course of a business (ss 8-1

  and 70-15)

- the gross consideration received upon disposal of trading stock is assessable as ordinary income (ss

70-80 and 6-5) when it is derived (which will be at the date of settlement — Gasparin v FC of T 94 ATC 4280; [1994] FCA 1057), and

- the excess of the closing value (s 70-45) of trading stock on hand at the end of the income year over

  the opening value (s 70-40) at the start of the income year is assessable (s 70-35) and conversely,

  where the opening value of trading stock is greater than the closing value, the excess is allowable as a

  deduction (s 70-35).

So expenses are generally deductible in the year in which they are incurred, with some exceptions.

Immediately deductible costs would include:

- administration expenses

- interest on construction loans

- rates and other taxes.

Certain development costs are included in the cost of trading stock (meaning that a deduction is effectively deferred until sale) such as:

- cost of materials

- direct labour costs

- production overhead costs (such as depreciation)

- infrastructure costs (eg costs to establish services on the land and build roads).

With an isolated profit-making undertaking, the land is more than "merely" realised, but it is not held as an item of trading stock.

Paragraph 6 of Taxation Ruling TR 92/3 says that the resulting profits would be assessed as ordinary income under s 6-5 if:

- the taxpayer entered into the transaction with a profit-making purpose

- a profit was made in the course of carrying on a business or in carrying out a business operation or

  commercial transaction.

The development profit will be treated as ordinary income on settlement under s 6-5. Development expenses are not deductible as incurred; they are offset against proceeds from sale with the net profit assessable at settlement. Holding costs (rates, taxes, interest, etc) are deductible as incurred, subject to the restrictions on claiming holding costs on vacant land.

The key differences between being treated as carrying on a business and an isolated profit-making undertaking include:

- the unrealised gain is treated as capital as at the contract date

- no election is required at the date the profit-making undertaking commences

- the profit from the venture is not taxed until realisation (settlement).

  There is a 3-step process for calculating the tax liability:

Step One Calculate the net assessable profit from the development in the year in which the contract is settled, being the difference between the gross proceeds less the development costs, including the cost of the land.

Step Two Calculate the capital gain as at the contract date. This represents the growth in the value of the property from the date of acquisition.

Step Three Reduce the capital gain calculated at Step Two by the assessable net profit calculated at Step One.

The amount taxable is the total of assessable income from Step One and the net capital gain (if any) from Step Three.

The example given in the question is indicative of being a property development business and should be taxed accordingly. However, the above illustrates the difference between the tax treatment of a transaction that relates to a property development business and a profit-making undertaking.
