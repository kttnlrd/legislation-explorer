# 9-320 - Section 99B Receiving payments or assets from a foreign trust

Worked example: Section 99B: Receiving payments or assets from a foreign trust Issue Amanda, a resident of Australia, received a cash legacy of $500,000 from the foreign estate of a non- resident deceased relative on 1 June 2025. This amount was paid to Amanda, who was a beneficiary of the will, from the estate by the executor, who is an independent third party. The relative died on 30 September 2024.

The $500,000 represents the proceeds from the sale of an investment property, which was originally bought for $300,000 and sold on 1 January 2025. There was no foreign tax payable on the disposal.

Amanda advises you that the only supporting documentation she can obtain with regard to the payment is a copy of her emails and letters with the independent trustee, as well as a letter from the trustee confirming the amount of the bequest and the date of death of the deceased, and a copy of the deceased estate's financial accounts confirming that the amount consists of $300,000 trust capital and $200,000 gains on the realisation of assets.

What are the tax implications for Amanda?

Solution Section 99B ITAA36 applies to any amount, being property of a trust estate, which is paid to (or applied for the benefit of) a beneficiary who is an Australian resident at any time during the income year. These amounts are included in the resident beneficiary's assessable income (s 99B(1)). This can potentially include:

- distributions paid by the trustee to a beneficiary

- assets transferred by the trustee to a beneficiary

- use of trust property by a beneficiary

- loans from the trustee to a beneficiary

- amounts received from a deceased estate.

However, s 99B will not apply where one of the following carve-outs under s 99B(2) applies:

- an amount represents corpus of the trust, except to the extent to which it is attributable to amounts

  derived by the trust estate which, if they had been derived by "a taxpayer being a resident", would have

  been included in the assessable income of that taxpayer (s 99B(2)(a))

- an amount that, if it had been derived by "a taxpayer being a resident", would not have been included

  in the assessable income of that taxpayer (s 99B(2)(b))

- an amount included in the assessable income of the beneficiary under s 97

- an amount in respect of which a trustee is assessed and liable to pay tax under s 98, 99 or 99A

- an amount that is non-assessable non-exempt income of the beneficiary under s 802-17 ITAA97

  (conduit foreign income), or

- an amount included in the assessable income of a taxpayer under s 102AAZD (transferor trust rules).

Therefore, s 99B is very much a provision of last resort which will catch all transactions not already caught (and taxed) by s 97, 98, 99 or 99A.

Once the elements of s 99B(1) are established, the taxpayer has the onus of establishing that one of the carve-outs in s 99B(2) applies in order to reduce (or eliminate) the amount included in assessable income under s 99B(1).

The Australian beneficiary may also be liable to pay an interest charge on the amount assessable under s 99B(1), back to the time when the trustee derived the income representing the amount.

Assuming that the amount is not excluded because it has already been caught under one of the other provisions listed, the taxpayer then has the onus of proving that one of the first 2 exemptions applies.

Specifically, where the payment or benefit comes from a trust, Taxation Determination TD 2024/9 provides guidance on what "corpus" refers to. At para 19 it states that: "Corpus, in the context in which it is used in section 99B and Division 6 more broadly refers to trust capital which is represented by the assets of the trust, excluding income which has not been accumulated. In determining whether an amount distributed represents corpus, for the purposes of paragraph 99B(2)(a), regard is had to the trust property distributed. The accounting records of the trust may assist in evidencing this but are not determinative of what the amount represents." If s 99B applies, the starting point is that the $500,000 is taken to be a capital payment in the hands of Amanda and is included in her assessable income. However, TD 2024/9 notes that concessions such as the CGT discount, the small business concessions and the main residence exemption will not apply. Therefore, Amanda stands to be taxed at 45% (excluding the Medicare levy) on the $500,000 received if one of the carve-outs in s 99B(2) does not apply.

In this case, the first carve-out is relevant. Of the $500,000 potentially included in assessable income, all of it is derived from the corpus of the trust estate. However, only $200,000 of it would have been included in the assessable income of a "hypothetical resident taxpayer" (being the profit from sale of the investment property). The other $300,000 is therefore exempt from tax under s 99B(2). Therefore, Amanda is required to include $200,000 in her tax return for the 2024–25 year.

In addition, she is required to pay interest on the $200,000 back to the time when the trustee first derived the amount, being the date of sale of the property.

However, there is potentially a sting in the tail. Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2024/3 says that the taxpayer is required to provide evidence to substantiate that s 99B(2)(a) or (b) applies to reduce the amount included in assessable income under s 99B(1). In other words, the burden of proof lies with the taxpayer to prove that either of the carve-outs is applicable to her situation. If she cannot satisfy the burden of proof, the entire $500,000 will be included in assessable income under s 99B(1).

PCG 2024/3 goes on to list the core documents and information that the resident beneficiary should obtain to discharge the burden of proof in respect of satisfying s 99B(2)(a). These are:

- a copy of the signed and executed trust deed or will

- signed trustee minutes, resolutions or distribution statements confirming an amount was paid or

  applied for the benefit of a beneficiary from the trust's corpus

- copies of the trust's financial accounts for the relevant years, prepared in accordance with the

  accounting principles of the relevant country.

Note that, in the context of s 99B, the "relevant years" will be far in excess of the year of payment of the trust property which gives rise to the application of s 99B(1).

The Guideline then goes on to state that the ATO will require further documentation and information on a case-by-case basis, including, but not limited to:

- records detailing the property used to settle the trust, such as payment records or documents

  demonstrating the transfer of property

- for a deceased estate, a document setting out the assets owned by the deceased at their date of

  death, or a valuation of their assets at the date of death

- documents showing property being contributed to the trust

- other records or working papers prepared by the trustee or their professional advisers; for example,

  accounting working papers

- bank statements or payment records

- copies of all trustee minutes, resolutions or distribution statements confirming the payment of capital

  amounts

- accounting records; for example, general ledgers

- correspondence from the executors or their legal advisers setting out the terms of the will

- advice from professional advisers, including foreign advisers, to support the evidence provided

- foreign legal advice

- tax distribution statements

- foreign country tax returns of the beneficiary where the beneficiary is required to lodge in the foreign

  jurisdiction

- foreign resident withholding tax statements from the foreign jurisdiction.

Therefore, there is a risk that Amanda may be unable to meet the substantiation requirements set out above as the proof available to her is entirely focused on the payment of $500,000 to her and the prior disposal of the property. She cannot necessarily provide the level of proof required as to the original cost base of the property.

However, the Guideline does state that the ATO will not devote compliance resources where the arrangement is considered "low risk", including situations involving distributions from a non-resident deceased estate to a resident beneficiary where the trust property is distributed to the resident beneficiary within 24 months of the date of death and the total value of trust property received does not exceed A$2 million. Even here, there are substantiation requirements which must be met before the ATO will regard the arrangement as low risk, namely the taxpayer must be able to provide:

- a document confirming the date of the deceased's death

- the will of the deceased, letter of wishes or correspondence from executors or their legal advisers

  stating the terms of the will

- documentation confirming that the assets were owned or held by the deceased at the time of death,

  and

- documentation setting out the distribution to the Australian-resident beneficiary and the assets of the

  deceased used to fund this distribution.

Amanda must disclose an amount of $200,000 (the profit on the sale of the property) under s 99B(1).

As Amanda can produce all of the above documentation, under PCG 2024/3, the ATO will not devote compliance resources to the other $300,000 of her bequest and is likely to accept her explanation that it was derived from the corpus of the trust.
