# 4-450 - Gifts and donations

Worked example: Gifts and donations Issue Albert Dondas made the following charitable donations and contributions during the year ended 30 June 2025:

- a donation to the Australian Diabetes Society of $250, for which he has a receipt

- a donation to the Australian Labor Party of $150, for which he has a receipt

- a raffle ticket bought at a charitable dinner held on behalf of the Cancer Council of Australia costing

  $50

- purchase of a ticket to attend the charitable dinner above, which was $125

- several un-receipted donations to "bucket appeals" of less than $10, and

- a donation of a painting that Albert had acquired several years earlier to the National Gallery of

  Victoria, which was valued at the date of donation at $10,000.

Albert is completing his tax return for the year ended 30 June 2025 and seeks your advice on the tax deductibility of the above amounts. Albert's assessable income for the year ended 30 June 2025 (ignoring any deduction for the above amounts) was $75,000.

Advise Albert.

Solution ITAA97 Div 30 permits deductions for certain gifts and contributions.

A deduction for charitable gifts or donations can only be claimed if the recipient organisation is a deductible gift recipient (DGR). Whether a charitable organisation is a DGR can be determined by looking at the Australian Business Register ABN Lookup page (Deductible gift recipients | ABN Lookup (business.gov.au)).

Most major charities are DGRs, including the Australian Diabetes Society.

When a "gift" is made, the taxpayer does not receive a material benefit in return. This is contrasted with a contribution (eg purchasing a ticket to attend a fundraising dinner) where a benefit is received in return.

To claim a tax deduction for a gift, it must meet these conditions (ITAA97 ss 30-1 to 30-320):

- the gift must be made to a DGR

- the gift must truly be a gift, that is, a voluntary transfer of money or property where the donor receives

  nothing in return, and

- the gift must be money or property, which includes financial assets such as shares.

For gifts of money, a deduction can be claimed where the amount is $2 or more and is claimable in the income year in which the gift is made.

The receipt that the charitable organisation provides, which will be needed to substantiate the deduction (ITAA97 s 30-228), should advise whether a deduction is claimable. If a DGR issues a receipt, its name, ABN, and the fact that the contribution was a gift or related to certain fundraising events must be specified.

Accordingly, Albert can claim a deduction for the donation to the Australian Diabetes Society.

For donations over $2 made through the internet or by phone, web receipts or credit card statements can be used to substantiate the deduction. If the donation was made through third parties, such as banks and retail outlets, the receipt that the third party provided is also sufficient. If the gift was made through "workplace- giving", the payment summary should show the amount donated (Law Administration Practice Statement PS LA 2002/15).

For "bucket collections" (eg for bushfire or flood victims) of $2 or more, a donation can be claimed as a tax deduction if the contribution was made without a receipt, provided the contribution does not exceed $10. As such, Albert will be able to claim a deduction for his donations to the "bucket appeals" as the amount of each donation was less than $10.

It is not possible to claim as a gift or donation anything that carries with it a personal benefit, such as:

- raffle tickets

- items such as chocolates and pens

- the cost of attending fundraising dinners, even if the cost exceeds the value of the dinner (but see

  below), and

- payments to school building funds made, for example, as an alternative to an increase in school fees.

As such, a tax deduction will not be available for the cost of the raffle ticket bought by Albert at the charitable dinner.

It may still be possible to claim a tax deduction for the cost of attending a fundraising event even though the payment made is not regarded as a gift for tax purposes. A portion of the amount contributed to the event is claimable as a tax deduction if the contribution is for an eligible fundraising event organised for a DGR and conducted in Australia, including fetes, balls, gala shows, dinners, performances and similar events.

A contribution of money (such as buying a ticket) is only deductible if the amount spent is over $150.

Therefore, Albert (who spent $125 on the ticket) cannot claim a deduction for the charitable dinner.

Political parties are not DGRs. However, in some circumstances, gifts and contributions made by individuals to political parties and independent candidates and members can be claimed as an income tax deduction (ITAA97 s 30-242).

To claim a deduction, contributions must be more than $2. The most that is claimable is:

- $1,500 for contributions and gifts to political parties, and

- $1,500 for contributions and gifts to independent candidates and members.

  Businesses cannot claim deductions for political contributions.

As Albert is an individual, he is able to claim a tax deduction for his contribution to the Australian Labor Party ($150).

In relation to the gift of a painting to the National Gallery of Victoria, ITAA97 s 30-15(1) Table 4(e) states that, where property is transferred to an institution in Australia that is a public museum or a public gallery, the general rule is that the amount of the deduction is the average of the GST inclusive market values specified in written valuations obtained from approved valuers for the property. The following special conditions also apply.

- The property must be accepted by the recipient for inclusion in a collection it is maintaining or

  establishing.

- The value of the gift must be $2 or more.

- The valuation requirements in ITAA97 s 30-200 must be satisfied. These state that:

  – the taxpayer must get 2 or more written valuations of the gift

– the valuations must be made by different individuals who are approved valuers of the type of gift to be donated – each valuation must state the GST inclusive market value of the gift on either the day the donation was made or the day the valuation was made, and – if the valuation states the GST inclusive market value of the gift on the day the valuation was made, it must have been made within 90 days before or after the donation was made.

Therefore, Albert can claim a deduction for the painting provided he obtains 2 written valuations from approved valuers as outlined above.

While not relevant in this case, it is worth noting that a deduction for a gift or donation cannot add to or create a tax loss. However, a donor taxpayer can choose to spread the tax deduction for a donation over a period of up 5 financial years, by using the "Election to spread gift deduction" form (NAT 73763).

*Refs: s 30-1, ITAA97 s 30-228), ITAA97 s 30-242), ITAA97 s 30-15(1), ITAA97 s 30-200*
