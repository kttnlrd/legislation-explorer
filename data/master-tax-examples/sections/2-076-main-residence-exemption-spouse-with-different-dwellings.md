# 2-076 - Main residence exemption spouse with different dwellings

Worked example: Main residence exemption; spouse with different dwellings Issue In late 2009, Rebecca Charlesworth acquired a dwelling in Mount Martha, on the Mornington Peninsula.

This property has always been used for private and domestic purposes, with Rebecca moving in as soon as practicable and establishing it as her main residence.

In late 2023, Rebecca got married to Royston Vasey, who at the time had his own property in Diamond Creek, in the north-eastern suburbs of Melbourne, which he had established as his main residence. This property was acquired in 2014. However, after their marriage, he moved into the house in Mount Martha and rented out the Diamond Creek property for a year, until its disposal in late 2024.

Despite living in the Mount Martha property, Royston has chosen to make the Diamond Creek property his main residence during the period from their marriage until the disposal. Rebecca also wishes to make the Diamond Creek property her main residence from the date of their marriage in late 2023.

In late 2024, they disposed of both properties and bought a single main residence in Brighton.

Can Rebecca treat the Diamond Creek dwelling as her main residence without ever residing there? If so, will the main residence exemption apply in relation to the proposed sale of Rebecca's property in Mount Martha?

What alternative ways are there to structure the main residence exemption on the 2 properties?

Solution In situations where each member of a couple has their own separate main residence during a particular period, s 118-170 of ITAA97 contains special rules which ensure that, during a period in which members of a couple are "spouses" of each other, the main residence exemption cannot fully apply in respect of both dwellings.

This means, where a taxpayer and their spouse each have their own separate main residence during a particular period, they must either:

- choose one of the dwellings as the main residence of both parties, or

- nominate different dwellings as the main residence of each person for the relevant period.

The definition of a spouse is specified in s 995-1(1) ITAA97 to cover persons who are legally married, and also de facto couples, ie a person who lives with another on a genuine domestic basis as that person's husband or wife (including same-sex couples). However, s 118-170 specifically excludes spouses living permanently separately and apart.

If the taxpayer and their spouse nominate one dwelling to be the main residence of both of them during the relevant period, the main residence exemption applies to that one dwelling only. Any other dwelling is not exempt.

A taxpayer may nominate their spouse's dwelling as their main residence, even where:

- the taxpayer does not have an interest in that dwelling (Taxation Determination TD 92/173, which

  despite being withdrawn is still applicable), and/or

- the taxpayer does not occupy the dwelling, but the dwelling is still treated as the spouse's main

  residence under the temporary absence rule in s 118-145 (Taxation Determination TD 92/174).

If the taxpayer and their spouse nominate different dwellings as their main residence for the relevant period, the main residence exemption will be applied to each dwelling as follows:

- If the taxpayer owns 50% or less of the dwelling they chose, a full exemption applies to the taxpayer's

  share for the relevant period.

- If the taxpayer owns more than 50%, their share is only exempt for half the relevant period.

- If the spouse owns 50% or less of the dwelling they chose, a full exemption applies to the spouse's

  share of that dwelling for the relevant period.

- If the spouse owns more than 50%, their share in that dwelling is only exempt for half the relevant

  period.

In this case, the property at Mount Martha was not Rebecca's main residence for her entire ownership period. She established the Mount Martha property as her main residence from the date of acquisition and it continued to be her main residence until the date in late 2023 when she and Royston got married and she nominated the Diamond Creek property as her main residence.

Therefore, as the Mount Martha property has not been her main residence for the entire ownership period, she is entitled to a partial main residence exemption by applying the following formula to calculate her capital gain: Amount of capital gain x (number of days in the ownership period when the dwelling was not her main residence / total number of days in the ownership period) The number of days in her ownership period when the Mount Martha dwelling was not her main residence will be from the date in late 2023 when she got married until the settlement date for the sale of the property, in late 2024. The total number of days in the ownership period is from settlement date to settlement date.

The resulting capital gain can be discounted by 50%.

She is able to claim the main residence exemption on the Diamond Creek dwelling even though she never lived there (TD 92/173), for the period from late 2023 until late 2024. Royston is also entitled to the main residence exemption on this property for the entire ownership period. The period of absence after their marriage is covered by the absence rule (s 118-145) and the base cost of the property is its market value in late 2023 (when it was first rented out — s 118-192(2)).

If Rebecca and Royston choose instead to treat the Mount Martha dwelling as their main residence, the main residence exemption would apply to this dwelling for the entire period of ownership of the property. Royston's property would not be treated as his main residence for the period that they owned both houses.

Alternatively, Rebecca can choose to treat the Mount Martha property as her main residence and Royston can choose to treat his property as his main residence. In this case, she would be entitled to a 100% main residence exemption for the period up until the date they were married and a 50% main residence exemption after they were married until she sold the property. As she owns less than 50% of the Diamond Creek property, she would not be liable for any capital gain on the sale of this property. As Royston owns more than 50% of this property, only a 50% main residence exemption would apply during this same period on the future sale of the Diamond Creek property.

The method they choose will depend on the respective capital gains on both properties. Ideally, they should structure their affairs such that they are using both of their half main residence exemptions to cover the property with the largest capital gain.

The choice must be made by the day they lodge their income tax returns for the income year in which they sold the properties (or within a further time as allowed by the Commissioner).

*Refs: s 118-170, s 995-1(1), s 118-145, s 118-145), s 118-192(2))*
