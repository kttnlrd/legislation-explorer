# 1-500 - Image rights

Worked example: Image rights Issue Cleveland Jones, a top-flight player in the AFL, has been playing at this level since 2015. Cleveland has acquired considerable fame, both on the field and off it, where he is in constant demand for appearances on chat shows, reality shows and to appear at public events.

In January 2025, he formed the Cleveland Jones Family Trust, the beneficiaries of which include his wife, their 3 children, his parents and his brother and sisters. The corporate trustee of the trust entered into a deed with Cleveland which grants the trust a right to use and exploit the fame of Cleveland throughout Australia.

The trustee contracts with Vigorous Shoes, a sports shoe manufacturing and marketing company, for the use of Cleveland's photo and name on a promotional campaign which will run nationwide for several months, in exchange for a fee. Payment by Vigorous Shoes is made to the trustee.

Explain the tax consequences of this arrangement.

Solution In July 2017, the ATO released draft practical compliance guideline PCG 2017/D11 (now withdrawn). The draft guideline dealt with the tax treatment of payments for the use and exploitation of a famous person's "public fame" or "image", including the rights to the use of their name, image, likeness, identity, reputation, etc.

The guideline proposed a "Safe Harbour" for apportioning lump sum payments for both the provision of a sportsperson's services and the use and exploitation of a professional sportsperson's "public fame" or "image" under a licence. The Safe Harbour meant that up to 10% of the payment received would be referable to the "image rights", as the marketing of the "public fame" or "image" of a famous person is known.

These rules allowed, for example, professional sportspersons to — at least partially — separate income earned from their fame, such as payments for image rights, from their earnings on the pitch. Athletes and others could establish separate companies or family trusts to serve as the beneficiary of those image rights payments.

This draft guidance was withdrawn by the ATO in August 2018, after an announcement in the 2018 Federal Budget proposed that famous individuals could no longer license their fame or image to another entity.

The issue of image rights was revisited by the ATO in Taxation Determination TD 2023/4, which contains guidance for the use of an individual's fame for income tax purposes. These rules could significantly affect how influencers, entertainers and sportspeople profit from their fame.

Case law states that an individual with fame has no property in that fame and, therefore, cannot vest or transfer that fame to another entity. As a result, the determination states that a related entity to the individual with fame is not in a position to exploit the image of that individual and financially benefit. This is because the image rests with the individual and not any related entity. Therefore, a related entity cannot derive income that is attributable to the use of an individual's fame.

The determination goes on to state that the income derived from the sub-licencing of those rights to a related entity remains the ordinary income of the individual, with the related entity merely receiving an amount that is being applied or dealt with on the individual's behalf.

Given the change in view, the Commissioner states that if individuals have already entered into an arrangement in good faith on the basis of PCG 2017/D11, the ATO will not devote compliance resources to apply the position in TD 2023/4 for the income years 2018–19 to 2022–23. It will, however, apply TD 2023/4 to income derived in 2023–24 and later income years.

Accordingly, the income arising from the use of Cleveland's fame is Cleveland's ordinary income. This is because, while the trustee has a right to use that fame, no property is transferred to the trustee which could allow a third party to use it for a fee. Therefore, Cleveland should include any fees received by the trustee in his assessable income in the relevant income year.

Despite TD 2023/4, there is still no change for an individual who wishes to use a related entity to provide services. The Commissioner indicates that it is still possible for individuals with fame to contract personal services through a related entity. An example of this is where the individual is engaged by a related entity to provide services, such as attending a promotional event or product launch for a third party. In that case, the related entity would be assessable on the income received by the third party. However, this could be subject to the personal services income rules in ITAA97 Pt 2-42. The anti-avoidance rules in ITAA36 Pt IVA could also be used to challenge such an arrangement.
