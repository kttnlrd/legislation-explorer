# 11-355 - Employee v contractor distinction

Employee v contractor distinction Issue Chase Construction Pty Ltd is a small construction company that employs a team of specialist tradies, such as plumbers, electricians, etc, to work on its projects. Chase has recently engaged a new finance director, Geoff Love, who believes that the company can cut costs and "red tape" by using contractors instead of in- house employees.

As part of the plan, all of Chase's employees will be made redundant and will then be rehired as independent contractors on broadly the same terms. The finance director believes that this will eliminate the requirement for PAYG(W) to be withheld on their wages, effectively passing the burden of this obligation to the contractors. The former employees are to be advised that they need to obtain their own ABN. However, they will continue to use the tools and equipment provided by Chase, which will retain the ultimate responsibility for their work and will stipulate the hours that they must work. They will not be offered the chance to delegate their work to anyone else.

Advise Geoff on the suitability of his plan and how it can be improved to enable him to achieve his goals for Chase.

Solution Generally, employees work in and are part of the business. Contractors, by contrast, are running their own business. It is important that Chase understands the difference between the 2 as getting it wrong could lead to Chase incorrectly categorising its workers as contractors when they are — in substance — employees. This can lead to underpaid PAYG(W) (which will fall on the employer to settle) and possible penalties. It is also a decision that can reach beyond tax and superannuation, impacting on a variety of entitlements, including leave, workers compensation and compensation for unfair dismissal. It can also affect the employer's liability to state-based payroll tax.

Traditionally, the courts have looked to the "multifactorial" approach to determine the distinction between employee and contractor. This consisted of a wide-ranging review of all aspects of the work arrangement — whether they were reflected in the contract or not. Relevant factors before, during and after the time the contract was entered into needed to be taken into account, including mode of remuneration (eg hourly), the right of a person to delegate work to others and representation as part of the payer's business (eg wearing a uniform with the business logo).

While this is still essential, a series of recent court cases such as the Personnel Contracting case (Construction, Forestry, Maritime, Mining and Energy Union v Personnel Contracting Pty Ltd [2022] HCA 1) and Jamsek case (ZG Operations Australia Pty Ltd v Jamsek [2022] HCA 2) now emphasise that the contract between the business and its workers is the most important element.

The Personnel Contracting case dealt with a labour hire company that engaged workers to supply labour to building industry clients. In 2016, Personnel Contracting engaged the services of Mr McCourt and offered him work at a building site operated by Hanssen Pty Ltd, one of Personnel Contracting's major clients.

Mr McCourt commenced basic labouring work on site with Hanssen, without signing a contract but under its direct supervision. He subsequently ceased working with Personnel Contracting and Hanssen on 30 June 2017.

Mr McCourt and the Construction, Forestry, Maritime, Mining and Energy Union (CFMMEU) commenced proceedings against Personnel Contracting for compensation and penalties under the Fair Work Act (FWA), claiming that he was a "common law employee" of Personnel Contracting, who had not paid him in accordance with his industry entitlements.

Ultimately, the case ended up before the High Court, which said that the Court's role was to characterise the relationship by examining the totality of the relationship having regard to the parties' rights and obligations contained in the written contract.

The Court said that where the parties had comprehensively committed the terms of the relationship to a written contract (and neither party disputed the validity of that contract), the characterisation of their relationship must be based on that contract rather than the subsequent conduct of the parties.

In short, the High Court rejected the traditional multifactorial approach, saying that an examination of all the dealings between the parties over the entire history of their relationship was unnecessary and inappropriate.

However, it did note that examination of post-contractual conduct is permissible in certain circumstances.

This might be where the contract was not in writing, or only partly in writing, or where the terms of the written contract were being challenged as invalid (such as sham) or varied.

The High Court concluded a crucial factor was the extent and degree of control exercised by the "employer".

When considering both the degree and nature of that control (and whether the worker was performing work in the business of the employer), the following contractual aspects needed to be considered:

- the way the worker was paid

- the provision and maintenance of equipment to do the job

- the obligation to work and the hours of work

- the provision for holidays

- the deduction of income tax

- the ability to subcontract the work.

The Commissioner was not a party to this case, which concerned entitlements under the FWA. Nevertheless, the ATO later released Taxation Ruling TR 2023/4 to provide guidance on the question of who is an employee for PAYG purposes. TR 2023/4 is intended to replace TR 2005/15, its former and now outdated review of the subject.

The ATO's guidance reflects the High Court decisions in both the Personnel case and the Jamsek case (the latter was another recent case decided along similar lines).

TR 2023/4 confirms that "where the worker and the engaging entity have comprehensively committed the terms of their relationship to a written contract and the validity of that contract has not been challenged as a sham nor have the terms of the contract otherwise been varied, waived, discharged or the subject of an estoppel or any equitable, legal or statutory right or remedy, it is the legal rights and obligations in the contract alone that are relevant in determining whether the worker is an employee of an engaging entity.

Evidence of how the contract was performed, including subsequent conduct and work practices, cannot be considered for the purpose of determining the nature of the legal relationship between the parties." Therefore, before progressing, Chase Constructing should ensure that legally effective contracts are drawn up for the services of the individuals who are to be contractors. Particular emphasis should be placed on the following: 1. The terms of the contract (and the existence of the contract itself) are critical: Chase should clearly record the terms and conditions of engagement in a comprehensive written contract that specifies that the individual is an independent contractor. It should include terms appropriate for a contracting relationship (see below) since, according to case law as it now stands, it no longer necessary to consider how the parties interact after the contract is signed. 2. Mere labels are not determinative: Merely attaching a label to the relationship (such as calling someone an "independent contractor" or shifting the tax and insurance obligations to the individual) will not be sufficient if the contract has terms that suggest an employment relationship. 3. If the contract is not comprehensive, the "totality of the relationship" becomes the deciding factor: The way that the parties interact with one other will determine the relationship if the contract is less than comprehensive, is ineffective or is a sham. This could create uncertainty, with the potential for disputes, liability for employee entitlements and prosecution.

At the moment, Chase's preferred future engagement with the workers (including the provision of its own tools and equipment, hours of work, terms of control and delegation) fall far short of the preferred standard and would not satisfy the ATO that the employment relationship has been severed.

Chase Construction should ensure that the contract minimises the level of control and direction that it has over the provision of services. This could be done by, for example, including terms that:

- allow the worker discretion as to how services are provided, for example, ensure that there is no

  control over the hours of work that the person is obliged to work

- require the worker to purchase the specific tools and equipment required to do the job

- engage the worker to produce a result

- include a right of delegation whereby the worker can delegate the task to someone else, and

- impose the commercial risk and responsibility for poor workmanship and injury sustained in the

  performance of their work on the worker, including a requirement that the worker has their own

  insurance and indemnity policies to cover risk.
