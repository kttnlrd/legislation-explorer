# Changes to planned use ¶6-300

A taxpayer can only claim an input tax credit (ITC) for something they acquire in carrying on their enterprise, and are not normally entitled to claim a credit if the purchase relates to the sale of input taxed goods and services, or is for private or domestic purposes (¶5-010). As the entitlement to an ITC generally arises when consideration is paid or a tax invoice is received (s 29-10; ¶5-010) it is not uncommon for the entitlement to a credit to arise prior to the acquisition being used. Accordingly, it might transpire that the actual use differs from that intended giving rise to the need to adjust the ITC in respect of the acquisition (s 129-5).

This type of adjustment is, subject to limitations (¶6-304), generally made annually. The calculation of each adjustment takes into account the actual total use since purchase, not just the actual use since the last adjustment (s 129-40).

The subsequent donation of something purchased does not give rise to an adjustment as it is not treated as a change of use (s 129-45): see further ¶15-040. If the "donation" is by way of a payment for something else the exclusion does not apply.

Adjustment thresholds Any change of use where the thing acquired was used in making financial supplies (¶10-010) is ignored unless the taxpayer exceeds the financial acquisitions threshold (¶10-032).

No adjustment is generally required for acquisitions, the GST-exclusive value (¶4-200) of which is, up to $1,000 (s 129-10). Where the original acquisition related solely or partly to providing financial supplies no adjustment is required for acquisitions with a GST-exclusive value of $10,000 or less.

Where several things are acquired together, the ATO is of the view that they should be considered separately for the purpose of applying the adjustment threshold (GST Ruling GSTR 2000/24). Similarly, each component of a progressive or periodic acquisition is treated separately — for example, each periodic payment of building lease is a separate acquisition.

Illustration: Applying the adjustment threshold An engineering business buys a computer and a desk for the GST-inclusive price of $4,400 and $660 respectively. Although the items were bought at the same time, appear on the same invoice and delivered together, they are treated separately for the purpose of applying the GST adjustment threshold. The GST-exclusive value of the computer exceeds the $1,000 threshold, but the desk is below it. Accordingly, the business may only be required to make an (increasing) adjustment in relation to the computer, not the desk.

Planned and actual use The amount of the adjustment depends on the difference between the "intended application" (ie planned use) and the "actual application" (ie actual use) of the thing for a "creditable purpose" (¶5-010; s 129-40).

The GST legislation uses both "use" and "apply". In many cases the use and application will be the same. However, the meaning of "used", which takes on its ordinary meaning, and "applied" should not be interpreted consistently as a matter of course (Domestic Property Developments Pty Ltd v FC of T [2022] AATA 4436, GST Ruling GSTR 2009/4DC para 134).

An increasing GST adjustment can arise where the intended application exceeds the actual application.

A decreasing GST adjustment can arise where the actual application exceeds the intended application.

The "intended application" is the extent to which the thing is acquired for a creditable purpose, ie in the course of furtherance of an enterprise, other than for making supplies that are input taxed or of a private or domestic nature (s 129-50). The "actual application" is the extent to which the thing has been applied for those purposes since its acquisition. A thing is "applied" if it is supplied, consumed, disposed of or destroyed, or the taxpayer allows another entity to do those things (s 129-55).

The use of a "direct" method for working out the extent to which the item is used for a creditable purpose is preferred by the ATO. For example, for a car, the proportion of business kilometres can be used. Where a direct method is not practicable, an indirect method, for example, an input- or output-based method (GST Ruling GSTR 2000/24) may be used (¶5-020).

Where an item is idle or not in use for part of a period, the non-use period may be disregarded to apportion the ITC according to the actual usage pattern (GST Ruling GSTR 2000/24).

Illustration: Treatment of non-use period for GST adjustments A sole trader uses a vehicle 80% for business and 20% for private purposes. The vehicle breaks down and is not used for a month during which it is repaired. The business use can still be treated as 80%.

Where there is a large number of assets the ATO will accept that a business can "pool" individual assets — on the basis of location, type of acquisition, business unit or profit/cost centre — in order to compare their actual and intended applications (GST Ruling GSTR 2000/24).

Advice acquired by a taxpayer about the feasibility of a proposed input taxed transaction (such as a takeover) is wholly non-creditable. There is no adjustment event if the taxpayer does not proceed with that transaction. However, if the same advice is then later reused for a different purpose in connection with a different transaction which is not input taxed, there may be a change of creditable purpose that may give rise to an adjustment event (GST Determination GSTD 2012/3).

Disposals on marriage breakdown A transfer of ownership (disposal) of business assets as a result of a marriage breakdown is not normally a taxable supply (¶4-090). The disposal is treated as an application to private use that may give rise to a GST adjustment if ITCs were claimed in respect of the original acquisition of the asset. As there is no ongoing use of the asset by the transferor after the disposal, an indirect method of calculating the extent of creditable use such as the "effective life" method for depreciating assets, or a "rental value" method for non-wasting assets, such as land (GST Ruling GSTR 2003/6) must be adopted.

Residential property developments Property developer taxpayers may carry out a property development with the sole intention of selling the individual residential premises, but may be tenanted pending sale. This gives rise to a difference between the extent to which premises were intended to be used for a creditable purpose (sale of new residential premises (¶11-020)) and the extent to which they are actually used (mixed purpose of input taxed residential rent (¶11-310) and marketing for sale (creditable)). This change in use gives rise to an increasing GST adjustment.

If the premises are sold before the end of the relevant adjustment period (¶6-304), an acceptable method of calculating the extent of creditable purpose in the actual application is (GST Ruling GSTR 2009/4): Consideration for the taxable supply of the new residential premises

consideration for the input taxed supplies of residential premises by way of lease Where the sale does not occur before the end of the adjustment period, but the premises are held for the purpose of sale for the whole of the period, the estimated consideration for the sale may be substituted for the actual consideration in the formula. However, if the premises were held for sale for only part of the time before sale, an additional reduction based on time would need to be made. Relevant factors in showing that the premises are being held for sale would include the fact that they are being actively marketed for sale, and that an ultimate sale is consistent with the taxpayer's business plan and past activities. Special considerations apply where the premises are partially completed, or are being undertaken in multiple stages.

Where there are no overt acts demonstrating that the residential premises are available for sale and the intention was not to sell in the short term, it will be difficult to prove the existence of relevant intention (Case 8/2009 [2009] AATA 569).

Apportionment based on an assumed effective life of the premises is not appropriate (GST Ruling GSTR 2009/4 paras 128–129; Case 2/2011 [2011] AATA 160).

Instead of revising the BAS (¶8-045) to reflect the increasing adjustment, the taxpayer may wish to consider making a voluntary disclosure in the approved form to the ATO (¶18-300).

Financial suppliers' reduced credits If a financial supplier is entitled to a reduced (75%) input tax credit (¶10-040) (RITC), any adjustment for a change of use is calculated on the basis that the item was acquired 75% for a creditable purpose (s 129-40(3)).

Illustration: Calculating adjustments on reduced credit acquisitions A credit union acquires eligible services for $22,000, including $2,000 GST, which it intends to use entirely for making financial supplies. This "intended application" entitles it to a 75% RITC of $1,500.

At the end of the adjustment period, the services have in fact been used 60% for making financial supplies and 40% for taxable supplies. The "actual application", ie the extent to which the services have been used for creditable purposes, is 85% (40% + (75% × 60%)).

The credit union is entitled to a decreasing GST adjustment of $200 calculated as: Full ITC ($2,000) × % difference between actual and intended application (85% − 75%) The credit union has received a net ITC of $1,700 ($1,500 (75% RITC) + $200 (adjustment)).
