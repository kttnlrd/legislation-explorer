# Luxury cars ¶23-100

A luxury car is a car whose value exceeds the luxury car tax (LCT) threshold (¶23-150), and does not fall within any of the exemptions (LCT Act s 25-1).

*Refs: s 25-1)*

(1) it must be a "car". For LCT purposes, this means a motor-powered road vehicle that is: (a) designed to carry a load of less than 2 tonnes and fewer than 9 passengers. This would include station wagons and 4-wheel drive vehicles, but not motorcycles or similar vehicles (b) a limousine, irrespective of its passenger-carrying capacity (LCT Act s 27-1). A limousine is a large luxurious vehicle, typically driven by a chauffeur (Uber BV v FC of T [2017] FCA 110, below example).

The ATO considers that whether a vehicle is a "road vehicle" depends on the class of vehicle, not the actual use to which a particular vehicle may be put. On this basis, it will be a road vehicle if it is in a class of vehicle that is designed for use on public roads, even though it in fact is not used on them. For example, a rally vehicle would be treated as a road vehicle unless its nature and fundamental design made it incapable of being registered for use on public roads (GST Advice GSTA TPP 077). In a 2020 private binding ruling, a racing car designed only for use on a racetrack ans could not be registered for use on public roads, was not considered subject to LCT.

Illustration: Application of LCT to vehicle An imported Hummer designed to carry 14 people was subject to LCT notwithstanding it could not legally be used on Australian roads without substantial modification. It was found to be a "road vehicle", as its essential character was that of a vehicle designed for on-road and off-road use.

It was also a "limousine", as it was an oversized chauffeur-driven vehicle that was promoted and perceived as luxurious, even though it was classed as a bus by the Department of Transport (Dreamtech International Pty Ltd v FC of T (No 2) [2010] FCAFC 103).

For income tax purposes (adopted in LCT Determination LCTD 2023/1), the designed load carrying capacity of a vehicle should be determined by reference to the Australian Design Rules (ADRs) (Case J63, 77 ATC 537). Namely, calculated as: Maximum loaded vehicle weight, ie gross weight (as indicated on the Compliance Plate) less Unladen weight, ie the weight of the vehicle with a full capacity of lubricating oil, coolant and fuel, but without goods, occupants or options other than options which are essential to the test for which the unladen weight is specified. In determining the number of passengers a vehicle is designed to carry, the driver would be included (Case J63 at p 539). (2) the car must not fall within any of the following exemptions: (a) emergency vehicles, such as ambulances or firefighting vehicles, as specified in the LCT

*Refs: s 27-1)*

(b) cars that have been specially fitted out to transport people confined to wheelchairs (unless the supply of the car is GST-free (¶12-150)) (c) commercial vehicles that are not designed for the principal purpose of carrying passengers (see below) (d) motor homes or campervans (LCT Act s 25-1).

In addition, LCT does not apply to supplies of cars that are over 2 years old (¶23-050).

Principal purpose of carrying passengers The exemption for commercial vehicles that are not designed for the "principal purpose" of carrying passengers ((c) above) would typically apply to hearses, trucks and cargo or delivery vans, but would typically not apply to station wagons, people movers or sports utility vehicles.

Where a vehicle is designed to carry both passengers and goods, the principal purpose is a matter of fact and degree, in respect of which the ATO takes the view (LCTD 2023/1):

- relevant factors include appearance and presentation of the vehicle, promotional/marketing materials,

  vehicle specifications, load carrying capacity and passenger carrying capacity

- in the case of utility vehicles, the principal purpose is determined according to what the majority of the

  designed load capacity is attributable. In this calculation, the load capacity of the vehicle is calculated

  in accordance with the ADR as the difference between its Gross Vehicle Mass and its Unladen Mass.

The component attributable to passengers is determined by multiplying the designed seating capacity (including the driver's) by 68 kg. If this amount is less than the balance of the load capacity, the vehicle satisfies the exemption

- permanent modifications may be taken into account, but modifications that can readily be reversed

  are ignored.

*Refs: s 25-1)*
