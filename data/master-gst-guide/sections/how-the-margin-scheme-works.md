# How the margin scheme works ¶11-100

The margin scheme enables the GST on otherwise fully taxable sales of real property, including new residential property developments (¶11-020), to be calculated on a concessional basis (Div 75). th Normally, GST on a supply is calculated as 1/11 of the GST-inclusive price, and a GST registered purchaser can claim an input tax credit (ITC) for the GST component. Where the purchaser is not able to claim a GST credit — such as individuals buying a home — the GST component of the price can be significant, potentially affecting the property market. Sales under the margin scheme, calculate the GST on th the sale 1/11 of the "margin" (¶11-110), and no ITC is allowed to the purchaser.

- the difference between the price for which the property is sold and the price paid for the property, for

  property acquired after 1 July 2000, or

- for property acquired before 1 July 2000, the increase in value from that date.

  Eligibility rules

- a taxable supply (¶4-000) of the following interests in real property:

– a freehold interest (including a fractional interest of a co-owner (Nullagine Investments Pty Ltd v Western Australian Club Inc (1993) 177 CLR 635; GST Ruling GSTR 2006/8)) – a strata unit, or – the grant or sale of a long-term lease (¶11-060), and

- both parties agree in writing that the margin scheme will apply (see "Documentation" below) (s 75-5).

  Margin scheme exclusions

The margin scheme cannot be applied to supplies by the seller where the seller acquired the property in any of the following circumstances:

- under a taxable supply to which the margin scheme was not applied. A seller who has not acquired

  the property under the margin scheme would have been entitled to an ITC on the purchase, and

  as a matter of policy not entitled to any further relief by way of the margin scheme. This means that

  subsequent (otherwise taxable) sales of property can only be made using the margin scheme if all

  previous sales of the property were made using the margin scheme. From a practical evidentiary point

  of view, absent error, each seller need only verify the basis in which they purchased the property.

- the property was acquired by inheritance, and the deceased person had acquired it through a supply

  that was not eligible for the margin scheme (see also ¶11-140)

- the property was acquired from another GST group member, and the last supply of the property from

  a non-group member was ineligible for the margin scheme (¶11-140)

- the property was acquired by the seller from the operator of a joint venture in which the seller was a

  participant, and the operator had acquired it through a supply that was ineligible for the margin scheme

(¶11-140)

- For supplies made from 9 December 2008 not made under a written contract made before that date,

  or pursuant to an option granted before that date that specified the consideration or a way of working it

  out:

– the property was acquired GST-free as a supply of a going concern (¶11-500), a farm (¶11-410) or subdivided farm land (¶11-420), from an entity that was registered or required to be, and that entity had acquired it through a taxable supply on which GST was worked out without applying the margin scheme. For other rules applying where there are GST-free supplies, see ¶11-140 – the property was acquired from an associate for no consideration (¶17-500) and the associate was registered or required to be, where the supply by that associate was not a taxable supply in the course of an enterprise (¶4-090), and the associate had acquired the property through a taxable supply on which the GST was calculated not using the margin scheme. This exclusion can also apply where, for example, a government entity acquires the property for no consideration from an associate without the associate technically making a "supply". For other rules applying to associates, see ¶11-140.

These exclusions prevent arrangements under which taxpayers attempted to "refresh" their eligibility to use the margin scheme by interposing a GST-free or non-taxable supply before selling the property.

The exclusions applicable to supplies made from 9 December 2008 do not apply if more than one GST-free or non- taxable sale is interposed, recognising the complexity that would arise if it were necessary to look back through multiple transactions. However, it is likely that the general anti-avoidance rules in Div 165 may apply in any event where there is a contrived arrangement designed to exploit this rule (¶20-040).

Illustration: Eligibility to apply the margin scheme Assume that all parties are registered for GST.

X sells a property to Y. X accounts for GST in the normal way and Y claims an ITC. Y sells the property to Z as a GST-free going concern. Z develops the property for residential use and sells it to Purchaser.

Z is not prevented from using the margin scheme on the sale to Purchaser, because Z itself did not acquire the property under a taxable sale on which GST was calculated in the normal way. However, Z is prevented from using the margin scheme because Z acquired the property from Y, who in turn acquired it under a taxable sale on which GST was calculated in the normal way.

Pros and cons of margin scheme The main advantage of the margin scheme is that the GST is reduced. The corresponding disadvantage being that no ITC can be claimed by a GST registered purchaser (s 75-20). This is of most significance in sales to purchasers, such as those unregistered for GST, who are unable to claim an ITC, for example, sales of new residential units by a developer, house and land packages by a project builder, or land. This gives property developers the flexibility to apply the margin scheme to certain sales and not to others, depending on whether the purchaser is entitled to GST ITCs on the purchase of the property.

Documentation The margin scheme can only be applied to the sale of property when both the purchaser and seller have agreed, in writing by the time of settlement (¶11-000), to apply the margin scheme to the sale (s 75-5).The Commissioner has the discretion to extend the time by which the parties must have made a written agreement where the Commissioner is satisfied that neither the recipient nor the supplier have reported their GST obligations based on the margin scheme not applying and all the other requirements for application of the margin scheme have been met, and there is no arrangement that produces an outcome contrary to the policy of the legislation, for example where the failure to make the agreement arose because of a mistaken belief that the supply was GST-free (Practice Statement PS LA 2005/15).

It would appear that once a supply has been made on the basis of an agreement to apply the margin scheme, the GST consequences cannot be changed by the parties subsequently agreeing to revoke the agreement (Interpretative Decision ID 2010/83). Notwithstanding, it is prudent to include an indemnity clause in a margin scheme agreement in the event that it transpires that the sale was not eligible to be made under the margin scheme.

A trustee, acting as a bare trustee, can sign a margin scheme agreement (GST Ruling GSTR 2008/3).

As use of the margin scheme prevents ITCs being claimed, there is no obligation on the vendor to issue a tax invoice (s 75-30). A developer who purchased property under the margin scheme and incorrectly claimed substantial ITCs on the basis of a tax invoice supplied by the vendor was penalised for making reckless false statement (Barcia Pty Ltd v FC of T 2008 ATC ¶10-062, [2008] AATA 1073).

Selling land as part of selling a business It appears that the margin scheme can apply where land is being sold as part of closing down a business, as such a sale is treated as being made in the course of carrying on an enterprise (¶3-020).

However, this would not apply if the sale was a GST-free supply of a going concern (¶11-500).

Subdivisions Taxpayers may use the consideration method, the valuation method or the GST-inclusive method, whichever is appropriate, when calculating the margin on a taxable supply of subdivided land (s 75-15).
