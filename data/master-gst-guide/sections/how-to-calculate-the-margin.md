# How to calculate the margin ¶11-110

The margin is generally calculated as the difference between the cost to the taxpayer (ie the consideration paid for the acquisition of the real property) and the amount the taxpayer charges on disposing of it (ie the th consideration received by the taxpayer for their supply of the real property). The GST is calculated as 1/11 of that margin (s 75-10). This calculation can appear deceptively straight forward. The more sophisticated a property development, the more complex the application of margin scheme can become, as the taxpayer in

At first instance, the taxpayer failed to convince the ART that the GST had been passed on, thereby resulting in a windfall gain if the excess GST (paid in the later period) were to be refunded (¶8-115). On appeal, the Full Federal Court in Geocon Land Holdings No 5 Pty Ltd v FC of T [2025] FCAFC 172 remitted the issue of whether GST had been passed on back to the ART for redetermination on the basis that where the taxpayer would have received the same amount from the purchasers irrespective of the overpaid GST, increased profitability alone (resulting for the refund of GST) could not be properly characterised as a "windfall gain".

The taxpayer's argument of "economic equivalence" with respect to the over-reported and under-reported GST such that there was no excess GST that could give rise to a windfall gain if refunded was unconvincing to the ART, and not required to be addressed on appeal, the Full Federal Court having remitted the passing on issue to the ART for redetermination.

Similarly, by not valuing the non-monetary consideration directly as implored by the clear language of s 9-75, the onus rested on the taxpayer in ZKSM v FC of T [2025] ARTA 1298, to show that what was being valued reflected that which was provided. Application of a deductive methodology, to a valuation commissioned by the taxpayer for the purpose of providing assurance to stakeholders that the post-development lots were being sold at their market rate, such that the difference between the valuations and the amount of monetary consideration paid for the undeveloped lots was a proxy for the value of the non-monetary consideration (development services) (economically, this method could not be expected to (and did not) produce a significant margin on which GST is calculated) was not accepted, as that which was acquired by the taxpayer in exchange for consideration was a large parcel of undeveloped land to be transformed under the contract not the long-term lease being sold by the taxpayer.

Calculating the cost In calculating the margin, the cost does not include any consideration for improvements, construction or development costs of building work, additional costs such as solicitors' fees and stamp duty, or any expenses in bringing the property into legal or physical existence (s 75-14; Sterling Guardian Pty Ltd v FC of T [2006] FCAFC 12).

However, where the grant of the land was conditional on certain preparatory works (as distinct from building construction work) being carried out, and would be forfeited if those works were not done, the cost of those works could be included as non-monetary consideration for the land (WYPF v FC of T [2021] AATA 3050).

A similar allowance was made in respect of non-monetary consideration by way of development costs provided by a related party in SFQV; however, the full Federal Court agreed with the ATO that liability to GST crystallised in an earlier tax period prior to the total consideration being known. The GST character of non-monetary consideration must be determined in accordance with the agreement between the parties. The fact that the total consideration was not known did not attract the application of s 29-25 (¶7-440).

Illustration: Cost of improvements when applying the margin scheme The cost of eligible land is $250,000. Improvements of $50,000 are carried out, on which input tax credits are claimed. The land is then sold for $360,000. In calculating the margin, the improvements are ignored. The margin is therefore $110,000 and the GST is $10,000.

The cost would include any additional amount you paid as an adjustment for council rates and land tax paid in advance by the vendor of the property prior to settlement (GST Determination GSTD 2006/3), but not legal fees, stamp duty or registration fees on the purchase.

If the sale is part of a subdivision, your cost is calculated on a pro rata basis (s 75-15). You may use any reasonable method of apportionment, for example, on the basis of area, or expected resale price. Note that certain subdivisions by farmers are GST-free in any event (¶11-420).

Illustrations: Allocating cost to subdivisions of land A developer acquires land for $500,000 which it subdivides into 5 blocks. The land has a uniform value per square metre and each block is of equal size. It is therefore reasonable to allocate a cost of $100,000 to each block. The developer sells one block for $155,000 and later sells the remaining 4 blocks for $166,000 each. Using the margin scheme, the GST on the first block is $5,000 th                                                                                      th (ie 1/11 × $55,000). The GST on each of the other 4 blocks would be $6,000 (ie 1/11 × $66,000).

Assume instead that one of the blocks is 1,200 square metres and the others are 600 square metres. It would be reasonable to allocate a cost of $166,667 (ie 1,200/3,600 × 500,000) to the first block and $83,333 (ie 600/3,600 × 500,000) to each of the other 4 blocks. If the first block is sold for $300,000, the margin would therefore be $133,333 and the GST would be $12,121.18. If the other blocks are each sold for $150,000, the margin on each would be $66,667 and the GST on each would be $6,060.63.

A developer buys land for $400,000 and builds 4 strata units on it. The expected sale prices of the units are $540,000, $480,000, $600,000 and $780,000, a total of $2,400,000. It is reasonable to allocate the cost of those units as follows: Unit 1: 540,000/2,400,000 × 400,000 = $90,000 Unit 2: 480,000/2,400,000 × 400,000 = $80,000 Unit 3: 600,000/2,400,000 × 400,000 = $100,000 Unit 4: 780,000/2,400,000 × 400,000 = $130,000 If, say, Unit 3 is sold for $610,000, the margin would be $510,000 and the GST would be $46,363.63.

Where the full price under the contract has not been paid, the margin will reflect only the amount paid (s 75-12). If there is a subsequent additional payment, the supplier will be entitled to a decreasing adjustment (s 75-27).

In calculating the margin where land was sold on the exercise of a call (purchase) option, the consideration for the supply did not include the amount paid for the call option, even though it was applied to the purchase price (GST Determination GSTD 2014/2). The option fee is instead treated as consideration for the supply of the call option, not for the separate supply of the land (¶4-020). This applies even if the contract specifically states that the purchase price is inclusive of the option fee (The Trustee for the Whitby Trust v FC of T [2017] AATA 343).

For special rules for calculating the margin in particular situations, see ¶11-140. The ATO has also warned that it is investigating the efficacy of certain schemes designed to minimise the GST payable under the margin scheme through the use of associates (Taxpayer Alert TA 2009/4).

For the ATO's practice on time limits for refund claims made where GST liability was miscalculated using the margin scheme, see ¶8-110.

- if you acquired the property before 1 July 2000, the cost is calculated as the value as at 1 July 2000.

Illustration: Using the margin scheme for property held at 1 July 2000 A developer bought land in 1999 for $75,000. At 1 July 2000, the developer was registered and the land was valued at $100,000.

The developer subsequently sells the land for $144,000. The margin is therefore $44,000 and GST is $4,000.

In this example, note that after allowing for GST, the developer clears $140,000. If the developer had not adopted the margin scheme, it would have had to sell at $154,000 to achieve this result. Using the margin scheme therefore enables it to make the price more attractive to those purchasers who cannot claim input tax credits (eg private purchasers). This may also lead to savings on stamp duty.

If the Commonwealth, a state or territory (¶11-400) held unimproved land as at 1 July 2000 and subsequently sells it, the cost of the land is calculated as the value of that land on the date the sale is made (s 75-10(3)). Typically, if the land is still unimproved at the time of sale, this will mean that the margin will be nil, because the consideration paid on the date of supply will normally be the market value of the land.

However, if in the interim the land has been "improved" (¶11-400), the margin will, in effect, be the added value that the improvement brings to the land, measured as the difference between the sale price for the improved land and the value of the land if it had been unimproved at that time. This applies even though, in the meantime, there has been a change, for no consideration, in the government agency that held the land (GST Determination GSTD 2006/4). Where a number of lots are sold together, the question of whether the land was unimproved as at 1 July 2000 is determined separately for each lot (FC of T v Landcom [2022] FCAFC 204; GST Ruling GSTR 2006/6). In the ACT each long-term lease granted in the form of a Crown lease, regardless of the number of blocks covered by the Crown lease, represents a single long-term lease to which ss 38-445 and 38-450 apply separately (GSTR 2006/6 para 47C).

Where a grant of the government land results from the post-30 June 2000 conversion of a short-term lease issued before 1 July 2000, the valuation date is 1 July 2000.

Where a taxpayer granted a long-term lease over improved freehold land acquired before 1 July 2000, the relevant valuation was that of the freehold as at 1 July 2000 (Interpretative Decision ID 2006/255).

Where the cost is based on a valuation, it will of course be the case that a lower valuation will increase the margin, and therefore the GST. The possibility of dispute over the valuation should therefore be considered in drafting the contract. For a case involving an unsuccessful attempt to sue a valuer over a valuation for margin purposes, see Derring Lane Pty Ltd v Fitzgibbon (Civil Claims) [2006] VSC 46.

It appears that there does not have to be strict identity between what the taxpayer acquired before the valuation date and what it subsequently supplied. For example, a developer was able to use the margin scheme in relation to the sale of strata units, even though as at 1 July 2000 it had only held an equitable interest under a contract to buy the building and no strata titles had been issued (Brady King Pty Ltd v FC of T [2008] FCAFC 118). This decision reverses an earlier restrictive court ruling that had cast doubts as to the circumstances in which the margin scheme could be used by unit developers.
