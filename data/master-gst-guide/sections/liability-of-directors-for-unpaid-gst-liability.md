# Liability of directors for unpaid GST liability ¶8-105

In certain situations, the directors of a company may be personally liable to pay an amount equal to the company's outstanding GST liability, including wine equalisation tax and luxury car tax. This brings the GST system into line with the rules that apply to income tax liabilities (Taxation Administration Act, Sch 1, Div 269). It applies to tax periods starting on or after 1 April 2020.

Under these rules, directors must cause the company to meet its obligation to pay its net GST, WET and LCT, including liability for instalments (¶8-037) and liability for estimated liabilities (¶8-095). This duty continues until the company pays its liability or goes into voluntary administration or begins to be wound up (Administration Act, Sch 1, s 269-15).

If the company has not satisfied one of these options on or before the due date for the payment of the net GST, the directors of the company are each personally liable to pay to the Commissioner a penalty equal to the unpaid amount of the company's liability.

This liability applies to persons who were directors of the company on or after the end of the tax period in relation to which the company's liability arose, irrespective of whether they cease to be directors after that date. Persons who become directors after the due date for payment may also become liable where 30 days after they become directors, the company has still not complied with its obligations (Administration Act, Sch 1, s 269-20).

Care should be taken to ensure that a payment made pursuant to a penalty notice is allocated to that penalty liability, as the Commissioner has wide powers to allocate any payment received against any other outstanding tax liabilities of the company (Kedwell v DFC of T 2020 ATC ¶20-765; [2020] NSWCA 238).

The Tax Office has been particularly active in warning directors of potential liabilities and in pursuing claims for payment.

Notice of penalty The Commissioner must give 21 days' notice to the person before instituting proceedings to recover the penalty (Administration Act, Sch 1 s 269-25). The notice must:

- set out what the Commissioner thinks is the unpaid amount of the company's liability

- state that the person is liable to pay a penalty of an amount equal to that unpaid amount, and

- explain the main circumstances in which the penalty will be remitted.

  The person to whom a notice is sent does not have to be a current director.

Penalties imposed on directors are not subject to GIC (¶18-300), but may be subject to pre-judgment interest (Canty v DFC of T 2005 ATC 4470).

Service of penalty notice The Commissioner can serve the penalty notice by delivering it or posting it to the address shown in ASIC documents to be the person's place of residence or business, or to have been so within the last seven days (Administration Act, Sch 1 s 269-50). The Commissioner may also send a copy of the notice to the person's tax agent's address (Administration Act Sch 1 s 269-52). The notice is taken to be given at the time that the notice is left or posted by the Commissioner (Administration Act, Sch 1 s 269-25), not at the time the letter would ordinarily have been delivered. The Commissioner does not need to satisfy the court that a notice was actually received by the person (DFC of T v Tannous 2016 ATC ¶20-591).

for unpaid GST liability The penalty may be remitted (cancelled) if the director complies with the obligation before the notice is issued or within 21 days of the date the notice is issued (Administration Act, Sch 1, s 269-30). However, a special rule applies where the compliance takes the form of an appointment of an administrator or a commencement of winding up, and this takes place more than three months after the date the company's GST liability arose. In this case, the director's penalty remission is limited to the amount of the company's liability reported to the Commissioner as at the end of that three-month period (TAA Sch 1 s 269-30(2)). If the director was appointed during or after that three-month period, the three months instead starts from the date of their appointment.

(1) because of illness or for some other good reason, it would have been unreasonable to expect the person to take part, and the person did not take part, in the management of the company at any time when the person was a director and the directors were under a duty to comply (2) the person took all reasonable steps to ensure that the directors complied or there were no reasonable steps that could have been taken, or (3) the company treated the GST legislation as applying to a matter in a way that was "reasonably arguable" and the company took reasonable care in applying the legislation (Administration Act, Sch 1 s 269-35).

In practice, it can be difficult to meet the requirements of these defences. In one case, a director who had concerns about the management of a company was unable to rely on the second defence that he had taken all reasonable steps to ensure compliance. The court noted that, although he had tried unsuccessfully to convince the other directors to comply, as a single director he could have caused the company to begin to be wound up and should have obtained professional advice at an earlier stage (DFC of T v Saunig 2002 ATC 5135). The need to seek legal advice as soon as a director becomes aware of the true state of a company's financial obligations was also stressed in DFC of T v Solomon 2003 ATC 4474.

A person who, two months after the unremitted amounts were due, became a director of a company for 17 days, and who received a notice nearly three months after ceasing to be a director, was also unable to rely on the defences (DFC of T v Fitzgerald 95 ATC 4587). Although the director had not been aware of the company's financial position or the amounts due while he was a director, this was not sufficient to provide a defence. The court warned that it was the responsibility of new directors at the time of, or before taking up, their appointments to make inquiries of the relevant officers of the company as to whether there were any moneys owing. If incorrect information was given to such an inquiry, a defence may be available.

A director who fails to make out the defence that he has taken reasonable steps to ensure compliance is also unable to obtain relief under the s 1318 of the Corporations Act 2001 (Administration Act, Sch 1 s 269-35(5)).

A director is entitled to be indemnified by the company, and any other persons from whom the Commissioner is entitled to recover, for any payment made by way of penalty under these provisions (Administration Act, Sch 1, s 269-45).
