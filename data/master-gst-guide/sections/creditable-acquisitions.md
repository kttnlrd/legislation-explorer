# Creditable acquisitions ¶5-010

A taxpayer is entitled to an input tax credit (ITC) in respect of the GST component of the price of "creditable acquisitions" (ss 11-5, 11-20) made by them. A creditable acquisition is a purchase or acquisition of something to be used in carrying on the taxpayer's enterprise, that satisfies all of the following conditions:

- the taxpayer must "acquire" something

- the taxpayer must acquire the thing for a "creditable purpose"

- the supply of the thing to the taxpayer must be a "taxable supply", ie it must be subject to GST

  (¶4-000)

- the taxpayer must provide consideration (¶4-020), or be liable to provide consideration, and

- the taxpayer must be registered or required to be registered (¶3-000) for GST. See ¶3-070 where

  registration is cancelled.

Whether a taxpayer has made a creditable acquisition is determined from the perspective of the taxpayer purchaser, not of the supplier.

Example: Creditable acquisition A government department paying a subsidy to a taxi company in respect of discounted fares provided for disabled customers makes a creditable acquisition of transport services. Notwithstanding that from the taxi company's (ie the supplier) perspective it was making a supply of travel to the customer (FC of T v Department of Transport (Vic) 2010 ATC ¶20-196; [2010] FCAFC 84: see ¶4-040).

Similarly, it would appear where an employer that provides a Cabcharge (or similar) facility for its employees who work late, is entitled to claim an ITC for the GST component of the taxi fare.

In addition, before an ITC can be claimed, the taxpayer is generally also required to hold a tax invoice (¶5-100) for the transaction at the time they lodge their BAS (Business Activity Statement) for the tax period in which they claim the ITC in respect of the transaction (s 29-10). "Acquisition"

An acquisition includes anything else that would be an acquisition within the ordinary meaning of the word.

However, receiving a payment of money for the supply of something else is not treated as an acquisition (s 9-10(4)) — this would result in ITCs in respect of a single transaction being claimed twice.

Taxpayers are entitled to ITCs that meet the legislative criteria, including illegal acquisitions!

The onus is always on the taxpayer to show that the acquisition actually took place. Claims for ITCs in respect of motor vehicles were unsuccessful in Byron Pty Ltd v FC of T [2019] AATA 2042 and Stallion (NSW) Pty Ltd v FC of T 2019 ATC ¶20-707; [2019] FCA 1306. Where the purported agreement completely failed, the taxpayer did not actually acquire anything, and no consideration was ever paid a claim for an ITC was disallowed in Ecosse Group Holdings Pty Ltd v FC of T 2024 ATC ¶10-730; [2024] AATA 2073. - the taxpayer acquired it in carrying on their "enterprise" (¶3-020). This requires identification of the

  particular nature of the enterprise. The ATO considers that this requires reference to matters such

The ATO also considers, on the basis of analogous income tax cases, whether a thing is acquired in carrying on the taxpayer's enterprise depends on the purpose for which the taxpayer acquired the thing (GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1). Purpose is determined objectively (AXA Asia Pacific Holdings Ltd v FC of T [2008] FCA 1834), though the taxpayer's subjective purpose may be a relevant consideration (Macquarie Finance Ltd v FC of T [2005] FCAFC 205; GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1; see also Konebada Pty Ltd ATF William Lewski Family Trust v FC of T [2024] FCAFC 42). A nexus between an enterprise and expenses is not an incident of substantiation. The taxpayer in Fraser v FC of T [2025] ARTA 2153 was unable to present convincing evidence that the expenses in respect of which ITCs were claimed had sufficient connection with the taxpayers social media video making enterprise to be creditable acquisitions.

- According to the ATO, some of the factors that would suggest that an acquisition is made in carrying

  on an enterprise are:

– it is incidental or relevant to the commencement, continuance or termination of the enterprise – the thing acquired is used by the enterprise in making supplies – the acquisition secures a real benefit or advantage – it is one which an ordinary business person would be likely to make – it does not meet the personal needs of individuals such as partners or directors – it helps to protect or preserve the enterprise entity, structure or organisation, and – it is made by the entity in accordance with statutory requirements (GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1)

- the acquisition must not be of a private or domestic nature. On the basis of analogous income tax

  cases, that ATO considers that this would normally cover matters such as living and childminding

  expenses, and most home/work travel (GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1), and

- the acquisition must not relate to making supplies that would be input taxed, unless specified

  exceptions apply (¶5-020). Typical input taxed supplies include financial supplies (¶10-000) and

  residential accommodation (¶11-310). The relationship with the supply must be "real and substantial",

  not merely trivial or incidental. However, it is not necessary that there be a direct link between the

  acquisition and an actual supply that is input taxed (HP Mercantile Pty Limited v FC of T [2005] FCAFC

126; GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1). Nor is the relationship subject to an overriding "purpose" test. For example, where a mining company acquired residential accommodation to lease to its employees in remote areas, it was denied ITCs on associated acquisitions as they related to the (input taxed) supply of the premises. This was the case notwithstanding that the (input taxed) supply (of residential accommodation) was not a commercial objective of the company itself, but was made as a purely incidental part of running its (taxable) mining operations (Rio Tinto Services Ltd v FC of T 2015 ATC ¶20-525; [2015] FCAFC 117).

Examples: Acquisitions related to making input taxed supplies The acquisition of a computer by a bank that it uses in making its input taxed financial supplies is not a creditable acquisition.

The acquisition of special software to be used exclusively by the loan staff of a retailer that also offers loan finance to customers is not a creditable acquisition.

It appears irrelevant whether the acquisition precedes or follows the input taxed supply (HP Mercantile Pty Ltd v FC of T 2005 ATC 4571; [2005] FCAFC 126).

Apportionment is appropriate where the acquisition is partly private or domestic, or partly related to making input taxed supplies (¶5-020).

Examples: Claiming ITCs — relationship to carrying on an enterprise The purchase of stock by a bookshop to sell in the business is a creditable acquisition for which ITCs can be claimed, as too a new broom that the shopowner buys to sweep the shop. But the bus ticket that the shopowner buys to travel to the shop each day is not a creditable acquisition for which ITCs can be claimed because it is a private or domestic expense. The same applies to other expenses such as food, clothing, shelter and child care that are treated as private or domestic under the income tax law (based on GST Ruling GSTR 2006/4).

Kat, an employee, is dismissed and commences business as a sole trader. Kat brings unfair dismissal proceedings against her former employer. Although Kat is now carrying on an enterprise, she cannot claim an ITC for the legal expenses, because they were incurred as a result of her employment, not her enterprise.

A public company that is subject to a takeover offer obtains an independent report on the merits of the takeover, and obtains legal advice on the preparation of a formal shareholder statement required by law. The ATO accepts these acquisitions are made in carrying on the company's enterprise (GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1).

Input tax credits in respect of life insurance premiums, home contents insurance, home electricity and gas charges, airfares for guests, cable TV subscriptions and dental and grooming costs incurred by a GST registered business person were disallowed. Input tax credits were allowable in part for newspaper costs and travel expenses; and a full claim was allowed for certain airfares, and for legal and accounting fees (Ryan v FC of T [2014] AATA 818).

Whether goods or services have been acquired for business or private purposes is determined from the perspective of the business.

Example: Business purposes Lavish Pty Ltd provides cars to its sales representatives as part of their salary packages. The cars can be used for personal purposes when not being used on company business. However, from the company's point of view, it acquired the cars purely because of the requirements of its business. They are therefore treated as being acquired wholly for business purposes. (Based on GST Ruling GSTR 2006/4.) It follows that items such as food, clothing, shelter and child care will be treated as private if they are acquired for the benefit of the business owner, but not if they are required to be provided to others as part of that business.

Examples: Private or domestic expenses paid by a business A religious order provides food and accommodation to monks who live and work at its monastery. Although the food and accommodation are private to the monks, it is treated as being acquired by the order wholly for business purposes (based on GST Ruling GSTR 2006/4.) A swimming pool constructed as part of the intended establishment of a naturist retreat is an acquisition made in the course of carrying on the taxpayer's enterprise notwithstanding that the pool is also capable of private or domestic use (Russell v FC of T [2011] FCAFC 10).

A business cannot claim ITCs for non-taxable expenses such as employees' wages (¶4-090), input taxed expenses such as life insurance premiums (¶10-100) and interest on loans (¶10-000), GST-free expenses such as water or sewerage (¶16-200), or most non-deductible fringe benefits such as entertainment.

No tax deduction allowed The criteria to determine whether a taxpayer is entitled to an ITC in respect of an expense are independent of whether the expense is an allowable deduction for income tax purposes. Generally, it is not necessary that an expense be deductible for income tax purposes to be entitled to an ITC. For example, the purchase of a capital item will not normally be tax deductible, but this does not prevent it being a creditable acquisition for the purpose of claiming ITCs. This applies equally to acquisitions of a capital nature which are made to provide an enduring benefit to the enterprise, or to restrict competition (GST Ruling GSTR 2008/1). Similarly, where goods or services are acquired in order to carry out a feasibility study, that acquisition may qualify for ITCs irrespective of whether the cost would be tax deductible (¶3-020). However, taxpayers are not entitled to claim ITCs to the extent that they are in respect of certain expenses that are specifically made non-deductible under the income tax law (s 69-5), including under the following provisions:

- recreational club expenses (ITAA 1997 s 26-45)

- leisure facility expenses (ITAA 1997 s 26-50)

- non-compulsory uniforms (ITAA 1997 Div 34)

- family maintenance (ITAA 1997 s 26-40)

- relatives' travel (ITAA 1997 s 26-30)

- entertainment (ITAA 1997 Div 32; ITAA 1936 ss 51AEA, 51AEB, 51AEC). However, ITCs may be

  allowable for acquisitions in the limited situations where the expenditure is deductible, for example,

  where morning and afternoon teas or light lunches are provided to employees during the working day

(Taxation Ruling IT 2675); food, accommodation and travel provided as part of a qualifying seminar (Taxation Determination TD 93/195); entertainment provided to employees as a taxable fringe benefit; or food and drink provided at a non-social occasion at an in-house dining facility to employees or, subject to conditions, to clients (Interpretative Decision ID 2005/122). See further ¶24-210

- penalties (ITAA 1997 s 26-5)

- certain non-cash business benefits used for private purposes (ITAA 1936 s 51AK).

There is no prohibition on the maker of an unlawful supply claiming ITCs for creditable acquisitions related to that supply, although no income tax deduction is allowable for outgoings that are related to activities for which the taxpayer has been convicted of an indictable offence (ITAA 1997 s 26-54).

Example: No ITC on non-deductible entertainment Colossus Enterprises buys $110,000 worth of sporting and theatre tickets which it distributes free to its major clients. These would be a non-deductible entertainment expense for which it would not be entitled to claim an ITC of $10,000 for the GST included in the price of the tickets.

Conversely, the fact that an expense is tax deductible does not necessarily mean that the taxpayer is entitled to an ITC in respect of its acquisition. For example, a bank is not entitled to claim ITCs in respect of deductible expenses incurred in making input taxed financial supplies. The GST component of those expenses would be deductible for income tax purposes as part of the cost incurred.

These restrictions apply equally to bodies that are exempt from tax, such as charities.

Purchasing from the government The Commonwealth Government is not liable to pay GST, but has a notional liability for the purposes of its dealings with others (¶1-300). If a taxpayer acquires goods or services for their business from the government, it can claim ITCs as if this notional GST had applied. Similarly, taxpayers can claim ITCs if they make a business purchase from a state government, and a notional GST liability is included in the payment (s 177-3). This should be distinguished from GST-free supplies made by governments (¶4-080), and the tax invoice requirements borne in mind when claiming ITCs for notional GST.

Corresponding rules apply to untaxable Commonwealth entities.

Acquisitions by partners and partnerships If a partner of a partnership acquires something in their capacity as a partner, this is treated as an acquisition by the partnership, not the partner (as an individual) (s 184-5). Factors that may indicate that the acquisition has been made in the capacity as a partner include:

- the acquisition is used in the partnership enterprise

- it is made with the consent of all the partners

- it is paid for out of the partnership profits or from a partnership account

- the invoice shows the firm or business name, or the names of all the partners, as recipient (GST

  Ruling GSTR 2003/13).

The requirement to hold a tax invoice is waived in certain situations where a taxpayer holds a document that contains a partner's identity and certain other requirements (LI 2023/9: see ¶5-130).

Corresponding rules apply to acquisitions made by members of the committee of management of any other unincorporated association.

A general law partnership (¶3-015) can claim an ITC on the acquisition of an "in kind" contribution by a partner on formation of the partnership. However, the partner cannot claim an ITC on the acquisition of an interest in the partnership, as its supply to the partner is not a taxable supply (¶10-010; GST Ruling GSTR 2003/13).

A partner who has resigned and later incurs legal expenses in recovering its equity in the partnership, is not entitled to an ITC in respect of those expenses, as they are not incurred in carrying on an enterprise (Interpretative Decision ID 2002/11). 4-year time limit on claiming input tax credit The tax period in which an ITC can be claimed will depend on whether the taxpayer remits GST on a cash (¶7-300) or accruals (¶7-205) basis.

In certain situations, it is open to a taxpayer to defer a claim for an ITC from the tax period for which it would normally be attributable, to a later tax period (¶5-125). However, any deferral is subject to a time limit. The effect is that a taxpayer will generally lose the entitlement to the credit unless it has been taken into account in an assessment within 4 years after the taxpayer was required to lodge a return for that tax period (s 93-5).

The words of the statutory provision are clear, unequivocally stating that the taxpayer ceases to be entitled to claim ITCs more than 4 years from the due date for lodgment of the relevant BAS. An objection, review or appeal of a decision disallowing such a claim does not effectively "refresh" the entitlement of the taxpayer to the ITCs (Trustee for Barth Family Trust v FC of T [2025] ARTA 1558) (on appeal).

Example: Deferring claiming an ITC A taxpayer remitting GST on a cash basis makes a creditable acquisition in August 2022 but neglected to take it into account in its September 2022 quarterly return, which was required to be lodged by 28 October 2022. The taxpayer has until 28 October 2026 to claim the ITC in a return, thereby giving rise to an assessment (¶8-080).

Artificial claims The ATO considers that certain arrangements where an entity uses an associate to secure ITCs on the construction of residential premises for lease, and to defer the corresponding GST liability, are not tax- effective (GST Ruling GSTR 2010/1 (¶20-000); Taxpayer Alerts TA 2009/4, TA 2009/5). Taxpayer Alert TA 2025/2 (¶18-300) addresses high-value fraudulent ITC claims more generally. The ATO is aware of instances where arrangements involve the purported sale of goods, including real property, equipment or machinery. The ATO also queries the effectiveness of certain arrangements where an entity claims an ITC on a purported acquisition of an intangible right (on non-commercial terms), with the provision of vendor finance under which payments are contingent on a future event (Taxpayer Alert TA 2012/5).
