# Special accounting for gambling ¶16-000

For registered makers of gambling supplies (Div 126) GST may be calculated on a "global" basis. Gambling supplies include:

- supplying tickets in lotteries and raffles

- accepting bets on the outcome of lotteries and raffles

- accepting bets on the outcome of races, games, sporting events or any other events (s 126-35).

Gambling supplies also include gambling in casinos, gaming machines in clubs and hotels, lucky membership draws for club members, betting (but not participating) in racing, football (GST Ruling GSTR 2002/3) but does not include GST-free raffles or bingo conducted by charities (¶15-020), or gambling outside Australia (see further below).

The global accounting basis is available to avoid the necessity of calculating GST on each gambling transaction such as, every spin of the roulette wheel, for every player, for every square on the table, by a casino operator. Broadly speaking, the global accounting basis applies GST to the difference between the total amount of the prizes and the total amount wagered, effectively applying GST to profits rather than revenue. The global accounting basis calculates GST on the margin for each tax period. This methodology is mathematically equivalent to applying GST on a transactional basis and allowing input tax credits (ITCs) on prizes paid out.

Illustration: Global accounting for gambling supplies Over one tax period, Percy the bookie receives $100,000 in bets and pays out $45,000 to winning punters. GST of $5,000 is payable th on the margin of $55,000 (($100,000 − $45,000) × 1/11 ).

The "total amount wagered" excludes refunds that become due. "Total monetary prizes" includes declared betting dividends, irrespective of whether they were paid during the tax period (TAB Ltd v FC of T [2005] NSWSC 552). The total monetary prizes also includes points that are awarded for a winning bet, or for participation, and are redeemed for money or casino chips. Points awarded as a result of the purchase of meals, drinks or other non-gambling services, or purchased by a participating member, are not treated as monetary prizes even when redeemed for cash as they do not relate to gambling.

Total monetary prizes exclude prizes paid in relation to GST-free supplies (s 126-10(3), overturning International All Sports v FC of T [2011] FCA 824). The exclusion of all GST-free supplies ensures that prizes paid in relation to GST-free bets placed by non-residents (see "Overseas bets" below) are not included in total monetary prizes.

Circumstances may arise allowing the dollar value of reward points that could be redeemed when wagering to be taken into account in determining both the total monetary prizes and the total amounts wagered (SLDL v FC of T [2024] AATA 912).

Payments (commissions and rebates) made to junket tour operators should not be taken into account in determining a casino's global GST amount (irrespective of how they are calculated), and should instead be treated under the normal GST rules (FC of T v Burswood Nominees Ltd as trustee for Burswood Property Trust & Anor [2021] FCAFC 151).

If the total monetary prizes are more than the total amount wagered, there is no gambling GST for that period (s 126-10). However, the excess amount can be added to the total monetary prizes for the next period (s 126-15). In effect, carrying forward the shortfall for the period to be offset against future profits.

Gambling operators can claim ITCs on their purchases in the usual way, but cannot claim ITCs for money prizes, as these have already been taken into account in calculating the margin. Non-monetary prizes are not taken into account in calculating the margin. It follows that ITCs can be claimed on their original acquisition if consideration was paid. The actual granting of the non-monetary prize to the winner is not treated as a taxable supply, as there is no additional consideration provided (GST Ruling GSTR 2002/3).

Illustration: GST treatment of gambling prizes Purchased non-cash prize. The prize in a commercial raffle is a car that the raffle operator bought from a dealer for $22,000 (including GST). The dealer had itself acquired the car for $15,000 (including $1,364 GST). The total paid for raffle tickets is $66,000. As there are no monetary prizes, the margin is $66,000, so the operator's GST is $6,000. However, the operator can claim th an ITC of $2,000 (1/11 × $22,000). The net GST payable by the operator is therefore $4,000. The net GST payable by the dealer is $636 ($2,000 − $1,364). The total GST payable by operator and dealer is therefore $4,636. (For illustrative purpose it is assumed that the raffle is conducted wholly within the same tax period.) Donated non-cash prize. If the car had been donated to the operator (eg by a car dealer), the position would be:

- the dealer could continue to claim an ITC of $1,364 on its own acquisition of the car, provided that it could show that the

  donation was in its business interests

- the $2,000 GST would not apply on the donation to the operator and no ITC could be claimed by the operator on the car

- GST for the operator would be levied on the margin (ie $66,000).

Accordingly, the net GST payable by the operator would be $6,000 and the dealer would be entitled to an ITC of $1,364. The combined net amount payable of $4,636 is the same where the non-cash prise is purchased.

Combination of cash and non-cash prizes. A hotel raffles a cash prize of $900 and a camera which cost $330 including $30 GST.

The proceeds collected from the raffle are $2,000. The margin is therefore $1,100 (proceeds of $2,000 − $900 cash prize). The th amount of GST is $100 (1/11 × $1,100).

The hotel can claim an ITC for the $30 GST included in the purchase price of the camera. The net GST payable is $70. The hotel has made a net profit of $700 (ie $2,000 − $900 − $330 − $70).

Casinos and other gambling operators do not need to issue tax invoices for gambling supplies (s 126-33).

Overseas bets A supply made to a gambler who is outside Australia is a GST-free export (s 38-190). This means that wagers received from those gamblers are excluded when working out the GST on the margin when applying the global method. The ATO suggests that a record of the overseas residential address and contact details of the gambler should be kept as the minimum supporting evidence (ATO Betting webpage).

Position of gambler As GST is imposed on the supplier's margin, no GST is paid by a gambler placing a bet or receiving a prize.

For a similar reason, a gambler cannot claim an ITC on the acquisition of gambling supplies (s 126-30).

However, in accordance with the normal rules, a professional gambler, who gambles in the course of carrying on an enterprise, may claim ITCs for other creditable acquisitions it makes.

If the recipient of offshore gambling supplies is an Australian resident (s 126-27), the offshore gambling supplies are connected to Australia (¶4-100), and subject to GST. This ensures that consistent GST treatment applies to all gambling supplies made to Australian residents. (See offshore intangible consumer supply rules applicable from 1 July 2017 (¶9-120).) A bookmaker who lays off a bet to spread the risk of loss is treated in the same way as any other professional gambler.

Lotto syndicates A commercial lotto syndicate that receives fees from members and acquires the tickets in its own name is making taxable gambling supplies for a consideration equal to the full amount of fees received (TSC 2000 Pty Ltd v FC of T 2007 ATC 2409; [2007] AATA 1629).
