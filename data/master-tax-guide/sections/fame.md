# fame ¶10-076

Broadly, sportspersons who are carrying on a business or who earn income from services provided in relation to a sport are assessable on income connected with those activities (TR 1999/17). However, money and other beneﬁts received from the pursuit of a pastime or hobby are not assessable (¶10-050).

Lump sum signing-on fees received by sportspersons, such as professional footballers, are generally treated as ordinary income (IT 2307). However, this will not be so where the fee is consideration for a restrictive covenant, eg to play only for a certain club if the taxpayer were to join another league (Woite 82 ATC 4578; [1982] SASC 6457) (although the fee would probably be income if the taxpayer actually played for that club). In Case R123 84 ATC 791, a lump sum received by a successful professional runner on joining a football club was not income where it was received in return for giving up his career as a runner, although the Commissioner treats this decision as being conﬁned to its own particular facts (IT 2307).

In some cases it has been found that the payment is really for services to be rendered in the taxpayer's employment and, on this basis, the payment has been held to be income (eg Riley v Coglan (1968) 1 All ER 314, Case R107 84 ATC 717). In one case, a payment received by a professional footballer from the Queensland Rugby League as an inducement to play in Queensland rather than in New South Wales (where he could have earned more money) was income (Case Y53 91 ATC 464) (¶10-050). So too were payments made to a football player through a sham trust structure that had been designed to avoid salary cap restrictions (Oliver 2001 ATC 2047; [2001] AATA 155).

Remuneration provided for the commercial exploitation of an individual's fame should be included in that individual's assessable income. TD 2023/4 (replacing Draft TD 2022/D3) was issued on 28 June 2023 to conﬁrm the ATO view that, under an arrangement where an entity (usually a related entity but not necessarily) is allowed to use the famous person's name, image, likeness, identity, reputation and signature (ie their "fame"), and that entity receives fees from third parties for their authorised use of such, the fee will be taken to be derived by the individual and not the entity.

Note that TD 2023/4 is concerned only with income from the use of an individual's fame, which the common law does not recognise as being capable of transfer or assignment. The determination does not apply to income from the provision of services (payment for which would be assessable to the related entity, rather than the individual, subject to the personal services income rules or the general Pt IVA anti-avoidance rules). Signiﬁcantly, however, the determination applies to any individual with fame (eg media personalities, entertainers, actors, artists and social media inﬂuencers, etc, as well as sportspersons). As TD 2023/4 diﬀers in approach from the ATO view previously held and expressed in Draft PCG 2017/D11, the ATO will not apply its revised view to arrangements already entered into on the basis of Draft PCG 2017/D11 for the 2018–19 to 2022–23 income years. The determination otherwise applies to income years before and after its date of issue.

There is an income averaging scheme applicable to sportspersons (¶2-140 to ¶2-144). Where amounts are not income, consideration also needs to be given to the application of the CGT provisions (¶11-280).
