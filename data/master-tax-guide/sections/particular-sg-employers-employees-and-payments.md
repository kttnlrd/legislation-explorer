# Particular SG employers, employees and payments ¶39-024

The Commissioner's views on whether particular categories of persons are employees under the SGAA and on payments for SG purposes include the following:

- A partner in a professional partnership who is a director of a company is not an employee of the

  company if they must pass on any directors' fees received to the partnership; however, where the

  partner's appointment as a director is unrelated to membership of the partnership, the partner is an

  employee of the company notwithstanding any agreement to pay the fees over to another entity or

  person (SGD 97/1).

- If a medical practitioner provides medical services within a practice (clinic) under an arrangement

(eg fee-splitting, management services etc) with the clinic, the totality of the relationship between the parties must be considered to determine whether, on balance, they are an employee or independent contractor (ID 2011/87: medical practitioner neither a common law employee nor employee under any expanded meanings in s 12).

- A person who provides home-based child care is generally not an employee, unless the carer

  provides the child care in the parents' own home (former SGD 94/4 covered arrangements

  which are less common in the current child care environment; most family day care services and

  in-home care programs are now being established as commercial ventures). Basically, the s

12(11) exemption applies to persons who are paid for work principally or wholly of a domestic or private nature for not more than 30 hours per week and the exemption would not apply to carers engaged by labour hire entities to provide child care services in the parent's home (Decision Impact Statement on Newton's case: ¶39-020).

- A sportsperson may be an employee under s 12(1) if their relationship to the payer conforms to

  the indicators and factors that typify a common law employment relationship (as discussed in TR

2023/4: see ¶39-020 – ¶39-022).

If a worker is not an employee under common law principles and an employment relationship does not exist, the speciﬁc provisions in SGAA s 12 which extend the meaning of employee in particular circumstances must also be considered, such as: – s 12(3) dealing with payments to work under a contract that is wholly or principally for the labour of the person (see Dental Corporation Pty Ltd v Moﬀet 20 ESL 07; [2020] FCAFC 118, JMC Pty Ltd 2023 ATC ¶20-861; [2023] FCAFC 76 and "High Court decisions on determination of work relationships" in ¶39-020 and ¶39-022), and – s 12(8) dealing with payments to a person who is paid to perform, present or participate in the performance or presentation of any sport, or where services are provided "in connection with" a sporting activity, or for payments for provision of services in, or in connection with, any television or radio broadcast (¶39-020) (see SGR 2009/1 and "Horse racing club payments" below).

with a sporting activity SGR 2009/1 explains how s 12(8) applies to sportspersons and persons providing services in connection with sporting activities, and whether prize monies and other payments made to sportspersons are "salary or wages" under SGAA s 11(1)(d) (¶39-026) or "ordinary time earnings" under SGAA s 6(1) (¶39-200), or both.

The ruling does not speciﬁcally deal with artists, musicians or other performers who are also referred to in s 12(8) (see above).

Section 12(8) applies on a payment-by-payment basis and the character of the payments received by a sportsperson are determinative of whether that person is treated as an employee of the payer. Each case must be examined on all the facts and circumstances. In this regard, reference must be made to the substance of the arrangement, and not merely by reference to what the parties have agreed to label the payment.

A sportsperson paid "appearance fees" and similar payments to participate in sporting activity is an employee of the payer under the SGAA. However, a sportsperson paid "prize money" would not be an employee of the payer (unless the sportsperson is an employee at common law) because the prize money is not paid for the sportsperson's participation in a sporting activity. Prize money is only payable if a speciﬁc result has been achieved (SGR 2009/1, para 11).

For s 12(8)(b) purposes, services are provided "in connection with" a sporting activity if the services are directly referrable (ie there is a direct causal link to the sporting activity). This will also cover persons providing services required so that the sporting activity can take place, including services provided before, during or after the sporting activity. Hence, s 12(8)(b) will include persons such as umpires, referees and other sporting oﬃcials and technicians who are not already under a common law employment relationship with the relevant payer (SGR 2009/1, para 12).

A sportsperson who is paid to appear on a television or radio broadcast will be an employee of the payer under s 12(8)(c). The terms of s 12(8)(c) do not require that the sportsperson participate or perform in such a broadcast using their physical or personal skills. The term "in connection with" is read in a similarly narrow way as under s 12(8)(b), ie the payment is made for services that are directly related to the making of any ﬁlm, tape or disc or of any television or radio broadcast (SGR 2009/1, para 13).

The SGAA will apply to non-resident sportspersons paid to participate in sports in Australia, unless the payer is exempted under a certiﬁcate of coverage (SGAA s 27(e): ¶39-030). Team oﬃcials and non-resident event organisers may also be excluded from the scope of the SGAA if they are a prescribed employee under SGAR s 11 (SGAA s 27(d): ¶39-030).

Horse racing club payments The ATO has successfully appealed against 2 Federal Court decisions with similar issues concerning the SG liability for payments to jockeys. In the ﬁrst case, the court at ﬁrst instance held that jockeys were not employees of the Racing Queensland Board (RQB) and that the RQB was not liable for unpaid SG charges on various payments made to the jockeys under a Centralised Prizemoney System (CPS), which makes prize money percentage payments and riding fee payments to jockeys on behalf of race clubs for all Queensland race meetings. In the second case, the court held that the Scone Race Club (SRC) was not liable to make SG contributions in relation to riding fees paid by it to jockeys. In upholding the ATO's appeal, the Full Federal Court held that the RQB and SRC were the entities liable to pay riding fees to jockeys for riding in a horse race and therefore were deemed to be their employers for the purposes of the SG legislation (Racing Queensland Board 2019 ATC ¶20-726; [2019] FCAFC 224; Scone Race Club Ltd 2019 ATC ¶20-727; [2019] FCAFC 225; the High Court has denied special leave to appeal). The ATO has stated that both decisions were consistent with its interpretation of s 12(8) that the RQB and SRC were the "employer" of jockeys to whom they had paid riding fees; it did not form the view that they were the "employer" of jockeys under the ordinary meaning of that term, or for any eﬀect other than under the SG legislation.

The vexed issue of payments of fees to jockeys under various riding engagement arrangements and the local rules for jockeys, and in particular whether the jockeys in those arrangements were employees of their racing clubs or employees of the trainers and/or owners under s 12(3) or 12 was examined in Australian Turf Club Ltd 2024 ATC ¶10-732; [2024] AATA 2728. There the AAT, after a comprehensive review of the cases (including Scone's case, see above), held that there was insuﬃcient evidence before it to determine whether s 12(3) applied. As to s 12(8), the AAT concluded that the taxpayer had not suﬃciently discharged its onus of proving, on the balance of probabilities, that it was not liable to pay jockeys for the races conducted by it pursuant to s 12(8). In so deciding, the AAT aﬃrmed the Commissioner's assessments of SG charge imposed on the taxpayer, with an amendment to remove the administrative component that was imposed in error. Note that this matter was heard at the same time as applications by 4 other horse racing clubs (viz, Armidale Jockey Club ([2024] AATA 2726), Clarence River Jockey Club ([2024] AATA 2729), Grenfell Jockey Club Ltd ([2024] AATA 2730) and Illawarra Turf Club ([2024] AATA 2727), including evidence given by those clubs. In each of the other matters, the jockeys were held to be employees of the relevant club.

Employee entitlement scheme payments A person can accrue long service leave credits with diﬀerent employers under a worker entitlement fund (WEF) arrangement where the employers make contributions to the WEF to provide for the person's long service leave. Once the required credits have been accrued, the person is entitled to receive a payment from the WEF. Neither the WEF nor the employer has an obligation to make SG contributions on WEF long service leave payments to the individual. There is no employer–employee relationship between the WEF and the individual receiving the payments, and the employer is not making or liable to make the payments to the individual (ID 2005/33).

The Fair Entitlements Guarantee (FEG) scheme provides assistance to employees who have lost employment due to their employer's liquidation or bankruptcy and are owed certain employee entitlements.

Where the Department of Employment (DE) makes a FEG advance to a former employee in relation to unpaid employee entitlements, the DE pays as a representative of, or in the interest of, the company in liquidation, ie a payment made "on behalf of the employer" within the scope of SGAA s 6(3) even though there is no agency relationship between the DE and the company. This is the case even if the advance is ultimately paid to the employee by a third party (eg an accounting ﬁrm) or the DE makes the payment directly to the employee (SGD 2017/1). The advance to the former employee is "salary or wages" paid by the employer to an employee for the purposes of working out an SG liability (¶39-026) and the employer is the entity with the liability for the SG charge.

employers The government has announced that the FEG Recovery Program will be recalibrated to pursue unpaid superannuation entitlements owed by employers in liquidation or bankruptcy from 1 July 2024 (2024–25 Budget Paper No 2, p 96).

Gig economy activity payments The gig economy comprises short-term, ﬂexible work arrangements organised around the supply of goods or services via a digital platform that connects the workers and payers for the goods or services. The gig economy (sometimes also referred to as the "on-demand economy") is therefore essentially a labour market characterised by the prevalence of short-term work arrangements or freelance work, as opposed to a permanent employer–employee relationship.

The types of services that an individual can provide through a digital platform include:

- delivering goods (eg UberEats, Menulog, Hungry Panda, Loadshift or Sherpa)

- performing tasks and activities for other people that relate to home or private aspects of daily life

  (eg AirTasker, Urban You or Paw Shake).

Whether an individual providing these activities in the gig economy will receive SG contributions depends on the individual's working arrangements with the platform, that is, whether as an employee or a contractor (ATO Fact Sheet The sharing economy and tax).

Scholarship payments Scholarship payments by an Australian university to a student undertaking a relevant course of study (where the scholarships are funded by industry-based organisations) are not subject to the SG scheme. The university has no obligation to make SG contributions on the scholarship payment as there is no employer– employee relationship (ID 2005/53).
