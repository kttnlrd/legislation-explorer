# Penalties for SG breaches ¶39-550

Penalties may be imposed on employers for a breach of their SG obligations. These include:

- an SG charge for entering into arrangements to avoid payment of the charge (SGAA s 30)

- additional SG charge (Part 7 penalty) for failing to provide an SG statement, or statements or

  information for the purposes of assessing the employer's SG liability (SGAA s 59) (see below)

- an administrative penalty or other penalties under the TAA (see below).

  GIC is imposed under the TAA for late payment of the SG charge (see below).

For the purposes of the TAA, an SG charge or additional SG charge is a "tax related liability" (TAA sch 1 s 250-10(2), table items 60 and 65).

Employers may object to an assessment of a Part 7 penalty or TAA administrative penalty in accordance with TAA Pt IVC (SGAA s 42; TAA sch 1 s 298-30(2)) (¶28-010).

Chapter 2 of the Criminal Code (which sets out the general principles of criminal responsibility) applies to all oﬀences against the SGAA.

information SGAA Pt 7 contains the provisions dealing with the imposition of additional SG charge (Part 7 penalty) on an employer who fails to provide, when and as required, an SG statement or information relevant to assessing the employer's liability to pay an SG charge for a quarter, for example:

- where an employer lodges an SG statement for a quarter after the due date (¶39-500), or

- where the ATO makes a default assessment of an employer's liability for the SG charge because

  the employer has not lodged an SG statement for a quarter (¶39-500), and the ATO is of the

  opinion the employer is liable to pay SG charge for the quarter.

An employer who is liable to pay an SG charge for a quarter must also keep records of the calculation of the amounts shown in the SG statement lodged with the ATO (SGAA s 59(2)).

The Part 7 penalty is equal to 200% of the amount of the SG charge payable for the quarter (minimum penalty is $20) (SGAA s 59).

A Part 7 penalty is calculated based on the full amount of the SG charge. In working out the amount of the SG charge payable for a quarter, employer contributions under s 23A which are made to oﬀset the SG charge (¶39-500) are disregarded (SGAA s 62A).

The AAT has jurisdiction to review the assessment of the SG charge and the penalty for failing to provide SG statements but not the late payment penalty or the GIC imposed under s 49 (see below) (Pye 2004 ATC 2029; [2004] AATA 143).

TAA oﬀences and penalties, including administrative penalties, and their machinery provisions are discussed further in Ch 29.

ATO direction to pay SG charge The Commissioner may, by written notice, direct a person to pay an amount of SG charge that is payable or an estimate of the person's liability to pay SG charge for a quarter (as referred to in TAA sch 1 s 268-10) (TAA sch 1 s 265-90). A penalty applies if the amount set out in the Commissioner's direction is not paid by the end of the period speciﬁed in the direction (this is a strict liability oﬀence) (s 265-95).

ATO may issue education directions The Commissioner may issue an education direction to an employer if the Commissioner reasonably believes the employer has failed to comply with its SGAA obligations, such as failing to pay an SG charge liability, to give a statement or information as required, or to keep records (TAA sch 1 s 384-10). The direction will require the employer to ensure that an individual (or an individual who makes, or participates in making, decisions aﬀecting the employer's business such as a company director) undertakes a speciﬁed approved course of education within a reasonable time and provides the Commissioner with evidence of completion of the course. An administrative penalty applies for non-compliance (this is an oﬀence against TAA s 8C, an absolute liability oﬀence).

*Refs: s 284-75(3)), s 288-25), s 268-10), s 265-90), s 265-95), s 384-10)*

penalty The Commissioner may remit all or a part of a Part 7 penalty and TAA administrative penalty, and may do so as part of the assessment of the penalty (an audit decision) or after the penalty is assessed (an objection decision) (SGAA s 62(3)–(5); TAA sch 1 s 298-20) (Weston 2008 ATC ¶10-052; [2008] AATA 869, DB Mahaﬀy & Associates 11 ESL 24; [2011] AATA 796, RFZD 2022 ATC ¶10-629; [2022] AATA 988, appeal dismissed at [2023] FCA 324, Burswood Care Pty Ltd ATF Roshana Family Trust 2023 ATC ¶10-668; [2023] AATA 1468, Delbake Pty Ltd 2024 ATC ¶10-716; [2024] AATA 449: exceptional circumstances for failure to lodge SG statement and Commissioner's power to remit Part 7 penalty).

PS LA 2021/3 sets out guidelines on what the ATO will consider in making a decision to remit a Part 7 penalty, when relief is appropriate, and how a Part 7 penalty interacts with other administrative penalties (see Appendix 1 for the 4-step penalty remission process). The guidelines set out an approach to remission that recognises the full range of SG compliance behaviours, and diﬀerentiates between taxpayers with a good compliance history and those who repeatedly make late or fail to make SG contributions.

GIC for late payment of SG charge If an amount of SG charge is not paid or not paid in full by its due date (¶39-500), the employer is liable to pay GIC (¶29-510) on the unpaid amount (SGAA s 49). This is so even if the Commissioner has extended the date for payment of the charge.

The GIC is deductible under ITAA97 s 25-5(1) (¶16-850).

Director penalty regime applies to unpaid SG amounts The director penalty regime in TAA sch 1 Div 269 (¶25-560) applies to cover SG amounts as follows:

- directors may be made personally liable for their company's unpaid SG amounts

- directors cannot discharge their director penalties by placing their company into administration or

  liquidation when PAYG withholding or SG amounts remain unpaid and unreported 3 months after

  the due date, and

- directors and their associates may be liable to PAYG withholding non-compliance tax (eﬀectively

  reducing credit entitlements) where the company has failed to pay amounts withheld to the

Commissioner (DFC of T v Lawson 2017 ATC ¶20-644; [2017] VSC 789: directors penalty notices validly served on the director of a company which had failed to pay SG charge).

Brieﬂy, company directors are responsible for ensuring a company fulﬁls its obligations to pay its SG charge liability (and PAYG withholding tax and GST liabilities) on time and in full. If a company fails to meet these obligations, the ATO will issue a director penalty notice making the directors become personally liable for a penalty equal to any outstanding and overdue amounts.

In relation to the SG liability, the director penalty represents the amount of the company's SG charge as assessed, either by the employer through the lodgment of an SG statement or by the Commissioner through a default assessment (¶39-500) or a notice of an estimate issued by the Commissioner (TAA sch 1 ss 268-10(1), (3); 269-10(1), table items 4 and 5). Any amount collected under the director penalty regime that represents the SG charge is dealt with in the same way as SG charge amounts collected under the SGAA (¶39-600) (SGAA s 63A(3)).

Practitioner article Have tax debts? Beware Director Penalty Notices (DPNs) provides practical insights, including the diﬀerence between lockdown and non-lockdown DPNs, and defences available to directors (ATW Issue 41, 10 October 2024).

Employee's recourse for non-compliance with SG obligations The ATO has no enforceable legal duty under the SGAA to recover alleged unpaid or underpaid employer superannuation contributions on behalf of an employee.

In Kronen 13 ESL 05; [2013] FCA 416, the employee (taxpayer) had a disputed claim with his employer about unpaid superannuation. The Federal Court held that there was no prospect of the taxpayer successfully arguing that he had, in the circumstances, an enforceable legal right to require the ATO to take action under either SGAA s 36 or 37 (ATO default assessments or amended assessments: ¶39-500), or under any other SGAA provision, to compel the employer to make the additional contributions alleged to be payable, or that the ATO had in the circumstances a corresponding legal duty to do so.

An ATO Fact Sheet provides guidelines on what employees can do if they think that their employers have not paid their full superannuation entitlements, eg by reporting the employer using the ATO's online tool or making a claim for unpaid superannuation under employment laws (see below). If an employee chooses to report the employer, the ATO will keep the employee updated on its investigation and any recovery action that it might take.

From 1 January 2024, an employee's entitlement to superannuation is a national employment standard in the Fair Work Act 2009 (FW Act) for most employees, and this provides employees with certain recourse under the FW Act against employers for any unpaid or underpaid compulsory superannuation as outlined below.

268-10(1)*

agreements Modern awards must contain a term that requires employers to make suﬃcient contributions to a superannuation fund for the beneﬁt of an employee covered by the award to avoid their liability to pay the SG charge (FW Act s 149B). Enterprise agreements may also contain terms relating to superannuation.

Industrial instruments with clauses about superannuation contributions for employees give aﬀected employees a right of action against their employer, under the terms of their employment contract or industrial agreement, to seek relief under civil remedy provisions under the FW Act (eg ss 539(2); 540; 545(1), (2)(a) and (b)). Such action will arise if the employer does not comply with obligations to pay superannuation where the prescribed contribution amounts in the contract or agreement are more than the minimum contributions required under the SGAA (BlueScope Steel (AIS) Pty Ltd v Australian Workers' Union 19 ESL 04; [2019] FCAFC 84; ATO Decision Impact Statement).

national employment standard The FW Act sets out National Employment Standards (NES) on the minimum standards applying to employment of employees about their entitlements, such as the maximum weekly hours of work, leave entitlements, notice of termination and redundancy (FW Act s 61). All employees in the national workplace relations system are covered by the NES regardless of the award, registered agreement or employment contract that applies to the employee, but certain standards do not apply to casual employees. Brieﬂy, the terms in awards, registered agreements and employment contracts cannot exclude the NES or provide for an entitlement that is less than the NES, but can supplement the NES by providing more favourable entitlements. Penalties apply for a contravention of the NES under the FW Act.

From 1 January 2024, FW Act Pt 2-2 Div 10A (ss 116A to 116E) provides an entitlement to superannuation contributions as a NES (FW Act s 61(2)(ha)). While superannuation underpayment issues are primarily the ATO's responsibility under the SGAA, the superannuation NES will provide employees, unions and the Fair Work Ombudsman with legal standing under the FW Act to directly pursue any unpaid or underpaid compulsory superannuation.

Division 10A does not apply to an employee or employer who is only a "national system employee" or "national system employer" because of FW Act ss 30C, 30D, 30M or 30N (which extend the meaning of these expressions in the FW Act) (s 116A). Therefore, Div 10A would not apply to all employers that are liable to pay the SG charge because the expanded meaning of employee and employer in SGAA s 12 is broader than the meaning of national system employer and national system employee in the FW Act (the Tax and superannuation page on the Fair Work Ombudsman's website: excluded employees).

Section 116B expressly requires an employer to make contributions to a superannuation fund for the beneﬁt of an employee so as to avoid liability to pay the SG charge in relation to the employee. This is a separate obligation to the SGAA regime and the ATO's broad regulatory powers to recover SG charge amounts from employers who have SG shortfalls. The s 116B obligation eﬀectively establishes the mechanism through which a broader range of employees (or an employee organisation or the Fair Work Ombudsman on their behalf) can enforce and recover underpaid or unpaid superannuation under the FW Act. This would particularly beneﬁt award and agreement-free employees and employees covered by enterprise agreements which do not contain a term providing a right to superannuation. It also reinforces the government's position that underpayment of superannuation is a form of wage theft and worker exploitation. Note that wage and superannuation theft has been prescribed as a criminal oﬀence under FW Act s 327A from the later of 1 January 2025 and the day after the ﬁrst time a "Voluntary Small Business Wage Compliance Code" is declared under FW Act s 327B.

Section 116B cannot be used to recover superannuation contributions above the SG charge percentage (¶39-100). For example, if an enterprise agreement provides that an employer must make superannuation contributions for employees based on 13% of OTE, an employee who alleges that no SG contribution has been made on their behalf can only use the NES to recover superannuation contributions at the deﬁned SG charge percentage. An employee who wishes to recover the full superannuation entitlement speciﬁed in the enterprise agreement can only do so by commencing an action for breach of the superannuation term in the enterprise agreement pursuant to FW Act s 50. This is consistent with other instances where awards or enterprise agreements provide conditions that are superior to the NES entitlements.

Under the SGAA regime, employers are entitled to either reduce the SG charge percentage which applies to assess their SG charge liability by making the required SG contributions to complying superannuation funds for the beneﬁt of their employees, or pay the SG charge as assessed by the ATO for any given quarter (SGAA s 19). Section 116C provides for a reduction of an employer's liability to the extent of any SG charge payments made by clarifying that s 116B is not contravened simply because an employer has elected to pay the SG charge with respect to an employee. Therefore, if an employer has paid only part of its SG charge liability as assessed by the ATO, s 116C will commensurately reduce, but not entirely resolve, the employer's liability to pay any outstanding amounts required to be paid under s 116B.

Section 116D applies to prevent an employee, or another person with standing, from commencing proceedings for an alleged contravention of Div 10A if the Commissioner has instituted proceedings to recover SG charge amounts calculated on an SG shortfall that overlaps with the person's claim for unpaid superannuation under Div 10A. Section 116D thus prevents an employer from being subject to multiple legal proceedings, under diﬀerent Commonwealth legislation, for eﬀectively the same unpaid superannuation.

However, s 116D does not prevent a person from commencing action for an alleged contravention of Div 10A if the Commissioner has engaged in other enforcement activity which does not involve court proceedings with respect to the same unpaid superannuation, or has discontinued court proceedings without obtaining a ﬁnal order for recovery.

Section 116E applies if a court makes an order under s 545 of the FW Act awarding compensation to an employee for a contravention of a civil remedy provision that relates to a contravention of Div 10A. Under s 545, the Federal Court may make any orders they consider appropriate if the court is satisﬁed that a civil remedy provision of the FW Act has been contravened (eg a NES). Section 116E provides that, when deciding the orders it may make pursuant to s 545, the court must have regard to:

- any relevant administrative guidance produced by the ATO regarding the interpretation of

  superannuation obligations on which the employer has relied (although such guidance is not legally

  binding), and

- such material when determining whether it would be appropriate to impose a pecuniary penalty

  order for the contravention pursuant to s 546 of the FW Act.
