# Crown instrumentalities immune from tax ¶1-080

Section 114 of the Constitution prohibits the Commonwealth from imposing any tax on property, of any kind, belonging to a state, while ITAA97 speciﬁcally exempts from income tax the revenue from municipal corporations and other public authorities. The scope of s 114 raises a number of issues that continue to be tested in the courts, including the meaning of a "state", and whether a particular tax is a "tax on property".

The former issue was considered in SGH Ltd 2002 ATC 4366; [2002] HCA 18, where the High Court held that SGH, a building society controlled by a state instrumentality, was not the state of Queensland for tax purposes. The court said that it was relevant to consider the activities undertaken by the entity as well as the legal relationship between it and the executive government. If the entity is wholly owned by the state concerned, and must act solely in the interests of the state, the conclusion that it is the state or an emanation of the state will readily follow (State Bank of NSW (1992) 174 CLR 235; 92 ATC 4079).

Whether the application of particular legislation is a "tax on property" has been considered on multiple occasions, most recently in Hornsby Shire Council v Cth 2023 ATC ¶20-868; [2023] HCA 19. In Hornsby, the Full High Court unanimously determined that a notional GST payment made by a local government council pursuant to provisions of Commonwealth legislation (enacted as part of the implementation of the intergovernmental agreements between the Commonwealth and all states and territories in respect of the redistribution of GST) was not a tax on state property. In State of Qld v Cth 87 ATC 4029; [1987] HCA 2 a majority of the High Court rejected a challenge by the state of Queensland to the imposition of FBT on states, concluding that FBT was not a tax on the property of the state but on transactions that aﬀected that property.

However, a challenge by South Australia succeeded in State of SA v Cth 92 ATC 4066; [1992] HCA 7, where the High Court found that a state superannuation fund was exempt from CGT (but not from ordinary income tax) under s 114 because CGT is a tax on property.

In Austin v Cth 2003 ATC 4042; [2003] HCA 3 the High Court declared the federal superannuation contributions tax (SCT) to be invalid in its application to a NSW Supreme Court judge on the ground that it placed a particular disability or burden on the operations or activities of the state of NSW so as to be beyond the legislative powers of the Commonwealth. In rejecting the Commonwealth's argument that the treatment in special legislation of constitutionally protected funds was dictated by the operation of the Constitution itself, the High Court said that a federal law cannot be justiﬁed on the basis that it is an indirect means of achieving what would otherwise be prohibited by s 114. In Rogers 2021 ATC ¶10-601; [2021] AATA 4478 the AAT held that a Senior Crown Prosecutor was a "statutory oﬃce holder of equivalent seniority" to the head of a government department, instrumentality or agency and thus was a constitutionally protected state higher level oﬃce holder for the purposes of Subdiv 293-E of ITAA97 (which was the Commonwealth's statutory attempt to deal with the superannuation arrangements of senior state oﬃcials in the wake of Austin).

Commonwealth bodies have had mixed results when they sought to use s 114 as protection from state taxes.

In Superannuation Fund Investment Trust v Commr of Stamps (SA) (1979) 145 CLR 330; 79 ATC 4429, the High Court refused protection against South Australian stamp duty. In contrast, in Allders International v Commr of State Revenue (Vic) (1996) 186 CLR 630; 96 ATC 5135, the High Court invalidated Victorian legislation imposing stamp duty on a lease of a duty-free store in a federal airport (under s 52(i) of the Constitution, the federal government has exclusive power to legislate with respect to "Commonwealth places").
