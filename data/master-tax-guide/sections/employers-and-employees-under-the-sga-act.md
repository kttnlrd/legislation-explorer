# Employers and employees under the SGA Act ¶39-020

The SG scheme applies to employers in respect of their full-time, part-time and casual employees, with only limited exemptions (¶39-030).

The terms "employer" and "employee" in the SGAA have their ordinary common law meaning (¶26-150, ¶39-022), as well as an expanded meaning in SGAA s 12(2) to (10) (see below).

Certain workers who are otherwise employees are regarded not to be employees under the SGAA (see "Persons who are not employees" below).

If 2 contracts of employment exist simultaneously between an employer and an employee, the employer will have an obligation under each contract to make SG contributions for the employee (ID 2006/321).

Taxation Ruling TR 2023/4 explains when an individual is an "employee" of an entity for the purposes of TAA sch 1 s 12-35 (which imposes an obligation on a paying entity to withhold an amount from salary, wages, commission, bonuses or allowances it pays to an employee, whether or not the paying entity is the employer). Appendix 2 to the Ruling updates the Commissioner's views in former SGR 2005/1 (withdrawn with eﬀect from 26 June 2024) on who is an employee under SGAA s 12 in light of the High Court decisions on employment relationships, particularly Personnel Contracting and Jamsek (see "High Court decisions on determination of work relationships" in ¶39-022). Appendix 2 is not legally binding on the Commissioner.

However, if the Commissioner later takes the view that s 12 applies less favourably to a taxpayer than Appendix 2 indicates, the fact that the taxpayer acted in accordance with the Appendix 2 will be a relevant factor in the taxpayer's favour in the Commissioner's exercise of any discretion on the imposition of SG penalties.

The application of SGAA s 12 has also been the subject of a signiﬁcant amount of judicial consideration (see "Employer–employee and other contractual relationships" in ¶39-022).

Expanded meaning of employer and employee in the SGAA In general terms, an employee is a person who receives payment in the form of "salary or wages" (¶39-026) in return for work or services rendered, or payment for work under a contract that is wholly or principally for the person's labour, and the person liable to make the payment is the employer.

Under its expanded meaning in SGAA s 12(2)–(10), a person is an "employee" or "employer" in the following circumstances.

The Commissioner's guidelines on each of the categories of deemed employees under s 12(2) to (10) are found in TR 2023/4 Appendix 2 paras 88 to 135. The Ruling states that in determining if a person is an employee under s 12(2) to (10), consideration of whether the personal services provided were done so in an "employment-like setting" is not relevant. This employment-like setting concept originates from Bromberg J in On Call Interpreters and Translators Agency Pty Ltd 2011 ATC ¶20-258; [2011] FCA 366) where His Honour commented in obiter that s 12(2) to (10) were intended to cover "… persons who may not be common law employees but who earn remuneration in exchange for the provision of personal services in the context of an employment like-setting." The Full Federal Court has said that Bromberg J's comments had no textual anchor to s 12 and constituted a gloss on the provision, such that s 12(2) to (10) are not limited to instances where a person provides personal services in what appears to be an employment-like setting, which is not of a domestic or private nature (Dental Corporation Pty Ltd v Moﬀet 20 ESL 07; [2020] FCAFC 118) (paras 142–145).

"employee" and "employer" The Full Federal Court has stated it was apparent that s 12(2)–(11) expand both terms "employee" and "employer" for 2 reasons. First, s 12(1)(a) identiﬁes that subsections (2)–(11) expand "those terms", referring to both "employee" and "employer". Given the word "employer" does not appear in any of subsections (2) to (11), the plural terms require that "employer" is coordinately expanded in meaning to "employee". Second, subsections (2)–(11) would be otiose if they did not expand the term "employer". The necessary implication of the terms of the subsections is that the term "employee" is expanded, and the person with whom they have a relationship as identiﬁed in each subsection is their "employer" (Racing Queensland Board 2019 ATC ¶20-726; [2019] FCAFC 224).

The expression domestic or private nature in s 12(11) was examined by the Federal Court in Newton 2010 ATC ¶20-234, 10 ESL 26; [2010] FCA 1440, an appeal from Care Provider 10 ESL 11; [2010] AATA 475.

The Commissioner had submitted that s 12(11) was not intended to relieve a labour hire business, or other businesses, that provides domestic services for clients, from an SG obligation and that the words of s 12(11) suggested that it was concerned with work of a domestic or private nature only from the payer's perspective.

Essentially, the phrase "work of a domestic or private nature" is a composite one. While some work done in a boarding school, hotel or hospital or in a retirement village might be characterised as domestic in some sense, it could not fairly be characterised as being of a domestic or private nature in the context of the SGAA. The Court agreed that the language of s 12(11) was by no means clear. In summary, whether s 12(11) applies requires an examination of the following — the nature of the work carried out by the worker, the identity or attributes of the payer, the relationship between the payer and worker, or the nature of the work or services that the worker provides to the payer. The AAT, on remittal of the case, found strong indications that the workers were employees pursuant to s 12(3) (ie persons engaged for their labour), and probably were also employees under its common law meaning in s 12(1) (Newton (T/A as Combined Care for the Elderly) 2011 ATC ¶10-226; [2011] AATA 897).

In TR 2023/4, the Commissioner states that the exemption under s 12(11) is not available to a business that pays a worker to do work of a domestic nature, so far as the end-user or client is concerned. For example, while some work done in a school, hotel, hospital or in a retirement village might be characterised as domestic, it cannot be characterised as being of a domestic or private nature in the context of the SGAA.

Whether a person carrying out such work is an employee for SG purposes will depend on whether they fall within the other subsections of s 12 (para 141).

Partnerships, unincorporated associations and trusts At common law, a partnership (except an incorporated limited partnership) is not a legal entity separate and distinct from its members (Rose [1951] HCA 68). An "Incorporated limited partnership" is a body corporate with a separate legal personality from the partners (eg Partnerships Act 1958 (Vic) s 84).

The SGAA applies as if a partnership or an unincorporated association were a legal person and speciﬁc provisions clarify their status for SG purposes (SGAA ss 72 and 73).

A partner in a partnership cannot be an employee of the partnership. In particular, the degree of control over an individual required for the individual to be an employee at common law is incompatible with the degree of independence that a partner has in relation to the conduct of the partnership.

As a partnership is treated as if it were a separate legal person under s 72, a person can be an employee of the partnership, rather than an employee of the individual partners themselves. Whether an individual (other than a partner) who is engaged by a partnership to perform work for the partnership is an employee of the partnership depends on the circumstances of the contractual arrangement (¶39-022).

An agreement that enables a partner to draw a salary does not create an employer/employee relationship but operates simply to vary the sharing of partnership proﬁts between the parties.

If a partnership has contracted to provide services, then the person who actually does the work is not the employee of the other party to the contract. This is so even if the person is a partner and even if the contract requires the partner to do the work. However, if an individual partner contracts outside the partnership in their own personal capacity to provide their labour to fulﬁl a contractual obligation, the individual partner can be an employee of the other party to the contract (TR 2023/4 paras 146–148).

The Full Federal Court has held that s 72 renders a partnership "a legal person" for the purpose of treating it as being itself party to a contract as putative "employer" under s 12(3). However, s 72 does not operate to deem a partnership to be "a legal person" for the purposes of being treated as a putative employee under s 12(3). This is because only an identiﬁed natural person, and not a partnership, could be an "employee" under SGAA s 12(2)–(11) (Jamsek & Ors v ZG Operations Australia Pty Ltd & Ors (No 3) 2023 ATC ¶20-858; [2023] FCAFC 48) (¶39-022).

A trust is not a legal entity (¶6-010). At common law, a trustee cannot be an employee of the trust as that would require the trustee to enter into a contract of service with himself/herself. However, in a particular case, there may be an employment relationship between the trust (ie the trustees of the trust) and individual trustees (in their capacity as workers who are receiving remuneration for their services) and, in those circumstances, the trustees of the trust (as the employer) have an obligation to make SG contributions for themselves as employees (Christie 2005 ATC 2009; [2004] AATA 1396, Trustee for Kitchen Unit Trust 2023 ATC ¶10-683; [2023] AATA 2831: ¶39-022).
