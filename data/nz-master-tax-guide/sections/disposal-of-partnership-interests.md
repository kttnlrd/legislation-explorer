# Disposal of partnership interests ¶23-050

Under the flow-through provision, each partner is deemed to hold partnership assets directly in proportion to their respective partnership share. Thus, a disposal of an interest in a partnership is treated as a disposal of the partner's share in the underlying assets of the partnership and the normal tax consequences on the sale of assets will result. However, except where the disposal is by way of a final partnership dissolution (ss HG 3(1) and HG 4(1)), ss HG 5–HG 10 provide a number of exemptions from the general rule (¶23-045).

*Refs: ss HG 3(1), ss HG 5*

## General de minimis exemption — s HG 5

A general de minimis exemption applies when a partner (referred to as the "exiting partner") disposes of some or all of their partner's interests in a partnership. Where the net gain on disposal of the partner's interest is less than $50,000, the purchaser (or "entering partner") will "step into the shoes" of the exiting partner. (The entering partner can include a person who is already a partner in the partnership.) The ability to "step into the shoes" of the exiting partner essentially removes any tax consequences to both the entering and exiting partners. Where the net gain is greater than $50,000, the exiting partner must account for tax on the proceeds of disposal.

The following formula is used to determine whether the exiting partner must account for tax on disposal of their partner's interest: disposal payment + previous payments # (gross tax value # liabilities)  $50,000 where, generally: disposal payment is the total consideration paid or payable to the exiting partner for the current interests (including any capital contribution that an entering partner agrees to as consideration for some of the current interests of the exiting partner, see IS 25/11 at [191]) previous payment is the total consideration paid or payable to the exiting partner for other disposals of the partner's interests in the year prior to the disposal of the current interests gross tax value is the total of: (i) the value of the current interests and other interests at the time the relevant interest is disposed of, to the extent that the interests are revenue account property or depreciable property, and (ii) the market value of the current interests and other interests at the time the relevant interest is disposed of, to the extent that the interests are not revenue account property or depreciable property liabilities is the amount of any liabilities at the time the interest is disposed of.

If the formula produces a result:

## less than zero — the disposal payment is excluded income of the exiting partner and the exiting

partner is not required to account for tax (s CX 62)

## greater than or equal to zero — the exiting partner is required to account for tax on any gain or loss

on disposal of the interest.

See Example 8 in IS 25/11 demonstrating the application of the de minimis rule.

QWBA QB 14/02: "Income tax — entry of a new partner into a partnership — effect on continuing partners", discusses the tax implications for continuing partners in a partnership when a partner sells their interest in a partnership. Inland Revenue (IR) considers that when a new partner simply "steps into the shoes" of an exiting partner and the continuing partners' interests in the assets of the partnership have not been changed, then no tax consequences should arise. However, there will be a taxable event for continuing partners if the addition of the new partner changes the continuing partners' interests in the partnership assets. In that case, the provisions of ss HG 5–HG 10 will need to be applied.

## Specific exemptions

A number of specific exemptions apply to the disposal of certain items of partnership property.

## Trading stock — s HG 6

An exiting partner will not be required to account for tax on the disposal of trading stock if total turnover of the partnership is $3 million or less in the income year of disposal. The entering partner will then step into the shoes of the exiting partner and take on the exiting partner's cost base for the trading stock. This applies even if the entering partner has paid market value for the stock, which could be more or less than the exiting partner's cost base.

## Depreciable property — s HG 7

An exiting partner will also not be required to account for tax on disposal of an item of depreciable property with an original cost of $200,000 or less. The exiting partner will not be entitled to claim any loss on disposal of the asset. The entering partner will not be entitled to any deduction in respect of the purchase price paid (ie the entering partner must take on the exiting partner's cost base regardless of the price paid).

However, the exiting partner will be entitled to a depreciation deduction in relation to the property up until the time of disposal. The entering partner will be entitled to depreciation deductions from the date of disposal.

The amount of deduction allocated to each partner depends on their share in partnership's income.

## Financial arrangements — s HG 8

An exiting partner will not have to account for tax on the disposal of a partner's interest to the extent that the interest includes a financial arrangement (or excepted financial arrangement) that satisfies the following criteria:

## the financial arrangement (or excepted financial arrangement) was entered into for purposes

necessary and incidental to the business of the partnership, and

## the partnership does not derive income from a business of holding financial arrangements.

Where these conditions are satisfied, the exiting partner will not be required to perform a base price adjustment on disposal of the financial arrangement. Any consideration received by the exiting partner will be excluded income and the entering partner will not be entitled to any deduction in respect of their purchase price.

As with the depreciable property exemption, the entering partner will be treated as stepping into the shoes of the exiting partner from the date of disposal. If the change in partnership interest occurs part way through an income year, an apportionment of income derived from the financial arrangement may be required between the entering and exiting partners in the year of disposal.

In this regard it is interesting to note that s HG 8(3) appears to prevent the exiting partner from claiming any deduction in relation to the financial arrangement (ie no apportionment between the entering and exiting partners appears to be contemplated in the case of deductions). In contrast the equivalent section of the depreciable property exemption only prohibits the exiting partner from claiming a deduction to which the entering partner is entitled.

## Short-term agreements for sale and purchase — s HG 9

Any disposal of a partner's interests comprising short-term agreements for sale and purchase (generally for property or services under which settlement must take place on or before the 93rd day after the agreement is entered into (s YA 1)) will fall outside the general flow-through rule. No de minimis threshold applies to such disposals. The entering partner will take on the exiting partner's cost base for the agreement (regardless of the actual purchase price paid) and will not be entitled to a deduction for the purchase price paid. The exiting partner will not be assessed on income from the sale.

## Livestock — s HG 10

The disposal of a partner's interests comprising certain specified livestock falls outside the general flow- through rule where the partners use the national standard cost scheme for specified livestock (s EC 22) or the cost price method for specified livestock (s EC 25).

Sections HG 10(2) and EC 26B provide that an entering partner may elect to spread any difference between the price they paid for specified livestock and the partnership's base cost when the partnership has breeding livestock. See Tax Information Bulletin ¶218-101 Vol 21, No 8, October/November 2009, Pt II at 152. The election is made by the entering partner furnishing a return of income that applies s HG 10 (s HG 3(3)).

## Small partnerships — s HG 3(2)

Small partnerships can elect to opt out of the specific exemptions from tax on disposal contained in ss HG 5–HG 9. A "small partnership" is defined as "a partnership that is not a limited partnership and has 5 or less partners, none of whom are companies or partnerships".

Where a small partnership chooses to opt out of the disposal safe harbours, the exiting partner is then required to account for tax on the disposal of their partner's interests. This election can be made by filing a tax return that ignores the effect of the relevant sections.

Example: Norah and her five children Sarah, Tommy, Kitty, Kevin and Justin operate a family business — The Walker Family Partnership — that manufactures and sells widgets.

Kitty decides to sell her share of the partnership to her Uncle Saul for $195,000. The partnership assets at the time of sale consist of trading stock and a building. Kitty's gain on the sale of her share is $55,000 calculated as follows:

## Building             Trading stock                          Total

Cost $900,000 $90,000 $990,000 Tax book value $750,000 $90,000 $840,000 Market value $1,050,000 $120,000 $1,170,000

Kitty's share of cost (1/6th) $150,000 $15,000 $165,000 Kitty's sale proceeds $175,000 $20,000 $195,000 less Kitty's share of tax book value (1/6th) $125,000 $15,000 $140,000 Kitty's gain on sale $50,000 $5,000 $55,000

Kitty's taxable gain $25,000 n/a $25,000 Kitty's capital gain $25,000 n/a $25,000

In the year of disposal, the partnership's turnover was $2m.

As Kitty's gain on disposal of her partner's interest is greater than $50,000, the general de minimis exemption in s HG 5 will not apply. However, the specific exemptions may still apply and will need to be considered in relation to each type of partnership asset disposed of.

As the building has a historical cost of more than $200,000, the depreciable property exemption will not apply. Kitty will therefore be required to pay tax on $25,000 being the taxable gain on sale of the building. Upon Uncle Saul's entry into the partnership, Uncle Saul's cost base will equal the portion of the purchase price attributable to the building.

As the partnership's turnover is less than $3m, the trading stock exemption in s HG 6 will apply. This means that Kitty will not be required to account for tax on the disposal of trading stock. Instead, effectively Uncle Saul will step into Kitty's shoes and acquire Kitty's interest in the trading stock. However, the effect of this is that Uncle Saul will acquire a cost base equal to Kitty's cost base in the trading stock even if he has paid a price based on market value that is higher than Kitty's cost base.

This example illustrates that as new partners enter a partnership the cost base of each partner's interest will change. Each partner may have a different cost base of assets in the partnership, which will not necessarily correlate with their respective partnership shares. It is therefore important to track each partner's cost base of their partnership assets.
