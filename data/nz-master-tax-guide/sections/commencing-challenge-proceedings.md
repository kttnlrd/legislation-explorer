# Commencing challenge proceedings ¶4-225

A taxpayer who disagrees with an assessment issued following the disputes process may challenge that decision by filing proceedings with the TRA or the High Court, provided that:

## The assessment includes an adjustment proposed by the Commissioner that the taxpayer, or the

taxpayer's look-through company (LTC), has rejected within the 2-month response period or was the subject of an adjustment proposed by the taxpayer that the Commissioner has rejected within the response period.

## The proceedings are filed with the TRA or the High Court within 2 months of the date of assessment

or determination. If the taxpayer issued a NOPA and the Commissioner decided not to amend the assessment, proceedings must be issued within 2 months of the date that the taxpayer was notified of the decision. The beginning of the 2-month period in which the taxpayer must file proceedings is not limited to the date of the Commissioner's NOP.

A disputant is also entitled to challenge a disputable decision that is not an assessment by commencing proceedings in the TRA or the High Court if:

## the disputable decision was the subject of an adjustment proposed by the disputant that the

Commissioner has rejected, and

## proceedings are filed within the 2-month response period for the notice from the Commissioner

rejecting the proposed adjustment.

## Non-disputable decisions

Not all decisions taken by the Commissioner are disputable decisions and subject to challenge. A decision to apply one of the exceptions to the time bar provisions and "re-open" tax assessments is not a disputable decision in the absence of an assessment by the Commissioner: Vinelight Nominees Ltd v CIR (No 2) (2005) 22 NZTC 19,519 (HC).

Other decisions that are not disputable decisions are decisions:

## to decline to issue a binding ruling

## that cannot be the subject of an objection or a challenge

## to issue a Commissioner's NOPA under s 89B, a Commissioner's disclosure notice or SOP under s

89M, or a challenge notice

## to issue, or to decline to issue, a Commissioner's COVID-19 response variation under s 6I

## to make, or to decline to make, a grant under the cost of living payments scheme under s 7AAA

## to grant, or to decline to grant, a loan under the small business cashflow scheme under s 7AA, or

## to make, or decline to make, a grant under the COVID-19 resurgence support payments scheme

under s 7AAB.

In Case W16 (2003) 21 NZTC 11,156, the Commissioner argued that the taxpayer had failed to comply with s 138B of the TAA by not giving the Commissioner a NOPA within the 2-month period required. The Authority rejected this argument, and the Commissioner applied for a judicial review of the TRA decision in C of IR v Taxation Review Authority (2004) 21 NZTC 18,634. The High Court held:

## an exchange of a NOPA and a NOR was essential to achieving the purposes of the dispute resolution

process (this had to be done before a taxpayer could bring a challenge proceeding)

## the wording of s 138B(1) suggests that an adjustment proposed and rejected before the issuing of an

assessment is a separate and tentative step, and

## it is the assessment, once dispute rights are exhausted or have expired, which is final.

In Case X2 (2005) 22 NZTC 12,056 the TRA held that a taxpayer could not proceed with challenge proceedings because the taxpayer's NOPA was filed out of time. Further, the Authority agreed with the

Commissioner that, with the introduction of self-assessment, the response period was triggered by the taxpayer's own self-assessment.

In C of IR v Taxation Review Authority (2004) 21 NZTC 18,707, the High Court held that s 138B applies to default assessments. If this were not the case, a defaulting taxpayer would be placed at an advantage over other taxpayers who had filed a return. This decision was affirmed in the Court of Appeal in Allen v C of IR (2005) 22 NZTC 19,473. The Supreme Court affirmed the decision of the High Court and noted that there was no general obligation to dispute an assessment. However, if a taxpayer wished to dispute an assessment, the disputes procedures must be complied with and that involved the filing of a return and the issuing of a NOPA (Allen v C of IR (2006) 22 NZTC 19,827).

In C of IR v Zentrum Holdings Ltd (2006) 22 NZTC 19,912, the Court of Appeal held that, where no disclosure notice has been issued, there was no rule expressly limiting an objector on appeal to the grounds stated in the objection. However, where a disclosure notice has been issued and the taxpayer subsequently challenges the assessment, the matters in issue are limited to the legal and factual matters identified in the statements of position, but all of those issues are legitimate matters of dispute in the challenge proceedings.

In Beckham v C of IR (2007) 23 NZTC 21,499, it was held that the TRA could assess an appellant on different grounds to those on which a challenged assessment was based, as the different grounds formed part of the "subject matter" of the disputable decision being challenged.

## Exceptional circumstances

If the TRA or the High Court considers that exceptional circumstances have prevented a taxpayer or their agent from issuing proceedings within the 2-month period, the taxpayer or agent will be allowed to start a challenge. An exceptional circumstance is defined as an event or circumstance beyond the taxpayer's control that provides the taxpayer with a reasonable justification for not complying with the time limit. An act or omission of the taxpayer's agent is not an exceptional circumstance unless it was caused by events beyond the agent's control that could not be anticipated, and the effect of which could not have been avoided if the accepted standards of business organisation and professional conduct were followed.

Example: The taxpayer's application for leave to commence proceedings 5 months out of time because of a misunderstanding and erroneous assumption by the taxpayer's agent was held not to constitute "exceptional circumstances" that were "beyond the control of the applicant" in terms of s 138D of the TAA. See Milburn New Zealand Ltd v C of IR (1998) 18 NZTC 14,005. Also see Case V1 (2001) 20 NZTC 10,001.

Example: Exceptional circumstances justified the taxpayer's agent not having commenced challenge proceedings within the designated response period. Several actions taken by IR were found to have led to confusion and could not have been anticipated by an agent complying with accepted standards of business organisation and business conduct. See Treasury Technology Holdings Ltd v C of IR (1998) 18 NZTC 13,752. Compare this with C of IR v Fuji Xerox NZ Ltd (2002) 20 NZTC 17,470 (CA). This case also interprets the term "agent" in s 138D of the TAA.

See also Case U35 (2000) 19 NZTC 9,330 for a discussion of exceptional circumstances in terms of reg 25(2) of the Taxation Review Authority Regulations 1998.

## Shortened process for taxpayer-initiated disputes

In limited circumstances the disputes process can be shortened (also referred to as being truncated) and can proceed to challenge in the TRA or the High Court. Section 138B(4) of the TAA is intended to be a mirror of the provisions under which the Commissioner can truncate a dispute. The taxpayer must notify the Commissioner of the proposed adjustment and identify how the adjustment fits into one of the following categories:

## the adjustment is in relation to a matter for which the material facts and relevant law are identical

to those for another assessment for the taxpayer for another period that is the subject of court proceedings, or

## the adjustment corrects a tax position taken by the taxpayer, or an associated person, as a

consequence or result of an incorrect tax position taken by another taxpayer that is, or was, the subject of court proceedings.

This process is intended to allow these subsequent disputes to be fast-tracked and catch up to the main dispute so that the TRA or court can consolidate the disputes if it sees fit to do so.
