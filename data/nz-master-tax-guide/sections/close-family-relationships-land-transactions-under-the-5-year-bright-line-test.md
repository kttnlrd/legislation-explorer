# Close family relationships — land transactions under the 5- year bright-line test ¶7-445

Land transactions involving family are not uncommon. Usually motivated by familial situations, tax implications may arise inadvertently. Below we discuss a number of common scenarios and the tax implications in regard to the 5-year bright-line test under s CZ 39 relating to transfers of land that are not a main home.

Interpretation statement IS 23/02 "Income Tax – Application of the s CZ 39 5 year bright-line test to certain family and close relationship transactions" considers how the 5-year bright-line test under s CZ 39 applies to a number of family transactions. IS 23/02 also contains a number of examples to illustrate the applications of the rules.

*Refs: s CZ 39*

## Parents assisting their children when buying a house

## Transfer from parent to child

Parents may assist their children to purchase a house in a number of ways, including:

## paying the initial deposit in full

## paying part of the deposit amount

## drawing down a mortgage on the property to pay the purchase price (partially or fully), either in their

personal names or the name of a trust

## buying the property in their/trust's name and gifting the property to the child.

In all the above cases the parents, or trustee, name is on the title to support with financial security.

The property is eventually transferred from the parents to the child, with the value increasing in the intervening period. The Commissioner, in interpretation statement IS 23/02, concludes that the bright-line test will apply to the following transfers of land:

## a disposal from parents, as individuals, to their child

## a disposal from a company (which is not a look-through company), where the parents are

shareholders, to their child

## a disposal from parents, as trustees of a trust, to their child who is a beneficiary of the trust.

If the disposal of residential land is for an amount less than market value, the Commissioner's view is that s GC 1 applies to deem the value to be market value.

The situation is legally different where the parent owns the land as bare trustee, or nominee.

## Transfer from parents as bare trustee or nominee to child

Due to the underlying legal nature, a transfer from a parent as nominee to their child is ignored for tax purposes (s YB 21). This is because when a nominee (or bare trustee) holds land or anything else for someone, the nominee or bare trustee is merely holding title on the child's behalf.

Consequently, when the nominee transfer the land to the child, it is not deemed a disposal of land. However, the Commissioner states at [144] in IS 23/02 that if the parents have taken out a loan to acquire the land and have assumed personal obligations for the repayment of the mortgage, that there is an active duty on the parents and they are not acting as bare trustees in this case. The statement also adds that the Commissioner considers that parents who borrow funds to purchase a property for their child are not their child's agents. They do not create or affect the child's relationship with third parties (for example, the bank). They borrow funds and purchase the property in their own right, not on behalf of their child. In

these situations the transfer from parents to child will constitute a disposal for the purposes of s CZ 39.

The interpretation gives guidance on the deductions allowed to the parents on such disposals. See [146] and [147]. The interpretation statement contains a number of examples to illustrate the various scenarios involving nominees and bare trustees.

## Transfer from company owned by parents to child

A transfer of residential land from a company (which has not elected to be treated as a look-through company), in which the parents are shareholders, to their child may also be subject to s CZ 39 if transferred within 5 years of acquisition.

## Transfer from family trust to child as beneficiary

A transfer of residential land from a trust where parents act as trustees to a child, who is a beneficiary of the trust, may also be subject to the bright-line test under s CZ 39 (see Example 3 in IS 23/02).

## Transfers of an interest in land to a new partner

An income tax liability may arise if a partner transfers a share of their residential land to their new spouse, civil union or de facto partner ("partner"), and results in them becoming co-owners of the land. A disposal takes place to the extent of the share that is transferred to the new partner. However, if the transfer takes place according to the terms of a settlement of relationship property, then s FB 3A applies and s CZ 39 does not apply. Note that the land must have been acquired before 29 March 2018. See s CZ 39(1B).

## Inherited land transferred between beneficiaries

The transfer by a beneficiary of land acquired under a will or the rules of intestacy to another beneficiary is not subject to the old bright-line test. See s CZ 39(7) read with s FC 9(2). Also see Example 8 in IS 23/02.

The subsequent sale of inherited land by the beneficiary may be subject to the bright-line test if disposed of to a third party as the transaction does not fall under the protection of the inheritance provisions (see Example 9 in IS 23/02).

See ¶7-405 for further discussion on the tax treatment of land transferred on the death of a person.
