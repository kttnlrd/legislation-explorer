# Tax avoidance contrasted with evasion ¶33-075

Evasion of tax is a criminal offence, with prison as a potential sentencing option, whether a taxpayer is charged under the TAA or the Crimes Act 1961. The offence requires knowing action with intent to evade. In C of IR v Challenge Corporation Ltd (1986) 8 NZTC 5,219 (PC) Lord Templeman said at 5,225: Evasion occurs when the Commissioner is not informed of all the facts relevant to an assessment of tax. Innocent evasion may lead to a reassessment. Fraudulent evasion may lead to a criminal prosecution as well as re-assessment.

Thus, where there is a statutory obligation to disclose one's income and where the taxpayer deliberately understates their income, that would be evasion and the Commissioner could successfully prosecute. The self-assessment process does not change this. In the case of tax avoidance, the tax return and the tax positions taken are correct in terms of the application of the substantive provisions of the Act, absent the application of the general anti-avoidance provision.

The 3-step analysis to application of anti-avoidance provision

There are 3 successive steps to the application of the general anti-avoidance provision. They are: 1. Identify what constitutes the arrangement. 2. Ascertain whether there is a more than merely incidental purpose or effect of tax avoidance as defined. 3. If there is a non-incidental purpose or effect of tax avoidance, consider what adjustments ought to be made to counteract the tax advantages.

This 3-step process has been explicitly endorsed by the Court of Appeal in C of IR v BNZ Investments Ltd (2001) 20 NZTC 17,103 (CA) at [42] and [175]. It has largely been endorsed by the Privy Council in Peterson v C of IR (2005) 22 NZTC 19,098; [2006] 3 NZLR 433 (PC) at [33]. The 3-step process is also explicitly endorsed in Ben Nevis Forestry Ventures Ltd v C of IR; Accent Management Ltd v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,188 (SC) at [160]. The practical operation of the 3-step analysis can be seen in action at [115] where the arrangement is mentioned and then the issue of whether the arrangement is a tax avoidance arrangement is introduced. The analysis from [116] to [156] concerns whether there is tax avoidance present. The Supreme Court, having concluded that tax avoidance was present, then dealt with the reconstruction or adjustment issue at [169]–[171]. This 3-step approach is also evident in Glenharrow Investments Ltd v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,236 (SC) at [35].

Most recently the Supreme Court in Frucor Suntory New Zealand Ltd v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (2022) 30 NZTC ¶25-024, when discussing the legal framework, commented that the result arrived at in Ben Nevis may seem paradoxical. A close analysis of the Parliamentary contemplation test means that the second step in the enquiry is paramount importance for the determination of whether tax avoidance exists ([57] and [58]).
