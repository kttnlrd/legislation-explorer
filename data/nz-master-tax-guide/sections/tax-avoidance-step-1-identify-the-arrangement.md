# Tax avoidance — Step 1: Identify the arrangement ¶33-095

The word "arrangement" is defined in s YA 1 of the IT07 as meaning: … an agreement, contract, plan, or understanding, whether enforceable or unenforceable, including all steps and transactions by which it is carried into effect.

The definition extends to agreements that are not legally binding but are binding only in honour: C of IR v BNZ Investments Ltd (2001) 20 NZTC 17,103 (CA) at [45] and [46].

The definition assumes a temporal connection. It assumes that a plan will be thought out and implemented in contrast to a situation where there are a series or sequential events that were not planned or coordinated at the outset but just occurred as a result of developing circumstance: see AMP Life Ltd v C of IR (2000) 19 NZTC 15,940. Thus, the transactions at issue in Newton v FC of T (1958) 11 ATD 442; [1958] AC 450 (PC) were described by Lord Denning in the following terms: The whole complicated series of transactions must have been the result of a concerted plan: and the nature of the plan is to be ascertained by the overt acts done in pursuance of it. … The whole of the transactions show that there was a concerted action to an end … The concept of planning at the outset needs to be qualified in that a situation where the broad steps of an arrangement are put in place at the outset and key decisions made on a yearly basis can still constitute an arrangement. Such a situation has been called an ambulatory arrangement: see C of IR v Penny and Hooper (2010) 24 NZTC 24,287 (CA) at [73]–[78] and Krukziener v C of IR (2010) 24 NZTC 24,563 at [6]–[27].

An arrangement can be both oral and written: see Ashton v C of IR (1975) 2 NZTC 61,030 (PC) at 61,033.

The scope of an arrangement is determined objectively: see Peterson v C of IR (2005) 22 NZTC 19,098 (PC) at 19,108–19,109.

The concept of an arrangement is wide enough to cover not just the initial setting up of a transaction but its outworking on a year by year basis: C of IR v Penny and Hooper (2010) 24 NZTC 24,287 (CA) at [70]–[78].

There is some uncertainty as to whether innocence or ignorance of the arrangement stops there being an arrangement: Peterson v C of IR (2005) 22 NZTC 19,098 (PC) suggests that ignorance or innocence is not relevant but the Ben Nevis judgment ((2009) 24 NZTC 23,188 (SC)) contains obiter comment at [160] and [161] that may imply that the Supreme Court does not necessarily agree with that aspect of the Peterson judgment of the Privy Council. That issue is also commented on by the Court of Appeal in Russell v C of IR (2012) 25 NZTC ¶20-120 (CA) at [49]–[54].
