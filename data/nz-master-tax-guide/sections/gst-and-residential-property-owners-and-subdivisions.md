# GST and residential property owners and subdivisions ¶32-570

The GST implications of minor one-off subdivisions have been largely settled by the Court of Appeal decision of Newman v C of IR (1995) 17 NZTC 12,097. The main issue is whether the subdivision activity is carried on continuously and regularly so as to constitute a taxable activity. The courts and IR have made the following statements:

## A straightforward, low-cost dividing off of one or 2 surplus sections and their subsequent sale would

not generate a taxable activity.

## Subdividing off 4 or more sections involving significant costs comprised a "continuous" and taxable

activity even though the original residence was retained for some time thereafter.

## IR considers that no "continuous" activity results if 3 sections are divided off a person's home

property and are sold, the subdivision involves minimal time and effort, and the person continues to live in the residence. However, a continuous activity might exist if other factors were involved, eg if significant and costly development work was undertaken as part of the subdivision. See discussion later in this paragraph.

In Newman, a taxpayer who subdivided a block of residential land into 2 blocks, keeping one and selling the other was initially found to have engaged in a taxable activity (even though it was not a part of the taxpayer's taxable activity as a builder for which he was GST registered). The Court of Appeal overturned the decision, finding that the limited activity in the case in question did not come within the statutory test of a "taxable activity". The Court looked at the transaction as a whole, rather than break it down into a series of sequential steps. McKay J observed that it was the one-off nature of the transaction that deprived it of the necessary "continuous activity" requirement. The absence of development work and lack of business aspect to the transaction was also noted as being of particular relevance.

More substantial subdivisions involving significant development work, financial investment and time and effort are likely to amount to taxable activities. For further discussion see ¶32-024.

## Commissioner's interpretation of "taxable activity"

QWBA QB 24/04, "When is a subdivision project a "taxable activity" for GST purposes?" sets out the Commissioner's view of when subdivision activity is carried on "continuously" or "regularly". It also covers when the sale of subdivided land is a supply made "in the course and furtherance of a taxable activity". The Commissioner considers the following 2 steps are required to determining whether a subdivision project is continuous or regular:

## the number of lots created and sold, and

## the level of activity involved in the subdivision project, relevant factors includes (not an exhaustive

list) the scale of the subdivision, level of development work involved, time and effort involved, amount of financial investment, commerciality of the transaction and level of repetition.

Generally, the more lots created and sold, the less activity is needed for a taxable activity to be continuous or regular. A subdivision resulting to only one supply will not be a taxable activity unless the scale of the activity and level of work involved is very high. The QBWA further states that while no specific number of lots created will be determinative of taxable activity, the Commissioner generally considers a subdivision project involving the creation and sale of 4 or more lots will be a taxable activity, unless the level of work involved is very low.

On the other hand, when only one supply is created from a subdivision activity, a very high level of activity is required for the subdivision activity to be continuous or regular, eg the construction and sale of a commercial building is a taxable activity due to the high level of scale and work involved with the activity.

If a taxpayer is already registered for GST, a subdivision activity that might not constitute a taxable activity on its own can still give rise to GST obligations if it was done in the course or furtherance of the taxpayer's existing taxable activity.

QB 24/04 contains a diagram that demonstrates the relevance of the level of activity involved in the project in relation to the number of supplies made (or planned to be made) and various examples that illustrate the approach the Commissioner is taking.

## Example: Simple subdivision

A scheme plan that divided property into 5 sections was subject to GST. During the course of his judgment, Barber DCJ observed that although the Court of Appeal in Newman had settled that a one-off subdivision creating 2 sections is not subject to GST, the cutting off of more than one section (eg 2) could also come within the Newman principles and not amount to a taxable activity. See Case S70 (1996) 17 NZTC 7,431.

## Example: Sequential activity resulting in 4 lots

A husband and wife who subdivided a block of land into 5 sections were found to be carrying on a taxable activity of subdividing and were liable for GST on the resulting sales. There had been a lengthy and uninterrupted period of sequential activity designed to achieve the sale of 4 sections, which amounted to a taxable activity. Citing Case S70 (see above), the Taxation Review Authority reiterated that a single subdivisional activity does not prevent it from being carried on "continuously". Whether an activity is continuous involves questions of fact and degree. See Case T40 (1997) 18 NZTC 8,267.
