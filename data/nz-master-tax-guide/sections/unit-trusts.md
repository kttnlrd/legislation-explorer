# Unit trusts ¶24-190

A unit trust is deemed to be a company for tax purposes. The unit holder is deemed to be a shareholder, the units are deemed to be shares and distributions are deemed to be dividends. A "unit trust" is defined as being any scheme or arrangement that is made for the purpose of, or has the effect of, providing facilities for the participation by the subscribers, purchasers or contributors in the income and gains earned by the trust.

That income may be of a capital or revenue nature, and the subscribers must participate in it as beneficiaries under a trust. Certain arrangements are expressly excluded from this definition:

## a trust for the benefit of debenture holders

## the Common Fund of Public Trust or any group investment fund established by Public Trust

## the Common Fund of the M#ori Trustee

## a group investment fund established under the Trustees Companies Act 1967

## a registered friendly society

## a superannuation fund

## an employee share purchase scheme

## a funeral trust, and

## any other trust declared by the Governor-General by Order in Council not to be a unit trust for the

purposes of s HD 13 (trustee as agent of unit trust).

The essential notion of the unit trust concept is that the scheme or arrangement must be in the nature of a trust. The concept would not therefore apply to a partnership, syndicate or joint venture. In these instances, the participants in the arrangement directly manage and control the undertaking. Many small investment clubs would also not be regarded as unit trusts.

*Refs: s HD 13*

## Single unit holder

Interpretation statement, IS 16/02, "Income tax — Unit trusts — when a unit trust can have a single unit holder", sets out the Commissioner's view that a trust with a single subscriber, purchaser or contributor can be within the definition of "unit trust" in the IT07. The item concludes that the "unit trust" definition requires only that the trust scheme or arrangement is "made for the purpose" or "has the effect" of providing facilities for multiple unit holders. This is to be determined by considering the legal relationships governing the trust — most importantly, the trust deed.

The view taken in this statement differs from the Commissioner's previously published position set out in the commentary to BR Pub 95/5A: "Relationship between the 'unit trust' and 'qualifying trust' definitions", which stated that to be a "unit trust" for income tax purposes required more than one unit holder (discussed below).

## Unit trust versus complying trust

In some cases, a unit trust may also fall within the definition of a complying trust under subpart HC. In public binding rulings BR Pub 95/5 and BR Pub 95/5A, the Commissioner set out the relationship between unit trusts and qualifying trusts (now complying trusts). Where a trust falls within both definitions, the unit trust rules prevail (s HC 1(2)(a)). BR Pub 95/5A (which replaced BR Pub 95/5) expired at the end of the 1999– 2000 income year and was not renewed. However, the Commissioner's view on the subject is unchanged.

Family trusts do not come within the unit trust regime since beneficiaries do not subscribe, purchase or contribute for their entitlement to distributions from the trust. However, some family trading trusts that provide for settlors to benefit in a fixed way might come within the unit trust definition.

Unit trusts and imputation

Tax paid by the unit trust is credited to the ICA and resident unit holders are entitled to imputation credits to the extent to which they are allocated to income distributions. Where units are redeemed, a debit will arise to the ICA if the circumstances in s OB 45 (or s OP 45 in the case of consolidated imputation groups) apply (¶17-045).

When the manager of a unit trust (or a person nominated by the manager) redeems units from unit holders in the ordinary course of business, the dividend derived does not include any imputation credits attached to the dividend to the extent it constitutes a recovery of the purchase price (s CD 16 and ¶16-630).

## Redemption of units

An amount paid by the manager of a unit trust upon the redemption of a unit may not be a dividend. Like companies, unlisted unit trusts (and group investment funds) must apply either the ordering rule or the slice rule to ascertain the amount of available subscribed capital that may be attributed to a redeemed unit, and therefore, be excluded from being a dividend.

Since 1 April 1996, unlisted unit trusts (and group investment funds) have had the option of issuing units on the basis that the ordering rule under s CD 22(2) will not apply to their cancellation or redemption. (Any units issued before that date were deemed to be issued on terms that their redemption was to be subject to the ordering rule, unless the manager elected before 31 March 1996 for those units to be redeemed under the slice rule (s CZ 13).

Broadly, when the "ordering" rule applies to a redemption, the proceeds from the redemption are taken first from the available subscribed capital of the particular class of unit being redeemed. To the extent that there is sufficient available subscribed capital for the class of units and one of the bright line tests in s CD 22(3) is met, the proceeds of the redemption will not be a dividend. On the other hand, if the "slice" rule in s CD 22(4) is applied, that part of the redemption that does not exceed the available "subscribed capital per share" (rather than the "subscribed capital per share cancelled", as applies in the ordering rule) is excluded from tax. There is no requirement for the bright line tests in s CD 22(3) to be met when the slice rule is applied. For further discussion of the application of the ordering rule and the slice rule, see ¶16-770.

For further detailed commentary on the taxation of unit trusts, see ¶79-113 Vol 7, No 9, at 7 and "Corporate tax regime for unit trusts now permanent" by Tony Lines in CCH, New Zealand Tax Planning Report, No 1, March 1996 at 5.

## Qualifying company regime

Unit trusts did not qualify for the qualifying company regime (s HA 2). Companies can no longer elect to be qualifying companies for income years beginning on or after 1 April 2011 (¶19-010).

## Bonus issues by foreign unit trusts

In relation to unit trusts that are foreign companies, a bonus issue made to a unit holder is a taxable bonus issue if the bonus issue is made instead of causing a beneficial interest in money or property of the unit trust to vest absolutely in the unit holder (¶16-573).

## Solicitors' nominee company

The Commissioner will not normally treat a solicitors' nominee company as a unit trust. This practice applies providing the company adopts the draft memorandum of association formulated by the Law Society for nominee companies.
