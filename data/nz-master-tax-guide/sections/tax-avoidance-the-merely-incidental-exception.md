# Tax avoidance — The merely incidental exception ¶33-115

The discussion in ¶33-105 concerns how a conclusion is arrived at as to whether an arrangement has a tax avoidance purpose or effect. If the arrangement does have a tax avoidance purpose or effect, a second separate question arises. The merely incidental exception is only relevant where one or more of the purposes or effects of the arrangement is referable to ordinary business or family dealing. Where there is a tax avoidance purpose or effect present and the arrangement also has a purpose or effect that is referable to ordinary business or family dealing, it is necessary to ascertain whether the tax avoidance purpose or effect is more than merely incidental to the arrangement.

## Classic statement on merely incidental exception

The "locus classicus" statement of what is meant by the "merely incidental" exception is contained in the judgment of Woodhouse P in C of IR v Challenge Corp Ltd (1986) 8 NZTC 5,001 (CA) where he said at 5,006: Against those considerations I think the bracketed words of sec 99(2)(b) have an important place as pointers to the intended scope of the section as a whole. The opening words of subsec (2) make every arrangement void as against the Commissioner where— "(b)… it has 2 or more purposes or effects, one of its purpose or effects (not being a merely incidental purpose or effect) is tax avoidance, whether or not any other or others of its purpose or effects relate to, or are referable to, ordinary business or family dealings,—".

Clearly enough para (b) is designed to catch an arrangement whenever it has a tax avoidance purpose (or effect) even if it has another and real purpose which is not tax avoidance. But the bracketed words enable a "merely incidental" tax avoidance purpose to be disregarded. So the meaning of that qualifying phrase is all important. Does it have the rather exiguous meaning and effect of excusing only "the causal" or "the minor" or "the inconsequential" tax avoidance purposes? If so, many "ordinary" dealings would probably be caught by sec 99 because inevitably the associated tax purpose could seem stronger than that. And the problem would be magnified if as well the assessment had to include estimates of the taxpayer's motives. However, I do not think the phrase "merely incidental" does have such a limited effect and in accord with Newton v C of T [1958] AC 450 I am satisfied as well that the issue as to whether or not a tax savings purpose or effect is "merely incidental" to another purpose is something to be decided not subjectively but objectively by reference to the arrangement itself.

As a matter of construction I think the phrase "merely incidental purpose or effect" in the context of sec 99 points to something which is necessarily linked and without contrivance to some or other purpose or effect so that it can be regarded as a natural concomitant.

## Factors to determine whether tax avoidance a natural concomitant

Woodhouse P went on to describe how one was to discern whether the tax avoidance was a natural concomitant or not. He considered that a number of factors were relevant. They included the degree of economic reality associated with the arrangement, the degree of artificiality or contrivance and the extent to which the arrangement seeks to exploit the statute in direct pursuit of tax advantages. There is currently an element of uncertainty as to whether the Woodhouse P test remains the law. The merely incidental exception was not at issue in Ben Nevis Forestry Ventures Ltd v C of IR; Accent Management Ltd v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,188 (SC) but there is an obiter comment made by the Supreme Court majority at [114] where the Court says:

It will rarely be the case that the use of a specific provision in a manner which is outside Parliamentary contemplation could result in the tax avoidance purpose or effect of the arrangement being merely incidental.

Despite the problems inherent in a repeated analysis looking at the same factors, it is now likely that the Woodhouse P formulation is still the law with respect to the application of the merely incidental exception — see Westpac Banking Corp v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,834, which largely accepted that the Woodhouse P formulation of the merely incidental test was still good law: [202]–[212].

## Other approaches to merely incidental test

There are also 2 other ways to approach the merely incidental test. The first is to retain the approach of first seeing if there is a tax avoidance purpose or effect, and then to weigh up whether the non-tax avoidance purpose or effect is merely incidental by reference to the relative size of the tax benefit. In this regard the merely incidental exception has been analysed by a court in terms of the monetary extent of the tax benefit in the past. Thus in Hadlee and Sydney Bridge Nominees Ltd v C of IR (1989) 11 NZTC 6,155 Eichelbaum CJ said at 6,175: In my opinion the purpose and effect of the arrangement was tax avoidance. Even if it were possible to regard that as one purpose and effect only (the other being to enable the objector's dependants to accumulate assets which would be secure from the risk of claims against the partnership) I cannot view it as "merely incidental". The potential tax benefits were too significant and obvious. I agree with the submission on behalf of the Commissioner, that it would require a considerable degree of naivety to conclude that they played merely an incidental part in the scheme.

The second approach is to interpret the para (b) limb of the definition of "tax avoidance arrangement" in s YA 1 as a composite whole and make a judgment as to whether there is a more than merely incidental purpose or effect of tax avoidance in one step. The advantage of such an approach is the elimination of the somewhat redundant exercise of repeating the same inquiry twice, which is implicit if one uses the approach in Ben Nevis at [108] and [109] to find tax avoidance and then the Woodhouse P approach to determine whether that avoidance purpose is more than merely incidental. Despite the savings in terms of eliminating conceptual repetition, this last approach has not been explicitly argued before or endorsed by a court.

The Commissioner's view of the merely incidental test is discussed at para 1.53 to 1.59 of interpretation statement IS 23/01 (¶33-137).
