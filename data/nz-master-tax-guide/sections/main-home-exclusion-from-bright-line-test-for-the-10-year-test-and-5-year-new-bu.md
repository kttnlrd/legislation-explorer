# Main home exclusion from bright-line test — for the 10-year test and 5-year new build test ¶7-220

## Note: This commentary covers the position relating to the main home exemption as it applies to the 10-

year and 5-year new build bright-line tests for residential land acquired on or after 27 March 2021 and disposed of before 1 July 2024.

A person's main home is not taxed under the bright-line test because an exclusion for main homes applies (known as the main home exclusion). These rules determine whether a person qualifies for the main home exclusion. There are 2 sets of main home rules for the bright-line test – the main home exclusion rules for the new 2-year test or the 5-year test (¶7-215), and the main home exclusion rules for the 10-year test (including the 5-year new build test).

## The principal exclusion

The principal exclusion from the bright-line test for the disposal of residential land acquired on or after 27 March 2021 and dispose of before 1 July 2024 is for the disposal of a person's main home. See former s CB 6A and ¶7-050.

## Land acquired on or after 27 March 2021

Residential land acquired on or after 27 March 2021 and disposed of within the bright-line period is not subject to the bright-line test if, for all of the days in the bright-line period, the land has been used predominantly for a dwelling that was the main home of the person, or of a beneficiary of a trust of which the person is a trustee: former s CB 16A.

Note that this main home exclusion does not apply to land acquired on or after 27 March 2021 where an offer was made on the land on or before 23 March 2021 and the offer was irrevocable before 27 March 2021.

The former main home exclusion in s CZ 40 applies in these cases (¶7-215).

## Used predominantly as main home

A main home of a person is the one dwelling that is mainly used as their residence (their home). Where a person has more than one home, their main home is the dwelling with which they have the greatest connection.

For the exclusion to apply, the land must have been used predominantly for a dwelling that was the main home. As discussed above, this means that most of the area of the land must have been actually used for the home. If less than 50% of the property is used for the main home, then the land apportionment test must be applied (see below).

## Time apportionment

The main home exclusion does not apply on an all or nothing basis. For land acquired on or after 27 March 2021, the exclusion applies only for the period the property is actually used as the taxpayer's main home. If the land has not been used as the main home for a period that exceeds 12 months, the main home exclusion does not apply. The sale proceeds on disposal are then taxable, but an adjustment is permitted to ensure that tax is paid only on any gains made during the period of time that the property was not used as the main home. See below for a discussion on the operation of the adjustment provision.

## Main home

For land acquired on or after 27 March 2021, the main home of a person is the one dwelling that is used as their residence, that is, the dwelling is their main home. Where a person has more than one home, their main home is the dwelling with which they have the greatest connection.

For the main home exclusion to apply, the dwelling must have been used as the home of the person disposing of the land or a beneficiary of a trust of which the person is a trustee (subject to some limitations, discussed below).

Note that for land acquired before 27 March 2021, the main home definition previously referred to the dwelling being mainly used as the person's home.

Used for all of the days in the bright-line period for the main home If more than 50% of the property is used for the main home, it is then necessary to consider whether the property has been so used for the relevant period.

The main home exclusion in former s CB 16A requires the land to have been used predominantly for a dwelling that was the main home for the person for all of the days in the bright-line period. The bright-line period is the period starting with acquisition date (generally the date of registration) and ending with the bright-line date of disposal. (¶7-050).

See Tax Information Bulletin ¶336-102 Vol 33, No 6, July 2021 at 18 for examples illustrating how the exclusion applies, as well as compares the result with the exclusion for land acquired before 27 March 2021.

12-month buffer period There is a "12-month buffer" under which any period of up to 12 months that the property is not used as the person's main home is counted as a main home period. This only applies if that period is immediately before or after a period during which the land was used as the main home. The rationale for allowing a grace period is to cater for situations where the property is empty for a period while the taxpayer is moving in or out or where it is used for another purpose for a short period of time (eg rented out while the owners were overseas). If the period of non-main home use exceeds 12 months, the main home exclusion does not apply and all of the sale proceeds from disposal are taxable. However, an adjustment is then made to the amount of taxable income to allow for periods that the land was actually used as the main home (see s CB 6A(8) and discussion below). See Tax Information Bulletin ¶336-102 Vol 33, No 6, July 2021 at 19 for examples to demonstrate the application of the 12-month buffer period rule.

## Main home test where construction takes more than 12 months

The 12-month buffer provision was rewritten by the March 2022 amendments to cover situations where a main home was under construction and takes longer than 12 months to complete. Under the previous wording of s CB 16A, land on which a main home was constructed would be taxed for some of the ownership period if the construction took longer than 12 months. The buffer period now covers the entire period that a main home is under construction, even if it exceeds 12 months. The "exempt main home period limit" in former s CB 16A(1C) is 365 days, or if the person is building a new home, the length of the construction period provided that extended period is reasonable.

## Adjustment for use as main home for only part of bright-line

period

Where a person has used their property as their main home for some, but not all, of the bright-line period, former s CB 6A(8) applies to determine the required adjustment from their taxable income. Effectively, their taxable income is reduced by an amount that reflects the periods of main home use by the person.

The amount of income from the disposal is reduced by an adjustment amount calculated using the following formula: unadjusted income × (exempted non-predominant main home days × main home percentage + exempted predominant main home days) / total days. where: unadjusted income is the amount of the gain on sale that is income under the bright-line test before application of the formula exempted non-predominant main home days is the total number of days:

## that the land was used as a dwelling for a main home, and

## of non-main-home use that interrupts main home use, but are within the exempt main home period

limit (generally, no more than 12-months in duration) main home percentage is the percentage area of the land that was used as a main home during the exempted non-predominant main home days exempted predominant main home days is the total number of days of predominant main home use and days that interrupt main home use for periods that are less than 12 months (the 12-month buffer period discussed above) total days is the total number of days in the bright-line period for the land.

The effect of this formula is to evenly apportion the sale proceeds from the property over the period it is held.

The amount attributable to the period the property was not used as the taxpayer's main home then becomes income for tax purposes.

See example from IR, "Special report on interest limitation and additional bright-line rules", 31 March 2022 at 183 to illustrate the required adjustment.

## Cost of land reduced

While an amount of gain on land sold within the bright-line period is taxable, a person is allowed a deduction for the cost of the land.

A person can claim a deduction under s DB 23 for the cost of property subject to the bright-line test.

However, where former s CB 6A(8) applies to make an adjustment to the person's taxable income, s DB 23C reduces the cost of the land that can be claimed. The cost is reduced by the excluded adjustment amount, which is calculated using the following formula:

cost × (exempted non-predominant main home days × main home percentage + exempted predominant main home days) total days

where: cost is the cost of the land.

The other terms are those as defined above which are applied to calculate another reduction from the amount that is to be income on account of main home use under former s CB 6A(8). See examples from IR, "Special report on interest limitation and additional bright-line rules", 31 March 2022 at 185 to demonstrate the application of the rules.

## Main home exclusion and trusts

The rules around the application of the main home exclusion for properties owned by a trust remain as they did under the old rules. That is, if a property is owned by a trust, the main home exclusion will apply to the trustee when the dwelling is the main home of a beneficiary of the trust. The land must be used predominantly for a dwelling that is the beneficiary's main home during the bright-line period. If a principal

settlor of a trust has a main home that is not the one being disposed of by the trust, the main home exclusion cannot apply to any property owned by the trust.

A principal settlor of a trust is the person whose settlements for the trust are the greatest or greatest equal, by market value. For these purposes, a person's settlements do not include the provision of services at below market value. In addition, a person is not a principal settlor if they have provided a "no strings attached" gift to the trust. A transfer of value will also be disregarded unless that transfer is by:

## a beneficiary

## a trustee

## a person with power of appointment or removal of trustees

## a person with a contingent interest in the trust property, in the case that the trust fails, or

## a decision-maker under the trust.

There is no guidance as to who "a decision-maker under the trust" might be. Presumably this will include protectors, advisory trustees and any other person in a role that gives the person any power over the trustees or the ability to exercise what would otherwise be trustee powers. A person with the ability to determine a trustee dispute or disagreement may also be a decision-maker under a trust. Accordingly, care should be taking when drafting deeds of trust to avoid inadvertently providing that a person is or could be a principal settlor of a trust.

To be able to determine who the principal settlor is, trustees will need to ensure adequate records of gifting and settlements onto the trust by all settlors (as defined for tax purposes), not just the settlors named in the deed of trust. The definition of "settlement" is modified for these purposes by the definition of "settlement" in s YA 1.

## Example: Trusts — student flat

Dave has two properties, a family home that he lives in, and a student flat that his son lives in while studying. Dave settles the student flat on a trust and makes his son a discretionary beneficiary of the trust.

The trust cannot use the main home exclusion because the principal settlor of the trust (Dave) has another main home.

Example taken from Tax Information Bulletin ¶281-115 Vol 28, No 1, February 2016 at 86.

## Multiple homes

Where a person resides in multiple homes, only one of those properties can be their main home. Their main home would be determined according to which property the person has the greatest connection with. The factors that determine these connections would include:

## the time the person occupies the dwelling

## where their immediate family, if any, live

## where their social ties are strongest

## the person's use of the dwelling

## the person's employment, business interests and economic ties to the area where the dwelling is

located, and

## whether the person's personal property is in the dwelling.

The factors are similar to those used to determine if a person has a permanent place of abode under current case law. Inland Revenue has stated that existing guidance on the "permanent place of abode" test (¶1-120) should assist in determining which property the person has the greatest connection with. See example from Tax Information Bulletin ¶281-115 Vol 28, No 1, February 2016 at 86 to demonstrate the application of the rules.

Using the exclusion for 2 properties

Under limited circumstances, a person may use the main home exception for 2 properties sold at the same time. An example is when a person lives in a home for less than one year as their main home and moves into a new home while trying to sell the original home. The original home may satisfy the requirements to be the person's main home for the period before moving into the main home. The new home may also satisfy the requirements to be the person's main home for the subsequent period.

The ownership overlap of the properties will not mean the original home fails to satisfy the requirements to be a main home for the previous period. If the 2 properties were sold at the same time, the owner may be able to use the main home exception for both properties (if they both satisfied the requirements to be a main home for the different periods).

## Habitual sellers

A person will not be able to use the main home exclusion if they:

## already used it 2 or more times in the previous 2 years, or

## have engaged in a regular pattern of acquiring and disposing of residential land used predominantly

for a dwelling as a main home.

QWBA QB 16/07 "Income Tax — Land sale rules — Main home and residential exclusions — Regular pattern of acquiring and disposing, or building and disposing" considers what constitutes a regular pattern for the purposes of the main home (s CB 16A) and residential (s CB 16) exclusions. See the discussion of the QWBA at ¶7-211. Despite amendments to the bright-line test, the principles remain the same regarding patterns of disposals of residential properties.

Note that although the bright-line period has been extended to 10 years for land acquired on or after 27 March 2021, and 5 years for land acquired between 29 March 2018 and 26 March 2021 (¶7-050), the application of the main home exclusion continues to consider how often the exclusion has been used in the previous 2-year period.

For the purposes of the main home exclusion, from 27 March 2021 the regular pattern restriction has been expanded to apply to a group of persons undertaking buying and selling activities together, rather than just the activities of a single person. A "group of persons" means 2 or more people when they occupy, or have occupied, the residential land together as their main home. It also includes a non-natural person (such as a trustee of a trust or another entity) if at least one of the people who occupies the main home has significant involvement in, or control of, the activities of the non-natural person. For the avoidance of doubt, it is provided that if the person is able to direct, either alone or as part of a group, the activities of the non-natural person, then they have significant involvement in, or control of, the activities of the non-natural person. See ¶7-211 for an example of a group of persons for these purposes.
