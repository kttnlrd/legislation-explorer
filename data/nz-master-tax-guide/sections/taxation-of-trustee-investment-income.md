# Taxation of trustee investment income ¶25-145

A trustee of a trust should account for investment income on the same basis as any other person. However, there are exceptions relating to calculation methods, thresholds and tax rates. Further, the trustee-specific provision may not apply to all trusts. Consequently, care is required to correctly identify how to calculate investment income.

## FIF and CFC regime

The foreign investment fund (FIF) regime and controlled foreign company (CFC) regime apply to tax shareholdings in a foreign company. The FIF regime effectively applies to foreign shareholdings of up to 50% (¶26-110). The CFC regime applies to a company defined as a CFC, which essentially is a foreign company controlled by 5 or fewer New Zealand residents (¶26-038 and ¶26-050).

## $50,000 exemption for FIF interest will not apply to most family trusts

The FIF rules do not apply to natural persons and certain trusts if the total cost of FIF interests is $50,000 or less at all times in an income year (¶26-125). This exemption only applies for the following type of trusts (s CQ 5(1), (5)):

## deceased estates within 5 years of the deceased person's death, or

## trusts settled by the Accident Compensation Corporation, or

## trusts where the settlor is required to pay damages under a court order.

## Certain trustees can change between the FDR and CV methods

Under s EX 62(8), an exception applies to testamentary and family trusts that allows a trustee to change an unlimited number of times between the fair dividend rate (FDR) and comparative value (CV) methods for determining FIF income (see ¶26-130 and ¶26-132 for FIF calculation methods and change of calculation methods). One method must be used for a minimum of a single income year before changing to another and all FIF income must be calculated in accordance with the same method.

To qualify for this exception, the trustee must be a trustee of a trust that:

## has no gifting settlor (defined below) who is not a natural person or a deceased person, and

## is a complying trust at all times in the income year, and

## is mainly for the benefit of a:

## natural person for whom the gifting settlor or settlors of the trust have (or if deceased had) natural

love and affection, or

## registered tax charity, and

## is not a superannuation scheme.

For the purposes of this rule, a "gifting settlor" is defined as a natural person, including a deceased person who (s EX 46(12)) is:

## a settlor of that trust or of a trust the trustee of which settles property on the first trust, whether the

settlement is made directly or via the trustees of other trusts, and

## not a trustee.

## CFC regime

The CFC regime applies to trustee investors in the same way as it applies to other taxpayers (¶26-038).

However, s EX 19 provides that where a CFC derives a taxable distribution from a non-complying trust and an investor has or would have had attributed CFC income if the taxable distribution was included in the CFC's net attributable CFC income, a pro-rated amount of additional CFC income is attributed to the investor. The tax rate on this income is the 45% rate that applies to taxable distributions.

## Trustee disclosure obligations in respect of FIF and CFC interests

Trustee investors are required to disclose details of any attributing FIF or CFC interests held during an income year unless an exemption is granted by the Commissioner (s 61). The details of exemptions granted by the Commissioner are published on an annual basis. For the exemptions granted for the 2023 income year, see Tax Information Bulletin ¶353-111, Vol. 35, No 3, April 2023 at 39.

## Note: The Taxation (Annual Rates for 2025–26, Compliance Simplification, and Remedial Measures) Bill,

which was introduced on 26 August 2025, proposes a new calculation method for calculating FIF income or loss from attributing interests in a FIF. The new revenue account method (RAM) would allow certain FIF interests to be taxed on a realisation basis — ie on dividends derived and gains or losses on disposal. An example on p 38 of the Bill commentary explains how this could work for a family trust.

## PIE regime

The portfolio investment entity (PIE) regime is a system for taxing collective investment entities that operate principally at the retail (or individual investor) level. The PIE regime provides that the returns of retail investors are effectively subject to the imposition and payment of income tax in the hands of the PIE as a collective investment vehicle (¶29-100).

## Tax rates for the trustee investor in the multi-rate PIE

Income derived by a multi-rate PIE must be attributed to its investors in proportion to their interests in the investment assets of the PIE (¶29-115). The multi-rate PIE calculates the tax payable using the tax rate as advised by the investor and pays the tax to IR.

The investors must provide the multi-rate PIE with a notified investor rate (NIR), that is the prescribed investor rate (PIR) set out in Sch 6 which is chosen by the investor as appropriate to the investor's income circumstances (s HM 60 and ¶29-145).

If a NIR is not provided by an investor, the multi-rate PIE must tax the attributed income to the investor at the default rate of 28% (s HM 60(6)).

A New Zealand resident trustee is permitted to choose a PIR of 0%, 10.5%, 17.5% or 28%. Generally, attributed PIE income derived by an investor in a multi-rate PIE is excluded income for the investor (s CX 56B). However, in the trust context, a trustee investor who has chosen a PIR of 0%, 10.5% or 17.5% is not able to treat the attribute multi-rate PIE income as excluded income (s CX 56(2)).

## A PIR of 0%

A trustee investor who has chosen a PIR of 0% is not able to treat the attribute PIE income as excluded income (s CX 56(2)). Essentially this means the trustee becomes responsible for attending to all taxation obligations (depending on whether the attributed income is taxed as trustee income or as beneficiary income) imposed in relation to the income attributed by the multi-rate PIE, including timely payments of both provisional tax and terminal tax.

When calculating the tax liability, the trustee is entitled to take into account any tax credits advised by the multi-rate PIE, including resident withholding tax (RWT), imputation credits and foreign tax credits (¶29-145).

Note the foreign tax credits available is limited to the lesser of the credits attributed by the multi-rate PIE or the attributed income multiplied by the trustee rate of tax (s HM 52(3)).

The trustee may also take into account any tax loss as advised by the multi-rate PIE. Effectively, a deduction is available for the amount of any losses attributed to the trustee investor (¶29-135).

The attributed income of the trustee who has not advised a PIR is taxed by the multi-rate PIE at the default rate of 28%. Under s HM 57, a trustee who derives attributed PIE income and does not choose a PIR rate is deemed to have a PIR rate of 0%. This deemed PIR of 0% means the attributed income is not excluded income and therefore the trustee must include the attributed income in a tax return taking into account the tax credits allowed for the 28% default rate applied by the multi-rate PIE. This also means the attributed income is taxable income and as a consequence, there is no capping of tax at the maximum PIR of 28%.

A trustee of a charity that is registered under the Charities Act 2005 is only permitted to use the PIR rate of 0% (s HM 57).

## A PIR of 10.5%

A trustee of a testamentary trust for minor beneficiaries may choose a PIR of 10.5%. A testamentary trust with minor beneficiaries is eligible for the PIR of 10.5% if all the settlements onto the trust were made under a will, codicil, intestacy or court variation, and (s HC 37):

## the minor beneficiary is alive within 12 months of the settlor's death, or

## the minor beneficiary has a sibling or half sibling alive within 12 months of the date of the settlor's

death.

A trustee with a PIR of 10.5% is not able to treat the attributed income as excluded income and, therefore, the trustee must include the attributed income by the multi-rate PIE in the trust tax return and is responsible for paying any further tax associated with the attributed income after taking into account the 10.5% tax applied by the multi-rate PIE.

The multi-rate PIE applies any available tax credits (including RWT and imputation credits) to satisfy the tax liability of 10.5% on the attributed income. If the credits exceed the tax liability, the multi-rate PIE appropriately increases the trustee's investor interest in the fund or makes a distribution to the trustee investor. Any loss attributed by the multi-rate PIE does not flow-through to the trustee. The multi-rate PIE utilises the losses and appropriately adjusts in a similar manner by either adjusting the trustee's investor interest in the fund or by making a distribution to the trustee investor.

## A PIR of 17.5%

A trustee with a PIR of 17.5% is also not able to treat the attributed income as excluded income and similarly, the trustee is required to include the attributed multi-rate PIE income in a tax return and satisfy any further tax obligations after taking into account the 17.5% tax applied by the multi-rate PIE. Any tax payable is to be paid by the trustee depending on whether the income is to be taxed as trustee income or as beneficiary income.

Any available tax credits and loss attributed by the multi-rate PIE follows the same treatment as discussed above for the PIR of 10.5%.

## Listed PIE dividends

A listed PIE calculates assessable income and pays income tax in the same manner as a conventional company (¶29-125). Dividends distributed by the listed PIE must be fully imputed (to the extent available).

Generally, dividends paid by the listed PIE are excluded income in the hands of shareholders. Treating the dividends as excluded income allows the listed PIE to distribute without taxation to shareholders any share sale gains that have been excluded income for the PIE itself. This also enables the listed PIE to distribute gains of a capital nature without taxation in the hands of shareholders.

However, s CX 56C permits for a New Zealand resident trustee or resident individual to elect to treat the dividends as taxable income in a tax return. This treatment provides shareholders access to any excess imputation credits and allows them to use these as tax credits against tax on other taxable income.

For discussion on the taxation of distributions from group investment funds, see ¶24-070.
