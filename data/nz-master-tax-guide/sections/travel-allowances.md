# Travel allowances ¶3-445

An employer may reimburse an employee for using a private vehicle for work-related purposes. The reimbursements are tax free to the employee if the requirements of s CW 17 are met. In accordance with s CW 17 employers can choose either to reimburse:

## actual costs incurred by the employee

## a reasonable estimate of the costs incurred by the employee

## published mileage rates, or

## IR's published rates.

In Tax Information Bulletin ¶185-120 Vol 18, No 5, June 2006 at 135, the Commissioner announced that employers may start using rates published by a reputable independent New Zealand source, eg New Zealand Automobile Association Inc, as an alternative to IR's published rates. Any alternative mileage rates used must be a reasonable estimate given the nature of the business and the type of vehicles involved. IR's published rates are set out in CCH, Dep Rates 2026, at ¶50-084.

It is important to note that the distance to which the mileage rate is applied is work-related mileage only, and not the total distance travelled by the vehicle for the year. A record needs to be maintained of the dates the vehicle is used, the mileage and the destination.

Reimbursements made in excess of the calculated amounts will be employment income to the employee and subject to PAYE.

*Refs: s CW 17*

## IR published rates

OS 19/04b "Commissioner's statement on using a kilometre rate for employee reimbursement of a motor vehicle" explains the acceptable method to establish the tax exempt portion of reimbursements to employees for the business running of an employee's motor vehicle (see also ¶10-710 for a discussion of OS 19/04a, "Commissioner's statement on using a kilometre rate for business running of a motor vehicle – deductions", which explains how the kilometre rates are to be applied for deductions for the business use of a vehicle). The statement accepts that the kilometre rate set under the statement can be regarded as being a reasonable estimate of expenditure under s CW 17. The statement sets out the following operational practice and rates for the 2018–19 income year:

Vehicle Type Tier One Rate Tier Two rate Petrol or Diesel 79 cents 30 cents Petrol Hybrid 79 cents 19 cents Electric 79 cents 9 cents

## Where the employee maintains a logbook, or other evidence that establishes the proportion of

employment use for an income year, the calculation of the exempt portion may be based on the IR's rates. The Tier One rate can be applied for the business portion of the first 14,000 kms (total) travelled by the vehicle in each income year, after which the Tier Two rates will apply. Note that even where the employee records a logbook test period in accordance with the rules in ss DE 6 to DE 11, the annual kilometres travelled must still be monitored so that it is known whether the 14,000 figure is reached each year.

## However, where no logbook or other records are maintained, the use of the Tier One rate to calculate

the exemption for employee reimbursement is limited to the first 3,500 kms travelled for employment purposes. The Tier Two rates may be used for kilometres travelled for employment purposes above the 3,500 km figure.

## The 3,500 kms is based on 25% of the average annual expected (14,000) kilometres travelled for

each motor vehicle. 25% is the maximum percentage allowed for business deductions where no logbook is maintained.

## The changes to the business deductions take effect for the 2018 income year onwards and require a

different approach and calculation of those rates. IR accepts that the changes with regard to employee reimbursement will always be subject to a time lag using those figures. As such, for the 2019–20 income year employers may reimburse employees using the above 2018–19 rates from the date of the statement, 16 August 2019.

OS 19/04b includes the following examples using the kilometre rate method to calculate employee reimbursement for the 2019–20 income year:

## Example: Single journey — no logbook

Employee used their private vehicle for a single 65 km journey for their employment and did not maintain a logbook. They are entitled to treat any reimbursement as tax free for the first 3,500 employment-related kilometres at the Tier One rate.

The employer must keep a record of how many employment-related kilometres are travelled in the income year (for that vehicle) because any kilometres over 3,500 will only be tax free calculated at the Tier Two rate.

The calculation (assuming the 3,500 km figure was not breached) would be: 65 km x 0.79 cents (the Tier One rate) = $51.35 tax free reimbursement

## Example: Regular journey — no logbook

Employee used their private vehicle, a petrol-powered motor vehicle, for a regular 65 km journey for their employment and did not maintain a logbook. They are entitled to treat any reimbursement as tax free for the first 3,500 employment-related kilometres at the Tier One rate.

The employer must keep a record of how many employment-related kilometres are travelled in the income year (for that vehicle) because any kilometres over 3,500 will only be tax free calculated at the Tier Two rate.

The calculation (assuming the 3,500 km figure was breached 2 months earlier) would be: 65 km x 0.30 cents (the Tier Two rate) = $19.50 tax free reimbursement

## Example: Regular journey — trial logbook

Employee used their private vehicle, a petrol hybrid-powered motor vehicle, for a regular 65 km journey for their employment and did maintain a logbook (albeit for a 3-month trial period). Employee's logbook evidences that the vehicle is used 40% for employment- related purposes and 60% for private/other use. They are entitled to treat any reimbursement as tax free for the first 40% of the vehicle's total kilometres up to the first 14,000 at the Tier One rate. The employee must notify the employer when the annual kilometres reaches 14,000. This is because any kilometres from that point on will only be tax free calculated at the Tier Two rate.

Alternatively, the employer could keep a record of the employment kilometres and use the Tier Two calculation once the claim reached 5,600 kilometres (40% of 14,000). Using this alternative approach will mean that the employee will not need to keep any records beyond the 3-month trial logbook period.

The calculation (assuming the 5,600 km figure was breached 2 months earlier) would be: 65km x 0.19 cents (the Tier Two rate) = $12.35 tax free reimbursement

## Allowance for additional transport costs

Any reimbursing allowance paid by an employer to an employee for additional transport costs is exempt to the extent that it meets the following criteria:

## it is paid for costs incurred in travelling between the employee's home and place of work, and

## the costs were incurred by the employee for the benefit and convenience of the employer, and

## the costs were attributable to one or more of the following work-related factors:

## Time of work — this refers to the time of day or days of the week the employee is required to work

whereby the employee might incur additional transport costs.

Example: Sandra has a job as a commercial cleaner. She travels by bus to start work at 8 pm and finishes at midnight, by which time the buses to her suburb no longer run. She travels home by taxi.

## Carrying work equipment — this refers to the need to carry goods or material for use or disposal in

the course of the employee's work.

Example: Aroha works for a catering company. On occasions, the company vehicle is double-booked. When this happens, Aroha uses her own van to deliver food to a function.

## Fulfilling statutory obligations.

## Temporary change in place of work — this refers to a temporary change from the normal place of

work of an employee in relation to the same employer.

Example: Sam works for a bank. He is assigned to work at another branch across town for 8 months. He incurs the cost of an additional bus trip for that period.

## Any other condition of work — this is a catch-all type of factor.

Example: Peter usually buses into work, but his employer requests that he bring in his car to carry out an occasional delivery while the company car is in the workshop.

## The absence of a public passenger transport service — a public passenger transport service must

adequately serve the place of work. Generally, this means that the public transport service must run near the place of work, adequately synchronise with the hours of work and be able to carry sufficient numbers of the workers who might use the service.

Once it is established that the additional transport costs are attributable to at least one of the above 6 factors, the employer may make an estimate of the additional component of those transport costs. Note that it is only the "additional" component of the employee's transport costs that qualifies for the tax exemption.

If any of the first 5 factors are responsible for the additional transport costs, then the costs in excess of those that the employee would ordinarily have been expected to incur in travelling between home and work, were it not for that factor, are taken as the additional component of the transport costs.

Example: An employee who normally buses to work at a cost of $1.50 a day is required to use their car to take tools to and from work, at a cost of $5 a day. The employee would be eligible to have their reimbursing allowance exempted to the extent of: $5 # $1.50 = $3.50 a day.

A different calculation must be made when the sixth factor (ie absence of a public passenger transport system) is responsible for the additional transport costs. In such cases, the additional component for each day is the actual travelling costs less $5. An amount calculated in accordance with that formula would be eligible for exemption. However, it is important to note that there is a proviso in relation to employees who must travel long distances. This proviso is to the effect that the cost of travelling distances of more than 70 km a day may not be taken into account in calculating the additional component unless special circumstances exist.

## Operational statement OS 23/01 on additional transport costs

Operational Statement OS 23/01, "When employee allowances for additional transport costs for home to work travel are exempt from income tax" sets out the Commissioner's view of when these costs are exempt under s CW 18. It confirms the general rule that an employee's private travel expenses reimbursed, or paid for, by an employer are taxable in the hands of the employee and subject to PAYE.

In order to determine if the exemption applies to the additional travel costs, and whether an apportionment must be made (apportionment is indicated by the use of the words "to the extent of" in the provision), a 3- step analysis must be undertaken. The Commissioner sets out the following analysis:

## Step 1 – Are one or more of the factors present that are required for the exemption to additional

transport costs to apply (see commentary above)? If none of these factors is present, then the additional transport costs exemption cannot apply.

## Step 2 – Did the employee incur the additional transport costs in connection with their employment

and for the employer's benefit or convenience?

## Step 3 – How much of a travel allowance is exempt under the additional transport costs exemption?

Calculate the additional transport costs and compare them with the allowance paid.

The operational statement states that employers can make reasonable estimates of additional transport costs for employees. Taxpayers are reminded of the 70 kilometres per day limit that applies (see above), however special circumstances may apply where an employer does include in excess of this distance. This only applies in exception, abnormal or unusual circumstances. If an employee has to travel more than 70 kms between work and home as a matter of course, then the limit applies as the circumstances are not exceptional.

The statement contains a number of useful examples to illustrate when special circumstances apply and when they do not. The statement concludes that where a travel allowance can only be received by entering into a salary sacrifice arrangement, the additional travel costs allowance is not a reimbursement for travel costs in reality and the amount of the travel costs are not exempt, and PAYE must be paid on the allowance.

Note: Fringe benefit tax does not apply to certain public transport fares subsidised by an employer (¶21-655 and ¶21-660).

## Operational statement on employer-provided travel

Operational statement OS 19/05, "Employer-provided travel from home to a distant workplace – income tax (PAYE) and fringe benefit tax" clarifies and simplifies the tax rules for employer-related travel to distant workplaces.

Payments made by an employer in relation to a private expense of an employee are usually subject to tax.

The cost of home-to-work travel is generally private expenditure as it is incurred so an employee can get

to work. As this expenditure is usually private, if the employer provides the employee's travel from home to work, the starting point is that it is taxable.

However, some travel from home to work is not taxable because the expenditure relates to travel that is "on work". This means not all employer-provided travel from an employee's home to work is taxable, and an employer who provides travel from home to a distant workplace must consider whether that travel is subject to tax. If the employee is provided with an allowance or is reimbursed in relation to the cost of the travel, then the employer may have to deduct and pay PAYE in relation to that payment.

The operational statement considers the question of whether home-to-work travel is taxable and is discussed in detail at ¶10-710.
