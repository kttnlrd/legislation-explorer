# Valuation of bloodstock ¶27-320

Special rules apply to the valuation of bloodstock for income tax purposes. In broad terms, the cost of stallions and broodmares is written down to $1 by applying a "specified write-down" formula.

Bloodstock can be written down if it is at least 2 years of age at the end of the income year. The bloodstock need not have been used for mating but merely purchased or owned with an intention of being used for such a purpose where the owner is in the business of breeding bloodstock (Drummond v C of IR (2013) 26 NZTC ¶21-023). The write-down is based on cost price but in some circumstances market value may be used if the value of the horse is reduced substantially, ie as a result of accident, birth deformity or infertility.

QB 21/09: "How to determine the cost price of bloodstock" outlines how to determine the cost price of bloodstock and is relevant to a person who has a bloodstock breeding business.

When valuing bloodstock for tax purposes, the general principle is that actual cost should be used as the basis of valuation. Where the actual cost is not known with certainty, for example home-bred progeny, a consistent means of establishing the cost price of that foal is still required.

The general rule is that is initially valued at cost price. This is the actual cost incurred in acquiring the animal (including transportation), or in the case of home-bred bloodstock cost price is the expenditure incurred by the breeder in producing the progeny.

At the end of each income year bloodstock must be valued at its cost price less any reduction applying for that year.

Determination of cost price differs for each type of bloodstock.

## Purchased bloodstock

Bloodstock that is purchased, either privately or at horse sales, should be valued at its acquisition cost plus the cost of getting the horse to its new location. This value may include items such as the acquisition cost, veterinarian fees, valuation fees, transportation and travel insurance.

Example: Megan is an Auckland breeder and purchases a new filly for $25,000 from a stud in Cambridge. The cost of transporting the filly to Auckland is $400. Prior to agreeing to the purchase, a vet did a health inspection, and this cost Megan $1,600. The total cost to Megan is $27,000. The filly has a cost price valuation of $27,000 for tax purposes.

## Broodmares

Broodmares not previously used for breeding in New Zealand are written down over a maximum of 6 years (if they start breeding at age 2). Broodmares aged 8 and over are written down in full in the year they are first used for breeding. In both cases, the write-down is accelerated by 25% pa.

Broodmares that previously have been used for breeding in New Zealand are written down over a maximum of 7 years (if they start breeding at age 2). Broodmares aged 8 and over are written down in full in the year they are first used for breeding. Write-downs for broodmares that previously have been used for breeding in New Zealand are based on cost price.

The purchase of broodmares in-foal raises 2 options for determining cost price due to a long gestation period that may straddle balance date. Determination of cost price is dependent on whether the foal is born before the first or second balance date of the new owner.

One of the components of a foal's cost is the stallion service fee. Where the mare has been purchased in- foal, the service fee would have been paid by the mare's previous owner. Usually, the stallion service fee is easy to determine. This service fee will contribute to the total purchase price of the mare but needs to be separated out at the time the cost price of the foal is determined. This is on the basis that the service fee component of the mare's purchase price is the amount of the premium paid for the unborn foal. Where the service fee component is not able to be ascertained, the Commissioner will accept an apportionment of the acquisition price between the mare and the foal, based on the recommendation of a recognised bloodstock valuer.

If the amount of the service fee is the same or exceeds the total acquisition price of the in-foal mare, then the mare is valued at $1. The remainder of the acquisition price will be attributed to the foal's cost price. This recognises the reality that, in these circumstances, the price paid was to acquire the foal and not the mare.

The 2 options available for those taxpayers buying an in-foal mare are:

## Where the foal is born before the first balance date of the new owner, the service fee component of

the acquisition price for the mare would be allocated to the foal. The service fee is deducted from the acquisition price of the mare before applying any reduction to the mare's cost price for that year, and

## If the mare is still in-foal at the new owners' first balance date, the cost price of the mare will be the

total acquisition cost of the mare. The reduction applicable to the mare in that year would then be applied to this total acquisition price.

In the second year of ownership, if no live foal results from the pregnancy, the mare will remain valued at the written down amount set out above and the year 2 reduction applied to it.

If a foal is born in the second year, a cost price will need to be determined for both the mare and the foal at the breeder's balance date for that year. To do this, the service fee component needs to be removed from the mare and attributed to the foal. See determination of cost price of the foal below. This results in a lowering of the mare's value (by the amount attributed to the foal). The year 2 reduction to the mare can then be applied to this resulting lowered value.

## Stallions

Stallions not previously used for breeding in New Zealand have an annual straight-line write-down of 50% of the cost price, unless the owner chooses to value the stallion using the reducing value method at the rate of 75%. Stallions that previously have been used for breeding in New Zealand are written down at the rate of 20% pa based on cost price. From 1 August 2007, the slower write down does not apply to shuttle stallions that have been previously used for breeding in New Zealand, if they are subsequently bought by a New Zealand breeder.

## Home-bred foals

Unborn foals are not required to be valued at balance date. This is also the case where a mare is purchased by a breeder in-foal. See discussion above for valuation of pregnant mares.

Foals are valued on the breeder's first balance date after they are born. The cost price of home-bred foals is made up of the following:

## the stallion service fee paid, and

## any reduction attributable to the mare in the year of foaling, and

## any foaling, weaning and vet fees incurred in rearing the foal between the birth of the foal and the first

balance date.

Where external parties are not employed for foaling and weaning, it may be difficult to determine the exact cost to the breeder in relation to these activities, therefore, the Commissioner will accept a de minimis amount of $500 to be used as an acceptable approximation of these foaling and weaning expenses.

Any expenditure incurred in raising a foal after the first balance date is not required to be added to the cost price of the foal.

Stallion service fees will be relatively easy to determine where the stallion is not the breeder's own, and a stud has been used. Where the breeder owns the stallion, the breeder can choose the determine the service fee in one of 2 ways:

## the service fee usually charged by the breeder in an arms-length transaction, or

## the direct costs of the stallion in the year of service divided by the number of mares serviced in that

year.

For the latter option, the direct costs are an accumulation of insurance, external service costs (e.g. vet fees), and the stallion's reduction in the year of service.

If a mare has been leased by the breeder, then the cost price of the progeny must include any lease fee paid in the year of foaling.

## GST and bloodstock

The valuation of bloodstock is not affected by goods and services tax as regards cost price or market value.

The cost price of bloodstock does not include input tax charged to the person at the time of supply. Nor is the tax included in the market value of bloodstock disposed of by the person.

The supply of bloodstock of any age can be zero rated for export within 24 months of supply, provided IR approval had been obtained. (The usual requirement is that goods must be exported within 28 days of supply.) Bloodstock owners must apply in writing to IR enclosing a copy of the sale contract to gain the concession (Tax Information Bulletin ¶193-101 Vol 19, No 3, April 2007 at 91).
