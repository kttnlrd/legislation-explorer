# What is a trust? ¶25-010

A trust, in its simplest form, is property that is subject to obligations owed by the property's owner (the trustee) to the beneficiary or beneficiaries for whom the property is held. Beneficiaries' rights to the due and proper administration of a trust is a fundamental aspect of the trust relationship. As noted by Millet LJ in Armitage v Nurse [1998] Ch 241 at 253–254: … there is an irreducible core of obligations owed by the trustees to the beneficiaries and enforceable by them which is fundamental to the concept of the trust. If the beneficiaries have no rights enforceable against the trustees there are no trusts.

As a trust itself has no legal personality, each trustee acts personally for the benefit of the trust's beneficiaries. This means that a trustee is liable for that trustee's actions as if those actions were on the trustee's own account. This is the case regardless of whether the trustee can benefit personally from the trust. A trustee is subject to the terms of the trust, the mandatory duties and the default duties (to the extent that these are not modified or negated) proscribed in the Trusts Act 2019 (TRU).

At one extreme, a trustee may have no duties other than the duty to transfer ownership of the trust property to the beneficiary when called upon to do so. Such a trust is commonly known as a bare trust.

The focus of the rules for the taxation of trusts is on trusts other than bare trusts. Bare trusts are largely ignored for tax purposes. Generally speaking, a bare trustee is treated as a nominee or agent (or akin to a nominee or agent) of the beneficiary (s YB 21 of the Income Tax Act 2007 (IT07)).

*Refs: s YB 21, Income Tax Act 2007*

## Settlor, trustee and beneficiary

A settlor of a trust can also be a trustee and a beneficiary of the trust. However, a person cannot be the sole trustee and beneficiary, as the essence of a trust is that the trustee holds or deals with trust property for the benefit of beneficiaries to whom the trustee owes fiduciary obligations. In the past, there were statutory concerns regarding settlors of a trust also being beneficiaries. With the abolition of estate duty and gift duty, the concerns regarding the possibility of "claw back" (eg for estate duty purposes, claw back into a settlor's estate the value of any property they transferred to a trust where the benefit of the property could be enjoyed directly or indirectly by them as a beneficiary) are no longer applicable. That said the possibility that some form of estate duty or gift tax may be reintroduced cannot be ruled out.

## Multiple settlements may be treated as one trust

In law, each settlement on a trust is the creation of a separate trust. See Baldwin v C of IR [1965] NZLR 1 and Tucker v C of IR [1965] NZLR 1027.

In practice, 2 or more settlements on the same trustee on the same terms are often managed as one trust with one tax return filed. This practice is allowed for tax purposes; the legislation provides that a trustee may choose to treat multiple settlements made on the same terms as one trust (s HC 3 and Tax Information Bulletin ¶135-112 Vol 13, No 5, May 2001 at 29).

## Application of the trust rules

The trust rules tax trusts largely by reference to the tax residence of the settlor rather than the trustee, as is the case with most other countries. As a general rule, income a non-resident trustee derives from outside New Zealand is deemed to be income of the trustee if any settlor of the trust is resident in New Zealand at any time during the income year. Conversely, a foreign-sourced amount derived by a New Zealand resident trustee is deemed not to be income of the trustee when, broadly stated, no settlor of the trust (who is not a transitional resident) is resident in New Zealand during the income year. Additionally, subject to certain

exceptions, New Zealand resident settlors are liable for tax on trustee income derived during an income year if no trustee of the trust is resident in New Zealand at all times during that year. For more on the taxation of foreign-sourced amounts derived by trustees, see ss HC 25, HC 26, HC 29 and ¶25-100.

The trust rules incorporate the following elements:

## a definition of both "beneficiary income" and "trustee income"

## a wide definition of "settlor"

## complying trusts, foreign trusts and non-complying trusts

## the concept of a "taxable distribution" in relation to a foreign or non-complying trust

## ordering rules in relation to distributions from a trust

## rules relating to a first-time New Zealand resident settlor, and

## specified rates of tax.

The trust regime applies to all trusts (other than bare trusts), both in New Zealand and offshore.

The trust rules do not apply to:

## a unit trust (for tax purposes, unit trusts are deemed to be companies and are taxed accordingly, see

¶24-190)

## a group investment fund to the extent it is treated as a company under the IT07 (¶24-070)

## a M#ori authority

## a distribution under s HZ 1 (distributions from trusts of pre-1989 tax reserves).

For commentary on the tax treatment of trust that is a tax charity, see ¶24-240.

## Trust law review

The TRU, which came into full force and effect on 30 January 2021, replaced the Trustee Act 1956 and the Perpetuities Act 1964 and made trust law more accessible, and clarified and simplified core trust principles and essential obligations for trustees. The TRU also preserves the flexibility of the common law to allow trust law to continue to evolve through the courts. The TRU does not amend or alter any provisions regarding the taxation of trusts or established trust law principles. The TRU:

## provides clarification of the key features of a trust and clear mandatory and default trustee duties so

trustees know what their obligations are if they are involved in managing a trust

## introduces for a presumption that trustees will provide beneficiaries with trust information on request

## provides more flexible trustee powers and updated rules to allow trustees to manage and invest trust

property in the most appropriate way

## provides clear rules for variation, resettlement and termination, and

## provides more options for removing and appointing trustees without having to go to court while also

preserving the ability to ask the courts to intervene to resolve disputes.
