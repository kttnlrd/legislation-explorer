# Legal professional privilege ¶1-595

A taxpayer can decline to comply with an IR request and, consequently, certain information or documents will be privileged from disclosure. There are 2 kinds of legal professional privilege. These are solicitor–client and litigation privilege.

## Solicitor–client

Solicitor–client privilege, which is enacted by s 20 of the TAA, covers the position of confidential information passing between a legal practitioner (as defined by s 20(7) and the Lawyers and Conveyancers Act 2006) in their professional capacity and another legal practitioner in that capacity or a legal practitioner and a client. Certain information or documents are privileged from disclosure when they are made or brought into existence for the dominant purpose of obtaining or giving legal advice or assistance. Communication does not necessarily have to pass directly between the persons referred to. Privilege can still apply if communication passes through an agent of any of the relevant persons.

There are exceptions to privilege. Privilege does not apply when the communication was brought into existence for the purpose of committing some illegal or wrongful act. However, privilege will not be waived on the Commissioner's suspicion of fraud. See Case W17 (2003) 21 NZTC 11,172. No protection from disclosure is conferred on a solicitor's trust account records and such records of client investment income that the solicitor may hold. See Allen Allen & Hemsley v FC of T 89 ATC 4294.

The right to claim legal professional privilege is not a conclusive reason for withholding information; rather, it is one that may be outweighed by other considerations which make it desirable in the public interest to make the information available. See s 9(2)(h) of the Official Information Act 1982.

Example: In-house solicitors holding current practising certificates are entitled to claim legal professional privilege so long as they are acting as lawyers and not in some other capacity such as a specialist adviser or executive. See Miller v C of IR; McDougall v C of IR (No 1) (1997) 18 NZTC 13,001.

Also the Privy Council decision in B v Auckland District Law Society (2003) 21 NZTC 18,221. In this case, the court examined the issues of legal professional privilege and litigation privilege in the context s 101 of the Law Practitioners Act. Briefly, the court held that s 101(3)(d) of the Act did not expressly exclude legal professional privilege. Also, limited waiver of privilege was possible. Note that the Law Practitioners Act was repealed and replaced by the Lawyers and Conveyancers Act.

In C and Multiple Parties v C of IR (2005) 22 NZTC 19,178, the High Court held that evidence given by a witness referring to legal advice that a taxpayer had obtained led to an implied waiver of legal professional privilege.

In Case T32 (1997) 18 NZTC 8,221, the TRA held that the opinions and reports from legal staff employed at IR constituted confidential communications between a client and a client's legal adviser. Their dominant purpose was the giving of legal advice, and they were not created as part of an executive function. The role of the writer of an opinion must be considered, not the consequence of the opinion.

In Martinovich v C of IR (2014) 26 NZTC ¶21-077, the High Court held that the common law on solicitor– client privilege was applicable to the Commissioner. The report in question in that case was held to be legal advice provided by an in-house lawyer, not an executive decision under delegated authority, and was not discoverable. A similar result was reached in Accountants First Ltd v C of IR (2014) 26 NZTC ¶21-081.

Litigation privilege

Litigation privilege arises when a communication is made between a solicitor and a third party (whether made directly or through an agent) or a client and a third party, and that communication relates to existing or contemplated litigation. The privilege protects the process of gathering evidence for lawyers acting for a party in civil or criminal litigation, or a party threatened with such litigation.

Although s 20 of the TAA appears to relate only to solicitor–client privilege (and to codify some aspects of the existing common law in respect of solicitor–client privilege), it is unclear whether it removes litigation privilege as well. In C of IR v West-Walker [1954] NZLR 191, the court stated that privilege would stand even in the face of express statutory provisions. The tacit acceptance of litigation privilege has since been acknowledged by the courts. However, in order for a claim of litigation privilege to be successful, it must be shown that the communication relates to a dominant purpose of conducting or advising on actual or reasonably anticipated litigation.

Example: Interview notes which recorded interviews between a bank and its auditors were not subject to litigation privilege because they were not prepared for the dominant purpose of defending the bank. Although the bank's solicitors had, as one of their purposes, the gathering of evidence for a prosecution, this was not a dominant purpose. The presence of a lawyer at the interview did not in itself confer privilege on the interview notes, particularly when they were not the lawyer's notes. See Dinsdale v C of IR (1998) 18 NZTC 13,583 (CA).
