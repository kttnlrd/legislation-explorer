# Tax avoidance — A practical guide ¶33-135

The following is an attempt to provide some practical assistance for the practitioner faced with the need to make decisions on a day-to-day basis as to the implications under the general anti-avoidance provision of transactions actual or proposed. While there will never be any substitute for a complete and exhaustive analysis of the existing case law, many difficult situations can often be avoided at an early stage and/or the areas of risk identified and possibly limited by following the checklist below:

## be rigorous in examining the facts

## identify all the steps or parts of the arrangement

## identify all the tax provisions being relied on in the arrangement

## analyse the consequences of the general anti-avoidance (or other specific anti-avoidance) provision

applying

## determine whether the leading cases cover the point or are analogous

## determine whether the transaction is unique or whether it is of a type that is occurring routinely in

commercial transactions

## determine whether the key driver is commercial business objectives or derivation of tax benefits

## if the transaction has a genuine commercial objective, albeit with some tax considerations, consider

whether it is appropriate to seek a private binding ruling, and

## consider the reconstruction provisions, to ascertain which parties would surrender the tax advantages

gained from the transaction, should the transaction be considered to amount to tax avoidance.

Also, one should pay particular attention to the guidelines issued by the Supreme Court in Ben Nevis Forestry Ventures Ltd v C of IR; Accent Management Ltd v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,188 (SC) and Frucor Suntory New Zealand Ltd v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (2022) 30 NZTC ¶25-024. See ¶33-105.

## Revenue Alert RA 21/01, "Diverting personal services income by structuring revenue earning

activities through a related entity such as a trading trust or a company: the circumstances when Inland Revenue will consider this arrangement is tax avoidance", which sets out the circumstances when Inland Revenue (IR) will consider an arrangement to be tax avoidance when personal services income is diverted through the use of a company or trading trust. This Revenue Alert was issued just before the increase in the top marginal rate which would result in a 6% differential between the top personal tax rate for taxpayers who earn over $180,000 pa and the trust tax rate, or a difference of 11% between the top personal tax rate and the company tax rate (¶33-325).

## Revenue Alert RA 13/01 covers salary packaging, or reducing income from employment, by replacing

part of salary or wages with vouchers.

Previously, when there was a large gap between the top marginal tax rate and the corporate (or trustee) tax rate, arrangements were entered into by some employers where an employee selected an amount of salary or wage that was substituted for vouchers (like, supermarket or petrol vouchers) which are very similar to the cash equivalent of the salary or wage. A "voucher" in this context may take many forms, including paper vouchers, digital vouchers and electronic vouchers on stored value cards.

guide

Under this "repackaging" or structuring arrangement, the vouchers are not being treated as income of the employee and no PAYE is deducted by the employer. The employee's income is treated as being reduced by the amount of the salary substituted with vouchers. As well as paying less income tax, employees who seek to reduce their income in this way pay less child support; decrease their student loan repayment obligation; reduce their KiwiSaver contributions and may claim a larger entitlement to Working for Families Tax Credit than they should. In addition, repackaging in this way has the effect of reducing the contribution that their employer is required to make to the employee's KiwiSaver scheme.

The Revenue Alert warns that IR considers these arrangements to be tax avoidance. While RA 13/01 was issued in 2013, with the increase in the top marginal tax rate from the 2021–22 income year, there is a risk that employers and employees could be looking to implement such arrangements. IR's position would remain unchanged and such arrangements would be considered tax avoidance arrangements.

In addition, after the decision in Frucor, IR released an updated interpretation statement on their approach to the application of s BG 1 (¶33-137).
