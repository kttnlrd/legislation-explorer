# Tax avoidance contrasted with sham ¶33-065

It is axiomatic that in the context of tax avoidance the documentation is not a sham. The rights and obligations intended to be created by the contracts accurately reflect the rights and obligations that the parties intend to create: Elmiger v C of IR [1966] NZLR 683 (HC) at 685 and upheld on appeal Elmiger v C of IR [1967] NZLR 161 (CA), Marx v C of IR; Carlson v C of IR [1970] NZLR 182 at 190 and Wisheart v C of IR 71 ATC 6001 at 6,014; [1972] NZLR 319 at 335. These cases explicitly confirm that a general anti- avoidance provision applies when the contracts are not shams. The proposition is implicitly confirmed in Accent Management Ltd v C of IR (2007) 23 NZTC 21,323 (CA) at [117b], C of IR v Challenge Corporation Ltd (1986) 8 NZTC 5,219 (PC) at 5,225, Glenharrow Holdings Ltd v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,236 (SC) at [33]. The Supreme Court put the point this way in Ben Nevis Forestry Ventures Ltd v C of IR; Accent Management Ltd v C of IR (2009) 24 NZTC 23,188 (SC) at [34]: It is important to keep firmly in mind the difference between sham and avoidance. A sham exists when documents do not reflect the true nature of what the parties have agreed. Avoidance occurs, even though the documents may accurately reflect the transaction which the parties intended to implement, when, for reasons to be discussed more fully below, the arrangement entered into gives a tax advantage which Parliament regards as unacceptable.
