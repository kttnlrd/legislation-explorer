# Group investment funds ¶24-070

The ability to form a group investment fund enables a trustee company to gather the funds of many small investors into an administratively efficient entity. The investors may be estates, trusts or other persons, who request the trustee company to act as an agent in investing funds. Funds from the latter are known as agency funds. A group investment fund may be established only by the Public Trust or by a trustee company under the Trustee Companies Act 1967, Public Trust Office Act 1957 or Public Trust Act 2001.

## Basis of taxation

Trustees of a group investment fund are required to file separate returns of "Category A" and "Category B" income. In terms of the core provisions, separate tax liabilities arise for each of these categories of income.

By s BC 7, trustees who derive Category A income (ie schedular income) are required to calculate an income tax liability for that income, under the general calculation provisions, as if it were their only income.

This gives rise to a schedular income tax liability. For these purposes, a group investment fund trustee's schedular taxable income for Category A income (ie income less deductions from investments not giving rise to Category B income) is taxed at the same rate as the corporate tax rate. The current tax rate for Category A income is 28%.

Category B income is income derived from the fund's traditional activities of investing in authorised trustee investments, eg mortgages, government stock and certain debentures. The trustee is taxed on that Category B income at the rate of 39% (from 1 April 2024, before this date the tax rate was 33%), subject to the trust rules and s HZ 2. The trustee of a fund is deemed to be the agent for any absentee investor's distributions from the fund.

The tax rate for Category B income depends on whether the group investment fund is a "widely-held GIF".

Widely-held GIFs are taxed at the rate of 28% on both their Category A and B income, whereas group investment funds that are not widely-held continue to be taxed at the trustee rate of 39% on their Category B income and at the rate of 28% on their Category A income. A widely-held GIF is one that satisfies the investor membership requirements for an entity other than a company in the portfolio investment entity (PIE) rules (s HM 14(1)) and one or more of paras (a) and (c)–(e) of the definition of a "public unit trust" (s YA 1 and ¶24-192).

## Distributions from a group investment fund

The definition of a "company" includes a trustee of a group investment fund (other than a designated group investment fund) but only in respect of its Category A income. Distributions of Category A income to investors are treated as dividends (ss CD 4 and YA 1 ("company" and "share")). Distributions of Category B income are treated as distributions from a trust and subject to the trust rules (¶25-310).

When the trustee or manager of a group investment fund deriving Category A income (or a person nominated by the trustee or manager) redeems investors' interests in the ordinary course of business, the dividend derived by the investor does not include any imputation credits attached to the dividend to the extent to which it constitutes a recovery of the purchase price (¶16-630).

In certain circumstances, where an interest in an unlisted group investment fund is redeemed or cancelled the redemption amount (or part of it) may be excluded from being a dividend. For a further discussion of the tax treatment of redemptions by group investment funds and a discussion of whether the ordering rule or slice rule will apply to those redemptions, see ¶16-705 and ¶24-190. It should be noted that if a superannuation fund held an interest in a group investment fund on 1 April 1999, any subsequent redemption of that interest by the group investment fund will not be subject to the slice rule, unless the group investment fund's trustee elected otherwise before that date.

## Deductibility of management fees

Under the Trustee Companies Amendment Act 2002, a trustee company can charge a fee to the group investment fund for its management services. These fees are deductible to the group investment fund in accordance with the general permission in s DA 1.

## Transfer of expenditure

Group investment funds investing in unit trusts or in other group investment funds incur expenditure on administration and management but may only derive dividend income that is usually fully imputed. Unless the group investment fund has income from another source, such as interest, the group investment fund accumulates excess credits that it cannot use. There are provisions that allow group investment funds to use their excess imputation credits without the need to enter into arrangements to ensure they have income streams to absorb them. A group investment fund deriving Category A income (member fund) that invests in a public unit trust or another group investment fund deriving Category A income (master fund) can elect to transfer expenditure to that master fund. A "public unit trust" is defined as either a retail unit trust (whose units are offered to the public and that has a hundred or more unit holders) or a wholesale unit trust (whose units are held by widely held investment vehicles such as other unit trusts or superannuation funds) (¶24-192).

All expenditure can be transferred, other than expenditure on revenue account property and expenditure on some financial arrangements. The master fund is able to deduct the expenditure, and the expenditure is treated as not having been incurred by the member fund. The master fund cannot deduct more than its taxable income for the year in which the income is transferred, less all non-resident passive income derived by the master fund in that year. For expenditure to be transferred, the member fund must have funds invested in the master fund at all times between the date on which the member fund incurs the expenditure and the last day of the tax year in which the expenditure is deducted by the master fund. If the master fund is a multi-rate PIE (¶29-115), the master fund is allowed a deduction for the expenditure transferred from a member fund. However, the amount transferred must not be more than the member fund's share of the multi- rate PIE's taxable income.

Any amount paid to the member fund by the master fund for the expenditure transferred is not income or a dividend for tax purposes (Tax Information Bulletin ¶1311-114 Vol 13, No 11, November 2001 at 39).

The expenditure transferred gives rise to a credit in the master fund's ICA on 31 March of the tax year in which the expenditure is deducted. Similarly, the expenditure transferred gives rise to a debit in the member fund's ICA on 31 March of the tax year in which the expenditure is transferred. If the debit to the member fund's ICA in these circumstances results in a debit balance, there is no requirement to pay further income tax on the debit arising on transfer of the expenditure.

## Available subscribed capital accounts (ASCA)

A group investment fund commonly overpays tax to counteract the loss of available subscribed capital when redemption payments are made to investors at less than the amount subscribed (commonly known as the "negative dividend" issue). Some public unit trusts are also affected by this issue (¶24-192). The situation usually arises when the group investment fund (or public unit trust) elects to use the "slice rule" for determining the tax treatment of the proceeds from the redemption of interests (¶24-190).

To preserve the available subscribed capital, a group investment fund deriving Category A income that is not a multi-rate PIE (¶29-115) can maintain an available subscribed capital account (ASCA). The ASCA rules also apply to public unit trusts. Specific provisions governing ASCAs are contained in subpart OF of the IT07. These supplement the general provisions applying to memorandum accounts. Generally, the ASCA is a memorandum account that records the amounts of available subscribed capital contributed by investors but not returned to them on redemption of their interests. The balance in the ASCA can be converted to imputation credits and transferred to the group investment fund's ICA at the end of a tax year or when the

group investment fund ceases to operate an ICA. Only an ASCA balance sufficient to meet the debit balance in the group investment fund's ICA can be converted and transferred.

If a group investment fund deriving Category A income has never established an ASCA, one may be established upon liquidation by carrying out the calculation in accordance with s OZ 5. In this case, the result of the calculation will be the closing balance of the ASCA.

The Commissioner has the power to correct any incorrect or omitted credit or debit entry in the ASCA. In doing so, they must determine the correct debit or credit amount and the date on which the debit or credit should be recorded. The correction is made by giving a notice of a determination. However, failure to give notice does not invalidate a determination. The determination may be given separately or included in a notice of assessment. Unless the determination is challenged, the ASCA must be corrected in accordance with the determination.

For discussion on the impact of the ASCA on tax refunds due, see ¶17-105.
