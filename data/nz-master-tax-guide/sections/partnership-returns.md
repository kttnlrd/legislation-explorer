# Partnership returns ¶23-100

## Note: The Taxation (Annual Rates for 2025–26, Emergency Response, and Remedial Measures) Bill

proposes an amendment to align how partnerships with non-standard balance dates return income with the method for applying for the Research and Development Tax Incentive (RDTI). Partnerships with an RDTI credit for an income year would be required to apportion income according to the partner's balance date when returning partnership income for that year.

For taxation purposes, a partnership is required to register with the IR. Upon receipt, IR issues a tax file number and may require the partnership to register for GST (¶23-110). In contrast to a company, a partnership is not a taxpayer in its own right. Although a partnership does not actually pay tax, it does have to file a return of income showing the income jointly derived from the partnership, how that income has been divided between the partners and a summary of the deductions allocated to each partner that relate to the income. In other words, the partnership files a return of net income for information purposes only. Even if the partnership has made a tax loss for an income year, a return of income must be filed to show whether the business had income for the year and how that result was calculated. See C of IR v Grover (1988) 10 NZTC 5,012 (CA). For these purposes, the partnership should complete form IR7. That return of income form should be accompanied by a copy of the firm's financial statements.

## Legal position of partnerships

General partnerships are not legal entities, meaning they have no existence separate from the individual partners that comprise them. Ownership of the partnership assets vests in each partner in accordance with the contract between them. Partners have joint and several liability for the liabilities of the partnership, although they may vary the sharing of those liabilities among themselves. This general legal position is followed by the tax system, with each partner being assessed on their proportionate share of partnership income as if it were derived directly by the individual partner and the deductions allowed to that partner in respect of that income. Various sources of income (such as interest and dividends) retain their character in the individual partner's hands.

## Summary

The tax position of general partnerships was summarised by Richardson J in Hadlee and Sydney Bridge Nominees Ltd v C of IR (1991) 13 NZTC 8,116 (CA) at 8,126: New Zealand tax legislation does not isolate partnership income as a separate source of income. In New Zealand law a partnership is not a separate tax entity. It is not a "taxpayer" and partners make a return of partnership income only for the purpose of providing information on which their separate incomes are calculated. The gross income is derived by the partners jointly and the partners severally claim the deductions to which they are entitled as taxpayers in terms of the legislation.

This approach is formalised in s HG 2 of the IT07 and s 42 of the TAA.

## Different balance date

A partner may have a different balance date from the partnership. For example, the partner may have the standard 31 March balance date and the partnership one of 30 June. The general rules for the approval of balance dates will apply to partnerships (¶2-055). Although the introduction of a new partner or retirement of an existing partner creates a new partnership, the anniversary date of the introduction of a new partner will not be allowed as a new balance date.

Where a partnership has a non-standard balance date, its partners may elect, in their tax return, to include their share of the partnership income in the same corresponding income year as the partnership (s 42(3) of the TAA). The election only applies to the specific partnership income and no other transactions of the partner.

Where a partner makes an election, it cannot be switched year on year and remains in force until either the partnership changes its balance date or the partner exits the partnership. Another option for a partner is to apportion the partnership income to their income tax year. See Example 45 in Tax Information Bulletin ¶375-102 Vol 37, No 5, June 2025 at 117 and Example 7 in IS 25/11 at [149].

## Returning partnership income

To meet the invoicing requirements of the Accident Compensation Corporation, partnership income should be analysed and returned by the individual partners as follows:

## partnership income derived from the partnership's trade or business that is a result of the partner's

active involvement

## interest and any resident withholding tax (RWT)

## dividends and any credits

## overseas income and overseas tax paid

## rental income, and

## other income (ie income received as a sleeping partner or income generated from other investment

activity of the partnership).

In effect, this divides the partnership income into its active and passive components so that ACC levies are calculated only on the active income.
