# Registration requirements for charities ¶24-195

## Income tax exemptions

Charities that meet certain requirements may benefit from income tax exemptions. There are 3 categories of exempt income that relate to amounts derived by a charity. These are:

## income derived by trustees in trust for charitable purposes or by any society or institution established

and maintained exclusively for charitable purposes provided that the charity is a tax charity (see below and ¶24-240)

## income derived by a business carried on for charitable purposes, or for business income derived by

or on behalf of or for the benefit of any society or institution established and maintained exclusively for charitable purposes (¶24-200), and

## income derived by an executor or administrator from funds held in trust for charitable purposes

(charitable bequests — s CW 43).

The first 2 categories of exempt income listed above require the relevant charity to be a "tax charity". This is defined to mean:

## a trustee, a society, or an institution registered as a charitable entity under the Charities Act 2005

## a trustee, a society, or an institution that, before 1 July 2008, has started to take reasonable steps in

the process of registration under the Charities Act, intends to complete that process and has not been advised by IR that it is not a tax charity

## a trustee, a society, or an institution that is a non-resident and carries out its charitable purposes

outside New Zealand and that the Commissioner approves as a tax charity in circumstances where registration as a charitable entity is not available, and

## persons removed from the register, for the period from when they are first registered to the later of

the day they stop complying with the rules and the day of final decision (see below).

Note that the exemption does not mean that the charity is exempt for GST purposes. If a charity breaches the $60,000 threshold, it may still be liable to register for GST (¶32-048 and ¶32-148).

## Eligibility for registration

To be eligible for registration, a charitable entity must satisfy the following conditions:

## for trustees of a trust, the trust is of a kind in relation to which an amount of income is derived by the

trustees in trust for charitable purposes

## for a society or an institution, the society or institution is established and maintained exclusively for

charitable purposes, and it is not carried on for the private pecuniary profit of any individual

## the entity has a name that complies with certain conditions (see below), and

## all of the officers of the entity are qualified to be officers of a charitable entity (see below), and

## from 5 October 2023, at any time, at least one officer of the entity is 18 years or older.

The trustees of a trust are treated as complying with the registration requirements for income (ie charitable income is derived) if:

## in accordance with a binding ruling made under Pt 5A of the TAA, the income derived by the trust is

treated as exempt income for income tax purposes, or

## the income derived by trustees is deemed to be derived in trust for charitable purposes under s 24B

of the M#ori Trust Boards Act 1955.

Similarly, a society or an institution is treated as being established and maintained exclusively for charitable purposes and not carried on for the private pecuniary profit of any individual if it is treated this way for income tax purposes in accordance with a binding ruling.

The effect of the binding ruling for registration purposes continues until the binding ruling expires or ceases to apply.

A trust is not prevented from being registered because the trust has not yet derived income for charitable purposes, if the Charities Registration Board is of the opinion that income will be derived by the trustees in trust for charitable purposes and it is fit and proper to register the trust as a charitable entity. In reaching its opinion, the Board may act on the basis of any reasonable assumptions concerning the future derivation of income for charitable purposes. An example is given in s 14 of the Charities Act.

## Name requirements

The name of the entity must satisfy one of the criteria set out below:

## the entity is incorporated under that name under one of the following Acts:

## the Incorporated Societies Act 1908

## the Charitable Trusts Act 1957, or

## the Companies Act 1993

## the entity is established or constituted by an Act under that name, or

## if the Board decided that the name is not offensive or liable to mislead the public.

## Registration

An application for registration as a charitable entity must satisfy the following conditions:

## it is in the prescribed form

## it is accompanied by:

## a document in the prescribed form, signed by, or on behalf of, every officer of the entity, that contains

a certification that the officer is not disqualified from being an officer of a charitable entity (see above under the sub-heading "Officers of charitable entities")

## a copy of the rules of the entity, and

## the prescribed fee for the application, and

## it contains, or is accompanied by, any other prescribed information or documentation.

The Board is required to consider the application for registration and may request further information or documentation be provided within 20 working days of the request. The Board must give the applicant notice of any matter that might lead to the Board declining the application and the applicant has 2 months, or longer period the Board agrees to on request by the applicant, to make submissions on the matter.

An application will be treated as withdrawn if the applicant does not adequately respond to a request for information or to make submissions within the relevant period. If the Board is satisfied that the entity is qualified to be registered as a charitable entity, the Board must register the entity as a charitable entity, allocate a registration number and notify the entity of its registration and number. Note, from 5 October 2023, if the Board declines an application, it must as soon as practicable publish its decision and the reasons on a publicly accessible website (maintained by or on behalf of the Board).

## Registration may be backdated

Registration as a charitable entity may be backdated if the Board is satisfied that the entity was qualified for registration as a charitable entity at all times during the period between the effective registration time and the time that the entity actually became registered as a charitable entity. The effective date of registration cannot be earlier than:

## in the case of a charitable trust, or charitable society or institution created or established by a gift

made after 1 February 2007 and that was exempt from gift duty, the time that the gift was made, and

## in any other case, the time the Board received a properly completed application for registration as a

charitable entity.

The backdating of registration applies for the purposes of the Charities Act, the IT07 and the Estate and Gift Duties Act 1968.

## Definition of charitable purpose

Generally, for the purpose of the Charities Act, s 5 defines the term "charitable purpose" as including:

## every charitable purpose, whether it relates to the relief of poverty, the advancement of education or

religion, or any other matter beneficial to the community

## the purpose of a trust, a society or an institution if the purpose would satisfy the public benefit

requirement, apart from the fact that the beneficiaries of the trust, or the members of the society or institution, are related by blood

## a marae, if the physical structure of the marae is situated on land that is a M#ori reservation referred

to in the M#ori Land Act 1993 (Te Ture Whenua M#ori Act 1993), and the funds of the marae are used only for the administration and maintenance of the land and of the physical structure of the marae or any other charitable purpose, and

## the promotion of amateur sport if it is the means by which a charitable purpose referred to in the first

bullet point above is pursued (this last category applies from 25 February 2012).

Furthermore, if the purposes of a trust, a society or an institution include a non-charitable purpose (eg advocacy) that is merely ancillary to a charitable purpose of the trust, society or institution, that non- charitable purpose does not prevent registration as a charitable entity. A non-charitable purpose is ancillary to a charitable purpose of a trust, society, or institution if the non-charitable purpose is:

## ancillary, secondary, subordinate, or incidental to a charitable purpose of the trust, society or

institution, and

## not an independent purpose of the trust, society or institution.

Example: In the first appeal under the Charities Act, the High Court in Travis Trust v Charities Commission (2009) 24 NZTC 23,273 held that, on the basis of first principle and on consideration of the authorities, the promotion of a horse race is not a charitable purpose in and of itself. The High Court noted that the Charities Commission (now known as the "Board") registered mixed trusts on the condition that any private objects of a charitable entity would not be given any effect by the trustees or governance body of the entity. The single issue before the Court, in this case, was, therefore, whether the provision of prize money for an annual and significant race on the New Zealand horse-racing calendar was a charitable purpose. The Court analysed the definition as contained in the Charities Act.

Charitable purpose and the common law The focus of the case was on the fourth general benefit head. The Court proceeded on the basis that the purpose of the trust was either the support of the Travis Stakes or of the Cambridge Jockey Club's annual programme of race meetings.

Sports and leisure cases After reviewing the sports and leisure cases, the High Court found that the weight of authorities ran powerfully against a finding that a gift towards the prize in the Travis Stakes was charitable. The High Court did note, however, that recent New Zealand cases tended to support the idea that the concept of charitable purpose was evolving in response to changing social and cultural circumstances. In the area of sport and leisure, the general principle appeared to be that sport, leisure and entertainment for their own sake were not charitable, but, where these purposes were expressed to be and were, in fact, the means by which other valid charitable purposes, such as health or education, would be achieved, they will be held to be charitable.

As noted above, the promotion of amateur sports can qualify as a charitable purpose if it is the means by which a charitable purpose is pursued. This applies from 25 February 2012.

Public benefit The High Court noted that, once it was established that the purpose of a trust was charitable in character, it must also be established that the benefits of the trust will accrue to the public. The Court considered that the widest valid category of beneficiaries of the trust in this case would be the members of the Cambridge Jockey Club. After drawing all the principles together, the High Court found that the purpose of the trust was to fund a race for the benefit of the Cambridge Jockey Club's race programme. As it was a private club, the High Court held that the Cambridge Jockey Club was not the community, or a sufficient enough section of it, to amount to

"the public". Even if the Cambridge Jockey Club did, in fact, constitute the community or an appreciable section of it, the promotion of horse racing generally was not a charitable purpose in and of itself. The Court said that "a trust to promote racing could only be charitable in nature if its deeper purpose was the pursuit of some other objective, either in principle or, in accordance with charities jurisprudence, a charitable purpose in its own right within the spirit and intendment of the Statute of Elizabeth. Thus, if it could have been established that the true intention of the support for this race was the promotion of health, education or perhaps even animal welfare, it might have satisfied the test". The High Court upheld the decision of the Commission and dismissed the appeal.

See also, Canterbury Development Corp v Charities Commission; Canterbury Development Corp Trust v Charities Commission; CEDF Trustee Ltd as Trustee of the Canterbury Economic Development Fund v Charities Commission (2010) 24 NZTC 24,143, Liberty Trust v Charities Commission (2011) 25 NZTC ¶20-054, Re Queenstown Lakes Community Housing Trust (2011) 25 NZTC ¶20-059, Re Greenpeace of New Zealand Inc (2014) 26 NZTC ¶21-088 (SC) and Re Family First New Zealand (2015) 27 NZTC ¶22-017.

## Effect of deregistration

An entity may be removed from the Charities Register at its request or at the direction of the Board. The grounds for removal include:

## the entity is not or is no longer qualified for registration as a charitable entity

## the entity has engaged in serious wrongdoing

## any person has engaged in serious wrongdoing in connection with the entity.

Legislative changes clarified the tax implications that arise when a charity is removed from the Charities Register and not eligible to derive exempt income under s CW 41 or CW 42. In particular, the rules clarify how existing legislation (including the FBT and donations tax credit regimes) applies to deregistered charities.

## Retention of tax exempt status until day of final decision

Amendments to s CW 41 ensure that charities that are removed from the charities register continue to be tax-exempt until the "day of final decision", providing they have acted in accordance with their rules contained in the charities register. The "day of final decision" is the later of:

## the day the charity is removed from the charities register

## the day on which the charity exhausts all disputes and appeals regarding its charitable status.

Thus, provided a deregistered charity has acted in accordance with its rules, there are no retrospective tax consequences. They become tax-paying from the day of final decision. However, where a charity does not comply with its rules, it loses its tax-exempt status from that earlier date.

## Retention of FBT exemption

The effect of the above amendment is largely mirrored in the amendment for FBT purposes. The definition of "charitable organisation" is amended to provide that a deregistered charity can still be a charitable organisation and eligible for the FBT exemption for a specified period (provided it otherwise satisfies the definition). The period in question starts from the date the charity is registered on the charities register and ends with the earlier of:

## the last day of the relevant quarter or income year in which the charity does not comply with their

rules, or

## the last day of the relevant quarter or income year in which the day of final decision falls.

Thus, only those charities that have not complied with their rules are subject to the earlier loss of their FBT exemption.

## Opening values for assets and consideration for financial arrangements

Section HR 11 dictates what the initial opening values for assets and consideration for financial arrangements are when a charity becomes a tax-paying entity. The section applies on and after the day the

charity is deregistered and no longer able to derive exempt income under ss CW 41 and CW 42, defined as the "date of cessation". The cost of premises, plant, equipment and trading stock of a deregistered charity will be the value that would be used at the date of cessation if s CW 41 or CW 42 had never applied.

A similar approach applies to determining the consideration for any financial arrangement, which will be determined by applying the following formula: consideration paid to person + expenditure # consideration paid by person # income where: consideration paid to person is the consideration that is paid to the charity before the date of cessation expenditure is the expenditure that would have been incurred under the financial arrangements rules before the date of cessation consideration paid by person is the consideration that is paid by the charity before the date of cessation income is the income that would have been derived under the financial arrangements rules before the date of cessation.

The charity will also be treated as having the unexpired portion of expenditure under s EA 3 (Prepayments) and the unpaid amount under s EA 4 (Deferred payment of employment income) that it would have had if s CW 41 or CW 42 never applied. A deduction will be available for the unexpired portion under ss DB 50 and DB 51 in the income year that contains the date of cessation.

Some charities may not have sufficient records on the actual historical cost of their assets and financial arrangements because they were not required to keep them or they are a small charity with low administrative capacity. In these circumstances, s HR 11(6) provides that charities may use information from their annual returns contained on the register of charities.

These rules apply from 14 April 2014.

## Tax on accumulated assets of deregistered charity

In addition, ss CV 17 and HR 12 introduce a tax on the accumulated assets of a deregistered charity. Under s HR 12, a deregistered charity will have an amount of income equal to the value of the net assets (being assets less liabilities) held on the day of final decision. The income will be derived on the day 12 months after the day of final decision. However, excluded from the amount of income will be:

## from 1 April 2024, assets distributed or applied for charitable purposes to a tax charity (except for

non-resident organisations that are tax charities under s CW 41(5)(c)), or a New Zealand resident persons that derives exempt income under any of s CW 38–CW 52, CW 55BA or CW 64

## before 1 April 2024, assets distributed or applied in that 12 months for charitable purposes or in

accordance with the charity's rules

## assets received from the Crown to settle a Treaty of Waitangi claim and/or in accordance with the

M#ori Fisheries Act 2004

## assets, other than money, gifted or left to the charity when it met the requirements to derive exempt

income under s CW 41 or CW 42

## the value of land and improvements on the land for marae built on reservation land established under

the Te Ture Whenua M#ori Act 1993, and

## from 1 April 2019, the value of a parent company's shares in a subsidiary if a parent entity and one or

more members of a charitable group deregister at the same time.

The provision will not apply if the charity is re-registered on the charities register within 12 months of the day of final decision. In addition, with effect from 1 April 2019, if a charity sells an interest in a subsidiary at arm's length for market value, s HR 12 does not apply to that subsidiary if it is deregistered as a result of the sale.

A de minimis threshold of $10,000 in net assets has also been introduced to exclude small-scale charities from the deregistration tax rules with effect from 1 April 2019.

Section CV 17 provides that an amount of income under s HR 12 is income of the deregistered charity for the income year that contains the day 12 months after the day of final decision.

Section CV 17 applies from 14 April 2014. Section HR 12 applies from 1 April 2015 with 2 exceptions. The first exception is where the charity voluntarily deregisters before 1 April 2015, in which case the provision applies from 14 April 2014. The second exception relates to community housing providers that have not voluntarily deregistered before 1 April 2015. In that case the provision applies from 1 April 2017.

IR255 "Charitable and donee organisations" (April 2024) at 35 provides further guidance concerning the consequences of deregistration.
