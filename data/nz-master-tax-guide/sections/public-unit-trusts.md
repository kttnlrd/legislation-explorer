# Public unit trusts ¶24-192

In general terms, public unit trusts are unit trusts that meet certain criteria and include retail unit trusts (whose units are offered to the public and that have a 100 or more unit holders) and certain wholesale unit trusts (whose units are held by widely held investment vehicles such as other unit trusts or superannuation funds). For the purposes of determining the number of unit holders and other percentage interests referred to in the definition, persons associated with one another are treated as one person. To reduce the compliance costs of determining whether unit holders are associated, unit holders must disclose their associations to the trustee upon application by the trustee. The trustee is entitled to rely on the disclosure unless there are reasonable grounds for believing the disclosure is not correct.

## Transfer of expenditure

Unit trusts investing in other unit trusts or group investment funds incur expenditure on administration and management and receive their only income in the form of dividends that are usually fully imputed. Unless the unit trust has income from another source, such as interest, so that the imputation credits can be utilised, the unit trust accumulates excess credits or losses (if the unit trust converts the credits to losses) that it cannot use. Sections DV 5 and DV 6 allow public unit trusts to utilise those imputation credits without the need to enter into arrangements to ensure they have income streams to absorb the excess credits. Public unit trusts (defined as "member funds") that invest in other public unit trusts or group investment funds deriving category A income (defined as "master funds") can elect to transfer expenditure to that master fund. As noted above, a "public unit trust" includes a retail or wholesale unit trust that meets certain criteria.

All expenditure can be transferred, other than expenditure that is revenue account property and expenditure on a financial arrangement. (However, expenditure on a financial arrangement that is denominated in New Zealand dollars and for which expenditure is allocated using the yield to maturity method can be transferred.) The master fund is able to deduct the expenditure and the expenditure is treated as not having been incurred by the member fund. The master fund cannot deduct more than its taxable income for the year in which the income is transferred, less all non-resident passive income derived by the master fund in that year. (This formula does not apply to an amount of expenditure that is transferred to a master fund that is a multi-rate PIE.) Expenditure that cannot be deducted by the master fund may be carried forward by the member fund as loss balance and transferred to the master fund in a later year. If, after the date it has filed its return, the master fund is able to deduct more than the amount actually deducted, the Commissioner may allow a member fund to transfer expenditure to the extent of the difference to the master fund after the return has been filed. A master fund that is a multi-rate PIE is allowed a deduction for expenditure transferred from a member fund. However, the amount transferred must not be more than the member fund's share of the multi- rate PIE's taxable income.

The member fund and the master fund must agree that the expenditure be transferred to the master fund and expenditure may only be transferred to the extent that the member fund has a tax loss. For expenditure to be transferred, the member fund must have funds invested in the master fund at all times between the date on which the member fund incurs the expenditure and the last day of the tax year in which the expenditure is deducted by the master fund. In the income year in which the member fund stops investing in the master fund, neither the member fund nor the master fund can deduct the expenditure, and the member fund must treat the expenditure as a loss balance.

Any amount paid to the member fund by the master fund for the expenditure transferred is not income or a dividend for tax purposes.

The expenditure transferred gives rise to a credit in the master fund's ICA on 31 March of the tax year in which the expenditure is deducted. Similarly, the expenditure transferred gives rise to a debit in the member fund's ICA on 31 March of the tax year in which the expenditure is transferred. If the debit to the member fund's ICA in these circumstances results in a debit balance, there is no requirement to pay further income tax on the debit arising on transfer of the expenditure (s OB 65(2)).

## Shareholder continuity

In determining the shareholder continuity of a public unit trust, if a public unit trust so chooses, all unit holders are treated as a notional single person that is not a company, exists as long as the unit trust exists and holds nothing other than the shares in the unit trust. This means that the public unit trust need not keep track of the unit-holding changes.

A taxpayer who is, directly or indirectly, wholly or partially owned by a public unit trust may choose not to apply the look-through rule in s YC 4 and to treat the public unit trust as applying the notional single person concession without the public trust having to separately choose to apply it.

## Available subscribed capital accounts

Like group investment funds, public unit trusts were also affected by the "negative dividend" issue. To remedy this, a public unit trust that is not a multi-rate PIE (¶29-115) may establish an available subscribed capital account (ASCA). For further detail on the ASCA, see ¶24-070 (references to a "group investment fund" should be read as references to a "public unit trust").
