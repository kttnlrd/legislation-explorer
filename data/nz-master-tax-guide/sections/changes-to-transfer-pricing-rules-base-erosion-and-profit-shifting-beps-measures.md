# Changes to transfer pricing rules — base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) measures ¶26-608

From 1 July 2018, New Zealand's transfer pricing legislation has been strengthened so it aligns with the OECD's transfer pricing guidelines and Australia's transfer pricing rules.

The following amendments have been made:

## Including a specific reference to using the July 2017 OECD transfer pricing guidelines as guidance for

how the rules are applied.

## The economic substance and actual conduct of the parties will have priority over the terms of

the legal contract. This is achieved by requiring the transfer pricing transaction to be "accurately delineated" consistent with section D.1 of chapter I of the OECD transfer pricing guidelines. This process involves identifying the following economically relevant characteristics:

## the contractual terms of the transaction

## the functions performed by each of the parties to the transaction, taking into account assets used and

risks assumed, including how those functions relate to the wider generation of value by the MNE group to which the parties belong, the circumstances surrounding the transaction, and industry practices

## the characteristics of property transferred or services provided

## the economic circumstances of the parties and of the market in which parties operate, and

## the business strategies pursued by the parties.

When deciding what characteristics are economically relevant, the test is to ask what characteristics 2 independent enterprises would take into account when dealing with each other. As part of accurately delineating the controlled transaction, the terms of the legal agreement must be disregarded to the extent they are inconsistent with the actual conduct of the parties.

## The ability to disregard or replace transfer pricing arrangements that are not commercially rational.

Where an arrangement is not commercially rational (for example, because it includes unrealistic terms that unrelated parties would not agree to), there is no price that would be acceptable to independent parties and therefore it is impossible to apply a transfer pricing analysis.

## Referring to "arm's length conditions" (as per Australia's legislation) when determining the arm's

length amount of consideration to clarify that the transfer pricing rules can be used to adjust conditions other than simply the price. For example, financial values, such as price, gross margin, net profit and the division of profits, as well as other conditions that may not involve a numerical value, such as the absence or presence of a loan guarantee, may be relevant.

## The onus of proof for demonstrating that a transfer pricing position aligns with arm's length conditions

is shifted from IR to the taxpayer (consistent with the onus of proof being on the taxpayer for other tax matters).

## The time bar that limits IR's ability to adjust a taxpayer's transfer pricing position is increased from

4 to 7 years, but only where IR has begun a transfer pricing tax investigation within four years of the relevant tax return being filed and has notified the taxpayer of this investigation.

## In addition to applying to transactions between related parties, the transfer pricing rules will also apply

to overseas investors who act in concert to control a New Zealand business. The acting together test will only apply to a group of non-residents (not non-residents and residents) and will not apply simply because two companies are consolidated for accounting purposes.

## Taxpayers with inbound related-party debt will be able to continue with the agreed pricing in any

relevant APA issued before 1 July 2018 until the end of the APA period.

Further, from 30 March 2025, amendments clarify that the transfer pricing and dividend rules apply concurrently regardless of whether the other party applies for a matching transfer pricing treatment under s GC 11.

*Refs: s GC 11*

## Guidance materials

IR released guidance material, "BEPS — Transfer pricing rules", in Tax Information Bulletin ¶313-104 Vol 31, No 3, April 2019 at 12. The materials discuss the amendments made in more detail.

## OECD Two Pillar Approach — the global digital economy and

large multi-national enterprises In 2021 the OECD released a framework on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) statement on the Two- Pillar Solution to address the tax challenges arising from the digital economy.

## Digital and online products and services

Pillar One comprises Parts A and B. Part A applies to about 100 of the biggest and most profitable MNEs and reallocates part of their profit to the countries where they sell their products and provide their services, where their consumers are. Without this rule, these companies can earn significant profits in a market without paying much tax there. The aim of the reallocation of profit is to ensure a fairer distribution of profits and taxing rights among countries with respect to the largest and most profitable multinational enterprises.

It will reallocate some taxing rights over MNEs from their home countries to the markets where they have business activities and earn profits, regardless of whether firms have a physical presence there.

## Large global multi-national enterprises

Pillar Two applies more broadly. It applies to any company with over €750 million of annual revenue. Under this Pillar these companies would now be subject to a global minimum corporate tax rate of 15% (known as the GloBE rules). This will be the case even where companies have arranged their tax affairs to make use of low tax jurisdictions. The global minimum tax agreement does not seek to eliminate tax competition between jurisdictions but puts multilaterally agreed limitations on it. The OECD are of the view that benefits will also arise from the stabilisation of the international tax system and the increased tax certainty for taxpayers and tax administrations, like IR. The OECD released model rules for implementing Pillar Two in December 2021.

New Zealand has adopted the GloBE rules by cross-reference, referring to them as global anti-base erosion model rules. Under subpt HP, MNEs with annual revenues above €750 million will pay a minimum 15% effective tax rate on their mobile income in every country where that income is earned. The applicable tax is called "Multinational Top-Up Tax".

The GloBE rules consist of an Income Inclusion Rule (IIR) which includes a domestic income inclusion rule (DIIR) and an Undertaxed Profits Rule (UTPR). The IIR and UTPR components of the applied GloBE rules apply for fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2025. The DIIR component of the rules applies for fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2026. Refer to Tax Information Bulletin Vol 36, No 4, May 2024 at p 16.

## Country-by-country reports

The requirement for large multinationals to file a country-by-country report has also been codified.

IR already required New Zealand headquartered multinational groups with annual consolidated group revenue of €750 million or more in the previous financial year to file a country-by-country report using the IR 1032 (May 2018) prescribed form from 1 January 2016. From 27 June 2018, the legislation codifies this requirement. See further at Tax Information Bulletin ¶313-103 Vol 31, No 3, April 2019 at 10.
